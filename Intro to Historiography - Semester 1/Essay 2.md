## Assess the impact of postcolonial perspectives on the history of photography

Scholarship of the history of photography prior to the advent of postcolonial historiography and the concept of 'Orientalism' traditionally maintained a focus on technological progress, the science behind the camera, and photography as a worthy successor to the canvas. Such histories had a tendency to err heavily on the side of a eurocentric narrative - images of a stereotypical British Victorian family sitting and staring blankly into the camera spring to mind. However, the popularisation of 'orientalism' by Edward Said in his book of the same name introduced new, postcolonial perspectives to the history of photography. More recent scholarship has seen photography redefined as a tool of imperialist projects and a medium through which the concept of the exotic 'Orient' was promoted in Western circles, as well as promoting a novel view of photography as being practised and moulded by indigenous populations, not just the European photographers whom were often the sole focus of histories of photography in colonised regions. This essay shall argue that postcolonial perspectives have revolutionised the history of photography and introduced a new paradigm to the field, through analysis of orientalist tropes which photography reinforced, the practise of photography as a means of benefitting colonialist designs, the active participation of the subjects of orientalist photography, and the use of the colonial archive to shape future conceptions of the 'orient'.

Many photographers who travelled into Africa, India, and various other colonised regions, did so with a purportedly 'scientific' purpose.[^1] This purpose was multifaceted: some photographers used their medium to scout the geography of a region, with the photographs later being used to plan offensives or settlements in that area;[^2] others used photography to compile comprehensive ethnographies so that those in charge of imperial projects could identify different native populations and their attitudes towards colonial hegemony;[^3] and there was also a 'salvage motive' for some photographers, which was an attempt to document dwindling indigenous populations before they became extinct (the most obvious example of this being the images of indigenous Tasmanians in Australia).[^4] The use of photography for these colonial purposes exposes a severe imbalance in power between colonisers and colonised: imperialists were able to maintain the upper hand when it came to dealing with indigenous populations through their improved knowledge of the territory and their ability to craft plans with the empirical information that the photograph provided. As well as this, the camera could be used as a weapon of fear: indigenous peoples who did not understand the concept of the camera had valid reason to fear its use. For example, Aboriginal communities in Australia often submitted to having their photos taken out of fear of the instrument which captured the image, and the notion that having one's image captured was equivalent to removing part of one's spirit further added to this fear.[^5] There is substantial evidence for an awareness and colonial weaponisation of this fear: the caption of one photograph taken of an Aboriginal woman reads "She Views the Photo-Fiend", strongly implying that the photographer did not have this woman's permission to take this image and that she has submitted to it out of fear;[^6] as well as this, the photographer Maxime du Camp boasts of tricking the subject of his photograph by telling him "that... the lens... was a cannon, which would vomit a hail of shot if he had the misfortune to move".[^7] It is clear from these examples that photography was used to support various colonial endeavours, and thus postcolonial scholarship redefines the photographic image and the camera itself, shifting it from being merely a piece of neutral evidence and a scientific instrument to a colonially charged symbol of domination over subjugated populations. 

Not only was photography complicit in the pursuit of imperial hegemony, it was also used as a tool to portray the 'orient' in a way that appealed to European sensibilities - as exotic, mysterious, and backwards.

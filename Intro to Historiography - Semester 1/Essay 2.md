## Assess the impact of postcolonial perspectives on the history of photography

Scholarship of the history of photography prior to the advent of postcolonial historiography and the concept of 'Orientalism' traditionally maintained a focus on technological progress, the science behind the camera, and photography as a worthy successor to the canvas. Such histories had a tendency to err heavily on the side of a eurocentric narrative - images of a stereotypical British Victorian family sitting and staring blankly into the camera spring to mind. However, the popularisation of 'orientalism' by Edward Said in his book of the same name introduced new, postcolonial perspectives to the history of photography. More recent scholarship has seen photography redefined as a tool of imperialist projects and a medium through which the concept of the exotic 'Orient' was promoted in Western circles, as well as promoting a novel view of photography as being practised and moulded by indigenous populations, not just the European photographers whom were often the sole focus of histories of photography in colonised regions. This essay shall argue that postcolonial perspectives have revolutionised the history of photography and introduced a new paradigm to the field, through analysis of orientalist tropes which photography reinforced, the practise of photography as a means of benefitting colonialist designs, the active participation of the subjects of orientalist photography, and the use of the colonial archive to shape future conceptions of the 'orient'.

Many photographers who travelled into Africa, India, and various other colonised regions, did so with a purportedly 'scientific' purpose.[^1] This purpose was multifaceted: some photographers used their medium to scout the geography of a region, with the photographs later being used to plan offensives or settlements in that area;[^2] others used photography to compile comprehensive ethnographies so that those in charge of imperial projects could identify different native populations and their attitudes towards colonial hegemony;[^3] and there was also a 'salvage motive' for some photographers, which was an attempt to document dwindling indigenous populations before they became extinct (the most obvious example of this being the images of indigenous Tasmanians in Australia).[^4] The use of photography for these colonial purposes exposes a severe imbalance in power between colonisers and colonised: imperialists were able to maintain the upper hand when it came to dealing with indigenous populations through their improved knowledge of the territory and their ability to craft plans with the empirical information that the photograph provided. As well as this, the camera could be used as a weapon of fear: indigenous peoples who did not understand the concept of the camera had valid reason to fear its use. For example, Aboriginal communities in Australia often submitted to having their photos taken out of fear of the instrument which captured the image, and the notion that having one's image captured was equivalent to removing part of one's spirit further added to this fear.[^5] There is substantial evidence for an awareness and colonial weaponisation of this fear: the caption of one photograph taken of an Aboriginal woman reads "She Views the Photo-Fiend", strongly implying that the photographer did not have this woman's permission to take this image and that she has submitted to it out of fear;[^6] as well as this, the photographer Maxime du Camp boasts of tricking the subject of his photograph by telling him "that... the lens... was a cannon, which would vomit a hail of shot if he had the misfortune to move".[^7] The lack of understanding surrounding the camera and the concept of the photograph meant that, for many photographers engaged in capturing indigenous populations, it was impossible to get informed consent from their subjects - they took images regardless. It is clear from these examples that photography was used to support various colonial endeavours, and thus postcolonial scholarship redefines the photographic image and the camera itself, shifting it from being merely a piece of neutral evidence and a scientific instrument to a colonially charged symbol of domination over subjugated populations. 

Not only was photography complicit in the pursuit of imperial hegemony, it was also used as a tool to portray the 'orient' in a way that appealed to European sensibilities - as exotic, mysterious, and backwards. European audiences provided a demand for such images and commercial photographers took the opportunity, travelling across the world in pursuit of images that they could sell at home. These photographs, however, were very rarely candid, nor produced on the terms of the subject. European photographers would surround their subjects with props, in front of carefully curated backdrops, in order to depict a scene which would pander to the pre-established convictions of an audience who had never experienced these regions of the world in person. For example, images of 'nautch' (dancing) girls are abundant in images of the Indian subcontinent. These are often highly sexualised, borderline erotic photographs which attempt to portray these girls as something exotic and prized, and as something 'other' to 'civilised' European society.[^8] In a similar vein, Aboriginal and African people were often photographed wearing certain garments in particular ways: women were often forced to lower their tops so that their breasts were exposed; men were made to wear clothing which the photographer believed to be 'traditional' for their culture.[^9] Such depictions reinforced the idea that these regions of the world were 'backwards'. Agency was entirely removed from many subjects of photography, and often indigenous people were either treated simply as aesthetic additions to photos rather than individuals, or were removed from the image entirely as they were not a 'desirable' aspect of the scene that the photographer wished to portray.[^10] Removing the humanity from these images allowed the photographer to characterise the orient as a constant, unchanging picture, and thus to enable some form of colonial nostalgia and pride, without the 'undesirable' aspects of empire.[^11] At its core, this appeal to the Orientalism of the audiences that were consuming these pictures was to reinforce the belief that the 'Western' world was civilised and held world hegemony, especially in the case of the British Empire, which could easily be portrayed as a benevolent and civilising power across the orient through this kind of imagery. Stark contrasts were depicted between local and imperial settlements, the former being dirty and disorganised and the latter quite the opposite. Photographers held the view that their task was to embellish and edit to provide an aspirational image, rather than one that was entirely realistic.[^13] Postcolonial perspectives have enabled historians to 'read between the lines' and to destabilise formalist and aesthetic readings of colonial photography, instead reading into the orientalist perspectives that these photographs would have provided their viewer with.

A highly characteristic feature of postcolonial perspectives is the recovery of information about those populations whose narratives may have been repressed under colonialism and orientalist perspectives. In the case of the history of photography, analysis under a postcolonial lens reveals photographic activity was not solely limited to European photographers, but that there was a significant amount of activity by local indigenous populations as well.  Because commercial photographic studios were, more often than not, European owned, the dissemination of these images could not have been as wide  Local government officials, for example, regularly used photography for their records and general administration, as did police departments. There is also evidence that Indian courts produced images of the aforementioned 'nautch' girls, and the Indian photographer Abbas Ali compiled a collection of twenty-four portraits of female courtiers, intended for an Indian male audience.[^12] Eurocentric histories have tended to ignore or omit indigenous photographical practices, and therefore more recent postcolonial perspectives have been invaluable in bringing these practices to light. As well as omitting those who took their own photographs, the record also excludes the agency of the subjects of photographs. Whilst often the subjects complied under duress, there is substantial evidence of exceptions to this rule. In some instances, monetary transactions took place - the photographer would pay their subjects to appear in the photograph; in other cases, certain demands were made which the photographer could not refuse without losing their prized image. In the case of one Ottoman princess, Zeïneb, compliance had conditions: the portrait must be taken "according to her wishes, or not at all". Despite protestations from her photographer, Mary Adelaide Walker (implying that Walker herself had designs on depicting Zeïneb as 'exotic' and 'oriental'), the photograph went ahead in the way that the princess wished, as Walker "could not risk the "not at all"".[^14] Here it can clearly be observed that the subject of the photograph had a certain level of agency in the taking of the image. In these cases, photographers had to enter into an unwritten contract with their subjects in order to procure what they needed. Colonial perspectives and histories often gloss over the ability of the subject to negotiate their terms, and thus postcolonial perspectives are necessary to understand that power imbalance between European photographers and those whom they were capturing was dynamic, and did not always favour the former.

Aside from the practise of photography itself, postcolonial perspectives on the history of photography have also significantly influenced views on the distributive and archival process of the images captured. One main aspect of this discourse is the dissemination of such images to the populations which they captured. The example that illustrates this best is the transmission of photographs to aboriginal communities in Australia. Photographers would often attempt to capture religious ceremonies, knowledge about which was often restricted to those elders who were senior enough. As a result of this practise, there were various instances in which members of the Aboriginal community were horrified when 'forbidden' religious knowledge was shown to them. This caused rifts between researchers and their Aboriginal subjects, which hindered research significantly. As a result, warnings were placed in anthologies of these images which warned researchers not to show any photographs to Aboriginal communities to avoid causing offence. Postcolonial perspectives and historical research on this topic have resulted in a very different contract between researchers and their indigenous subjects in Australia, both legally and socially - researchers now must ask permission to take photographs on Aboriginal land, and must ensure that their subjects consent to their photo being taken. From an archival perspective, postcolonial views and research have caused a major shift in the contextualisation of colonial imagery. Previously, archives and museums had very few qualms about displaying violent, colonial, or offensive imagery which had been captured by photographers throughout history, and, as above, this often caused offence to those who were either in or related to an individual who featured in a particular image. However, in more recent years, curators of archives and museums have taken steps to adopt policies of decolonisation in their displays and exhibits, contextualising images that they display if not choosing not to display them at all. Postcolonial perspectives and the framing of photography through an orientalist lens have led to substantial changes in the ways that archives and collections function, as well as changes in the ways which researchers undertake modern-day research.

From the information presented above, one must inevitably form the conclusion that postcolonial historiographical attitudes, heavily influenced by the 'Orientalism' first coined by Edward Said, have had a major impact on the history of photography as scholars practise it today. Prior to these influences, the field was characterised as highly eurocentric, prioritising the narratives of European explorers and photographers whilst omitting details of indigenous populations and their collective and individual agency. Decolonising the narrative behind the history of photography reveals that colonial-era photography grossly misrepresented the reality of the British Empire and the 'orient', and was often used as a tool for imperialist and colonial projects of expansion, as a weapon to assert technological superiority over subjugated populations, and to pander to occidental viewership's pre-determined ideas of what the 'orient' should look like. The conclusion that can be drawn from this is that the impact that postcolonial perspectives had on the history of photography was very substantial indeed.

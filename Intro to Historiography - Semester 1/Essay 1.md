### Is 'history from below' a reflection of the theories of Marx and Engels?

</br>

Whilst 'history from below' has its roots in pre-Marxist scholarship, its actual genesis was heavily influenced by the theories of Marx and Engels.[^1] Scholars such as E.P. Thompson, Eric Hobsbawm, and Raphael Samuel all had a Marxist or Communist background, and practised history using Marxist methodology. However, modern 'history from below' or 'people's history' has developed significantly since the scholarship of the mid-20<sup>th</sup> century and has diverged significantly in both methodology and ethos. One cannot therefore argue that 'history from below' remains true to its Marxist roots as its modern form has evolved too far to remain identifiable with Marxism, and this essay contends this position.

'History from Below' was indeed the brainchild of Marxist scholarship in the post-war years: the History Workshop at Ruskin College and the Fabian Society were major participants in its development. The works of Thompson, Hobsbawm, Samuel, Hill, are inherently Marxist: Thompson's seminal work 'The Making of the English Working Class' maintains a focus on the proletariat (in the Marxist tradition).
Marx saw 'history from below' as a method of studying History that would allow its practitioner to understand societal change and class struggle. The primary focus of this form of history was the 'working class' (although Marx never used this term), and thus it was a majoritarian study. Early Marxist scholars often spoke of 'people's history' as synonymous with 'labour history' or, as Thompson himself spoke of, 'history-as-industrial relations'.[^2] Given the Marxist focus on the revolutionary transition from Capitalism to Socialism, this makes sense: the proletariat were understood to be the primary agents in bringing this about. However, late-19<sup>th</sup> Century scholarship often makes the criticism that 'history from below' in this form focuses too much on class and collective agency, and tends to ignore individual characteristics such as gender, age, race, and individual agency. These criticisms led to significant development in the field: the modern form of 'people's history' distances itself from the focus on class struggle and the proletariat. Whilst still majoritarian, the majority is no longer identifiable with class or economic ideologies. This divergence from Marxist historical tradition leads one to argue that 'history from below' no longer reflects the theories of Marx and Engels.

Further to this, since the development of 'people's history' by the aforementioned groups, microhistories have become more common. Marx's position was that 'history from below' was the means to realising a final goal, this goal being an understanding of why and how societies change on the whole. However, the rise in the pursual of microhistories undermines this goal somewhat: this history may not reveal a wider class struggle or societal shift, and is not a case study for broader theories, but might simply provide the historian with an understanding of an individual's life, a certain culture, an event, and so on.[^3] Further to this, microhistories are not deterministic: a central tenet of Marx's form of history was that History develops necessarily and inevitably, with little interest in the 'mundane'; microhistories stray from this determinism These microhistories still fit the definition of 'history from below' but do not fit Marx's original aims.

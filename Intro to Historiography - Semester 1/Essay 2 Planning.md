## Assess the impact of postcolonial perspectives on the history of photography

### **Essay Plan**

#### **Introduction**

- Briefly outline traditional narratives in the history of photography (e.g., Eurocentric, technological progress, artistic lineage).
    
- Introduce postcolonial theory and its entry into photographic historiography (e.g., Edward Said, Homi Bhabha, Gayatri Spivak).
    
- State the essay’s argument: postcolonial perspectives have profoundly reshaped understandings of photographic practice by revealing power imbalances, challenging archival authority, and reframing images as sites of colonial encounter — but they also face methodological limits.
    

---

#### **Theme 1: Photography as a Colonial Technology**

**Key ideas**

- Photography historically aligned with imperial projects: documentation, classification, surveillance.
    
- Missionary, ethnographic, and anthropological uses of photography to construct racial hierarchies (e.g., Alphonse Bertillon, colonial ethnography in British India, French North Africa).
    
- Postcolonial critique exposes asymmetries of power in image production and circulation.  
    **Historiographical impact**
    
- Shift from viewing images as neutral evidence → understanding them as tools of domination and representation.
    
- Greater attention to the politics of looking, framing, and archiving.
    

---

#### **Theme 2: Representation, Othering, and the Visual Construction of Race**

**Key ideas**

- Orientalist visual tropes (Said) and how photography naturalised ideas of the exotic, primitive, and unchanging.
    
- Gendered colonial images (e.g., veiled women, eroticised bodies) and the intersection of race, gender, and power.  
    **Historiographical impact**
    
- Destabilising formalist and aesthetic readings of colonial photography.
    
- Emergence of visual culture approaches that prioritise discourse and ideology over artistic intention.
    

---

#### **Theme 3: Agency, Resistance, and the "Colonised Gaze"**

**Key ideas**

- Postcolonial scholarship highlights indigenous participation in photography: studio photographers in West Africa (e.g., Seydou Keïta, Malik Sidibé), South Asia, Caribbean.
    
- Counter-archives and subaltern uses of photography challenge colonial dominance.
    
- Examining moments of refusal, disruption, and self-fashioning in colonial-era portraiture.  
    **Historiographical impact**
    
- Rebalancing narratives from European photographers to colonised subjects and local practitioners.
    
- Reinterpretation of archives to recover suppressed histories.
    

---

#### **Theme 4: The Archive and Its Power**

**Key ideas**

- Colonial archives as instruments of control; archivists decide what is preserved and how it is classified.
    
- Postcolonial theorists critique the authority of archives and foreground gaps, silences, and erasures (Ann Stoler, Christopher Pinney).  
    **Historiographical impact**
    
- Shift from seeing archives as neutral repositories → understanding them as contested, politically charged structures.
    
- Photographic historiography becomes more reflexive about provenance, curation, and the ethics of display.
    

---

#### **Theme 5: Contemporary Re-readings, Decolonial Practices, and Ethical Challenges**

**Key ideas**

- Museums and galleries adopting decolonial curatorial approaches; debates over display, repatriation, and contextualisation of colonial images.
    
- Contemporary photographers (e.g., Zanele Muholi, Yinka Shonibare) reworking colonial visual legacies.
    
- Ethical questions around exhibiting colonial photographs today: consent, trauma, and the “right to opacity” (Glissant).  
    **Historiographical impact**
    
- Expanding the field beyond academic writing into public history, curatorial practice, and visual activism.
    
- Ongoing debates about how to show, interpret, or restrict access to violent/colonial imagery.
    

---

#### **Critiques and Limitations of Postcolonial Approaches**

- Risk of over-politicising images or reading colonial intent into ambiguous photographs.
    
- Possibility of re-centring the coloniser by focusing excessively on imperial contexts.
    
- Tension between theoretical abstraction (e.g., Bhabha) and material/empirical photographic study.
    
- Need for interdisciplinary balance between visual analysis, anthropology, and archival work.
    

---

#### **Conclusion**

- Reaffirm that postcolonial perspectives fundamentally altered photographic historiography by shifting its focus from aesthetic and technical narratives to power, representation, and global histories.
    
- Emphasise ongoing relevance: decolonial approaches continue to shape scholarship, museum practice, and ethical debates.
    
- Acknowledge complexities and the need for balanced, reflexive methodologies.

### The Orientalist Photograph

“Even a cursory glance at early Orientalist photography reveals its indebtedness to the conventions of Orientalist romantic paintings” (Behdad, 2013, p. 16)

“On the other hand, by photographically re-presenting these sites, Frith provides further evidence for their studies while at the same time popularizing Orientalism as a discourse. “the Orientalist photograph has a supplementary function, providing the viewer with a visual experience of the Orient otherwise unavailable for most people. Orientalist photography, therefore, neither displaced its painterly counterpart nor did it outdate its textual precursor, but joined them in making Orientalism thrive as a dominant mode of representation.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 18)

“European travelers were nurtured as early as the 1850s by images of Constantinople, Jerusalem, and Egypt by pioneer photographers such as James Robertson, Du Camp, Teynard, and Salzmann. The Orientalist photograph democratized the access to Orientalist representation by liberating it from its elite confinement in the salon. The Orientalist photograph was thus not merely an expression of a European desire for the Orient but also productive of the lure of the East.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 18)

“the Orientalist photograph did not counter the traveler’s own experience of the Oriental real by making it seem banal but rather enhanced it through the pleasure of identification” (Behdad, 2013, p. 19)

“The photographic image was not merely an indexical reference point for the Orientalist traveler but the mediator of his or her desire for the Orient. The Orientalist photograph thus “nurtured” the desire for the Orient, helping its development as a cultural phenomenon throughout the West.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 19)

“and opens a new way of investigation to Orientalists, just as it offers a horizon particular to artists’ studies.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 20)

“From the very beginning of its invention, the camera provided Orientalists with a tool to visually “rescue” the remnants of the Orient’s antiquities just as they were pillaging the remnants of historical monuments in the Middle East” (Behdad, 2013, p. 22)

“Viewed as “bad ghosts” without any photogenic quality, the indigenous people either are utterly erased from the scene or appear occasionally as anonymous physical blotches to provide a sense of scale.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 24)

“In contrast to the superabundance of decontextualized, studio-based images of people produced by resident photographers in the Middle East, the absence of humanity in the Orientalist view photography of Egyptian antiquity and the Holy Land at once participates in a form of static monumentalism that underpins Orientalist historiography and enables the possibility of colonial nostalgia.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 24)

“The photographer’s intervention here goes well beyond mere framing, lighting, or focus, for everything in the image denotes a staged scene: the artificial backdrop, the everpresent props, the unnatural gaze of the sitter.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 24)

“The Orientalist image is marked by excessive textual anchorage. In principle, the Orientalist photograph has the potential to be polysemous like any other image, but its potential for the “floating chain” of signifieds it carries is undermined by the almost ever-presence of the title in the frame and the repetition or translation of the title in the album” (Behdad, 2013, p. 25)

“In this sense, the Orientalist photograph is excessively labeled, demonstrating a profound anxiety about the potential for the plurality of signifieds in it” (Behdad, 2013, p. 25)

“The title in the image is not simply a denoted description of what is portrayed but an expression of a profound desire to fix the meaning of the image, to deprive it of any symbolic message or alternative meaning.” (Behdad, 2013, p. 26)

““Is the correct paradigm for interpreting this kind of work one of politics and power, or one of imagination and inspiration?”” (Behdad, 2013, p. 28)

## Orients and Occidents: Colonial Discourse Theory and the Historiography of the British Empire

“he sought to show how, through rhetorical devices, linguistic conventions, and narrative structures, these texts represented Oriental subjects in arbitrary, demeaning, and inferioriz-ing ways, while claiming to speak in the name of objective science and universal truth.” (Washbrook, 1999, p. 2)

“By taking as his point of reference its relationship to an Oriental ‘Other’, Said was able to reveal a number of previously ‘occluded’ features in European culture itself. First, he showed how Europe’s aversion to this Other, through successive epochs, re ected deep epistemic continuities across conventional historical periods. Secondly, he demonstrated the ways in which the Enlightenment’s approaches to knowledge had the effect of putting Europe at the centre of all resulting ‘universal’ schemas—as representing the standards by which the rest of the world was to be judged, the markers from which it was to take its own identity. ‘The Orient’ was ‘backward’ in relation to Europe: its only meaningful history—its path to Modernization—was to follow Europe’s course. Finally, he argued that Europe’s propensity to demean Others was crucial to how it identi ed and evaluated itself.

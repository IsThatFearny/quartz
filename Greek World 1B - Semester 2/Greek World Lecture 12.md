### The Trial of Socrates

- Socrates himself wrote nothing - our knowledge of his philosophy comes from Plato (who is believed to generally be the more reliable source) and Xenophon (although not Aristophanes' *Clouds* as he is presented as an amalgamation of various intellectuals).
- Socrates was tried for 'impiety' (not believing in the city's Gods and 'introducing new divinities') and 'corrupting the youth' - the prosecutor (Meletus) sought a penalty of death
- The prosecutor claimed to be acting in the public interest, and had to show that Socrates was an active danger to the state

</br>
#### Synopsis 

1. Proemium - had the aim of encouraging the goodwill of listeners. Claims that the opposition is lying, consider the facts rather than how they are stated, etc.
2. Prothesis - makes the distinction between 2 sets of accusers: the old accusers condemn Socrates as a teacher, the new accuse him of 'religious innovation' and corrupting the youth. Socrates explains the difficulties in making his case
3. The defence
	- Against the old accusers: Socrates argues that he never took any payment and is therefore not a sophist (a natural scientist). They think they know *all* because they know something. Those with leisure (i.e. young aristocrats) imitate Socrates, and thus annoy their parents (argues that the charges are political)
	- Against the new accusers: Meletus is not serious as he has never cared about these things before; Socrates affirms his faith and argues that he is not an atheist
4. Excursus - Socrates refuses to stop his actions if this were a condition of his discharge; he proves his earnestness in his care for the city; he argues that he has acted the same in public and in private; he says that the real cause of the anger directed at him is his pleasure taken in refuting the ignorant
5. Epilogue - Refuses to appeal to pity; a counter-charge is proposed by Socrates (who has now been convicted); prefers to die than live a lie, speculates on the nature of death

</br>

#### Was Socrates Guilty?

- Athenian 'guilt' was not necessarily based on the technical contravention of law, but rather what was best for the good of the community. By this standard, Socrates was justly condemned - his conviction was intended to 'purify' the population by removing an 'irreligious traitor'
- It is, however, also argued that Socrates was perhaps simply unorthodox - Plato portrays him as more pious than an average Athenian (his *Apology* of Socrates is actually the prosection of Athens, wanting the reader to be the jury)

### Greek Historiography

- 5<sup>th</sup>-4<sup>th</sup> centuries BCE: Herodotus, Thucydides, Xenophon
- Hellenistic period (323-31 BCE): Polybius, Diodorus, and Dionysus of Halicarnassus

It is important to note that these are *surviving* sources - we know of approx. 600 alternative authors who may have been writing history in different ways on different topics

</br>

### Roman Historiography

- Early 2<sup>nd</sup> - mid 1<sup>st</sup> centuries BCE: Very very little surviving history
- 1<sup>st</sup> century BCE - 1<sup>st</sup> century CE: Sallust, Velleius, Paterculus, Livy, and perhaps Caesar. Enough remains of these sources to build a relatively comprehensive bibliography of Roman history

We are missing Sallust's *Histories* (aside from specific speeches and letters); approx. 100 books of Livy; nearly two thirds of Tacitus' *Histories* and *Annals*, etc. etc.

</br>

### How can we get further?

#### Testimonials

</br>


Occasionally, other writers mention their contemporaries or predecessors, whether that be in terms of style, genre, character, etc. e.g. Nikolaos of Damaskos is mentioned in a 10<sup>th</sup> century CE Byzantine 'encylopaedia', so to speak. This excerpt describes his relationship with Herod, the fact that he was a Platonic or Peripatetic philosopher, and the fact that he wrote multiple works including a Universal History and a Life and Upbringing of Caesar.

Whilst this is helpful, we must then fact-check the information that we are receiving, usually through other historians or mentions in classical texts, e.g. Nikolaos is mentioned in the works of Josephus. However, we must then question the objectivity of alternative historians, whether they have their own agenda, etc.

</br>

#### Fragments

Fragments of classical works survive in various future works, through which we are able to get a sense of how an author wrote and the content of their works. However, again, there are difficulties in this: those who decide which fragments to include and what form to include them in may have had their own agenda or have made 'corrections' for legibility, clarity, etc.

</br>

#### Epitomes

Ancient writers occasionally gave summaries of certain works, describing what happened in each book in a shortened form, which allows us to understand a basic overview of the works of a certain author. For example, there are various epitomes of Livy's books which we are missing. A unique thing about epitomes is that we sometimes have the full surviving work as well as its epitome, which means that we are able to compare and contrast the two and understand to what extent the epitomisers are editing the content of the work, and how reliable they can be when attempting to understand works which may have been epitomised, but for which we do not possess the full manuscript.

</br>

#### Developmental Ideas: Ancient and Modern

Certain excerpts from classical authors serve the sole purpose of commenting on another author. A prime example is Dionysus of Halicarnassus' *Thucydides*, which was a letter to another historian (Tubero) commentating on Thucydides prose, character, motivations, methodology, etc. We know, for example, that Thucydides exercised significant critical judgement, excluded so-called 'legendary material' and myth, and that his methodology was largely autobiographical (i.e. he was present at many of the events which he describes).

In the modern era, one of the most influential voices in ancient historiography was Felix Jacoby. He wanted to gather every excerpt and fragment pertaining to Ancient Greek Historiography in six volumes. Only the first three parts were published (due to his untimely death), but the rest was completed by an editor. Jacoby came up with a developmental and categorical structure, as below:

**Categories of Greek Historiography (according to Jacoby)**

- Mythology/Genealogy
- Ethnography (the broader study of lands, peoples, etc.)
- Chronography (local histories in a very fixed form (i.e. dating))
- Contemporary History (Jacoby believed that this is what the pinnacle of History is)
	- War monographs
	- Continuous universal history
	- Individual-centered history
- Horography (similar to chronography, but Jacoby saw this as being developed later)

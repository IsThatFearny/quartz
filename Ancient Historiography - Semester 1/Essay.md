## What can we say about the audience of Josephus and how did this shape his approach to history?

</br>

Flavius Josephus (c. 37-100 CE) is often characterised as a 'Judeo-Roman' historian, positioned in between Roman, Greek, and Judean literary and historiographical traditions. His audience was, in a similar fashion, comprised of multiple different ethnic, political, and social groups, and his writing often reflects such a diverse readership. In writing *Bellum Judaicum*, *Antiquitates Judicae*, *Contra Apionem* and *Vita*, Josephus charted a course intersecting the views of his readership so as not to offend their sensibilities or to bring himself into disrepute.[^1] This essay shall discuss the composition of Josephus' audience, and analyse the content (and lack thereof) of his works, his use of rhetoric, and his presentation of controversial events to conclude that Josephus' audience caused his methodology in writing history to tread the line between apologetic retelling and accurate reporting.

A focus of much scholarship on Josephus has been his position in Rome and his Roman audience. Josephus was of a status in Roman society that was enviable for a foreigner: the emperor Vespasian (with whom he was in close contact) bestowed Roman citizenship upon him as well as providing him accomodation; he changed his name to align with Roman naming conventions; he was a close adviser of Titus during the siege of Jerusalem; and was engaged with Roman political and literary life. His closest audience in both social and geographical terms, therefore, was Roman in nature. Josephus was able to present copies of *War* to Vespasian and Titus, so one can imply that one aspect of his audience was the elites of Rome. Not only this, but intellectual and religious circles may have also provided Josephus with a Roman audience - certain sympathisers with the plight of the Jews such as Flavius Clemens (who would later be executed on charges relating to involvement in Judaism) and others who had suffered at the hands of Domitian for similar offences. It is clear, then, that Josephus had an audience in Rome.
On account of this, it has been argued by some scholars that Josephus' audience was solely Roman. However such an extreme view inevitably excludes discussion on the transmission of his works to the Jewish diaspora and Greek readers. His audience, for example, must have had mastery of the Greek language, being the language in which he chose to write. This is significant because if Josephus had intended his audience to remain solely Roman he might have been inclined to write in Latin. Writing in Greek had the potential to expand Josephus' audience because the average literate Roman had a command of both Greek and Latin, but their Greek equivalent was unlikely to understand Latin. Language choice strongly implies that Josephus chose to write for as Greek audience as well as a Roman one. Aside from language, Josephus expressly states that he hopes *Antiquities* will be transmitted to "the whole Greek-speaking world" (A.J. 1.5). We know, therefore, that Josephus is writing for a Greek audience as well as Roman one. This includes Jewish readers, most notably Herod Agrippa II, who was then King of Judea, and with whom Josephus was in near continuous correspondence as a source for *Antiquties*. Josephus' audience was varied in its composition and was not by any means limited solely to his Roman contemporaries.

Such diversity in audience compelled Josephus to adapt his historical methodology. A key aspect of this was avoiding causing offence to his powerful Roman audience whilst also presenting the events of which he wrote in the most accurate way possible. One of the ways in which he regularly does this is ascribing certain events to the actions of God. For example, it was not thought well of in Roman culture to destroy the worship place of a deity, even if said deity was not Roman. During the sack of Jerusalem, the Romans did just that and burnt the temple; therefore when reporting the event Josephus has to avoid accusations. He therefore makes statements such as "That building, however, God, indeed long since, had sentenced to the names" (B.J. 6.250), implying that it was not the fault of the Romans, but rather God's will. Throughout *Antiquities* and *War*, he continues to associate Roman victory over the Jews with divine will, further avoiding any accusations that he might be criticising his audience and their lineage. In a similar vein, throughout *War* the Romans are entirely absolved of blame for the devastation of Judea and the atrocities of the war. Instead, Josephus blames the Jewish rebels and the wider Jewish community for forcing the Romans' hand (B.J. 1.10). Josephus also makes certain omissions from his writing: for example, he omits any reference to the fourth kingdom (viz. the Roman Empire) being the weakest in the succession as implied in Daniel 2:39, and also omits the biblical implication that the Empire was flawed, being made of iron and clay: Josephus merely reflects on the strength of the iron and the everlasting nature of the fourth kingdom (Dan. 2:42; A.J. 10.209).[^2] The distinct lack of discussion about the covenant between YHWH and the Jews is another attempt at 'romanising the narrative' - the language of the covenant may have seemed offensive to Roman tastes.[^3] Whilst we cannot guarantee that Josephus was writing to avoid offence to his Roman audience, we may easily discern this: blatant transgressions against the Flavian regime under which he was living would have spelled doom for Josephus, and he would likely have been punished severely. Such a motive would compel one to cater to their audience, and so it can be asserted that Josephus edited his work to do so.

The narrative above, that Josephus' works are Flavian propaganda, is one that, when taken in isolation, is highly problematic. It portrays Josephus as a historian whose only purpose is to pander to his patrons. But the reader of Josephus must also take note of the apologetic nature of his writing when referring to the Jews. Josephus is, primarily, a Jew, and he makes sure to position himself as such, not only to give authority to his account but also to allow him to make the case for the defence of the Jewish people.

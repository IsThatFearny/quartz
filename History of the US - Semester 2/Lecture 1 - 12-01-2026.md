## The Colonial Backdrop

- Declaration of Independence instantly makes native americans scapegoats by accusing them of being on the side of the British
- First British Colonies: Jamestown 1607; Plymouth 1620
- Thirteen colonies appeared over the next 100 years
- British authority gradually asserted itself throughout the 17th century

</br>

- Mainland colonies in British North America grew at approx. 0.5% per annum during the 18th century. By 1775 the colonies' GDP was 40% the size of Britain's, largely owing to the surge in population and transatlantic trade
- Population: 600,000, inc. 280,000 African slaves; 200,000 Scots-Irish; 85,000 Germans

</br>

### Religion

- Puritanism did not thrive beyond the 17th century, but non-conformism amongst the colonists did
- 'The Great Awakening' was a religious revival that swept the colonies in the 1730s-40s

</br>

### Governance

- Each colony was ruled separately
- Each colony had a governor appointed by the crown, who appointed a council. The governor answered directly to the crown
- Benjamin Franklin's Albany Plan was a suggestion to change this form of governance

</br>

### Economy

- BY 1700 colonial imports (inc. West Indies) were 20% of British imports and the colonies received 10% of British exports
- By 1773 colonial imports and exports were worth 33% of the British totals. The colonies exported tobacco, flour, fish, rice, and wheat. They imported luxury manufactured items: clothing, books, etc.

</br>

### The Seven Years War

- Between 1689 and 1763 the major European powers fought a series of wars which involved their colonies in the New World. The most decisive of these was the Seven Years' War which resulted in the expulsion of the French from North America
- After victories at Quebec and Montreal the British American Empire was at its high-water-mark
- For many American participants, this picture is idealised and they felt marginalised and undervalued
- At the end of the Seven Years' War (1763) the British budget deficit was in excess of £122 million. Prime minister George Grenville proposed the Stamp Act in March 1765, intended to raise revenue in America

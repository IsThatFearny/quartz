# Historiography Essay

## How revolutionary was the American Revolution

</br>

The American Revolution is often described as the seminal moment in the birth of the modern USA, a 'radical' movement which upturned the character of the nation entirely, transforming it into what was allegedly the first modern democracy. However, there is great debate over how 'radical' this revolution truly was: many historians argue that the American Revolution was in fact not revolutionary at all, and was instead the embodiment of failed political aspirations to equality and virtue. This essay shall analyse these two perspectives, specifically with relation to two of the primary debates within the discourse: whether the Revolution's 'radicalism' should be judged by the amelioration of African-Americans and women; and whether the revolution transformed the nation's governance and commercial aspirations as opposed to simply maintaining the ongoing trends of the colonies.

Perhaps the most recognisable proponent of the 'radical' revolution is Gordon Wood. Wood makes the argument that radicalism should be measured by the amount of social change that took place, and that the American Revolution "created a society fundamentally different from the colonial society of the eighteenth century".[^1] He argues that the common American felt liberated by their oppression under the contempt of the elites. However, this argument is subject to fierce debate from two fronts. Some, such as Zuckerman and Smith, make the criticism that it excludes 'bottom-up' testimony and only focuses on the lived experience of elites - this seems entirely contrary to Wood's own argument.[^2] He reserves the term 'revolutionary' for these elites, and there is also the glaring omission of the experiences of those living in the South. It is therefore argued that he cannot claim give fair treatment to non-elite Americans. Other historians contend that this view of the American revolution marginalises the oppression of African-American slaves and of women, and therefore cannot be a true exposition of the 'liberation' of the American people. Not only this, but the criticism is levelled that this view also omits the role of African-Americans in the revolution itself, for example in the case of Lord Dunmore's 'Ethiopian Regiment'.[^3] Wood makes a single defence of these criticisms: he contends that "saying that all white males were equal in 1776 was something revolutionary and new under the sun", accusing his critics of gross anachronism when discussing the liberation of minority groups.[^4] He argues that the oppressed, in his narrative, are not the same oppressed that one might think of in present discourse. He also makes the point that the revolution formed the basis for the future emancipation of these minority groups, causing a chain of causation that would not have occurred without the American Revolution's success. Overall, on this argument, Wood succeeds in defending his view - when reading his critics, their anachronistic perspectives are easily identifiable and fallaciously judge the American Revolution by modern standards.

The second facet to the discourse surrounding the 'revolutionary' nature of the American Revolution is whether it succeeded in substantially changing society and governance in the colonies. Here, we must return again to Gordon Wood, and his argument that, despite little transformation in wealth disparity, there was significant enough social change, both real and perceived, to call the American Revolution 'radical'.[^5] He makes the case for a transformation in the definition of 'labour', stating that an essential characteristic of the revolution was that "suddenly, all who worked for a living were no longer willing to put up with their hitherto degraded and oppressed condition", and that the revolution was an expression of disdain with the aristocratic class.[^6] Further to this, Wood also makes the argument that the revolution transformed the governance of the union, with democracy developing as a direct result. These arguments, as with the ones above, are also subject to harsh criticism. Wood and others who hold a similar view are accused of exaggerating the size of the aristocracy, as well as the control that they allegedly exerted over the common man. Zuckerman, for example, claims that "small farmers did not defer or even submit to the dictates of mercantile masters", and Greene points out that pre-revolutionary society lacked the "social foundations necessary for either an aristocracy or a social system of legally established ranks".[^7] Beyond this, Wood and his peers are also charged with mischaracterising the evolution of democracy during the revolution. Greene, for example, makes a strong case for the revolution was not as 'radical' in its transformation of governance as others might suggest, because the population of the pre-revolutionary colonies were already subscribed to the ideas that "society was anterior to government".[^8] Greene sums his remarks by stating that "[the revolution] changed the form, but not the substance of their government".[^9] One is inclined to agree with Greene and his fellows, and Wood's view of radicalism does not seem to hold substantially in this context - despite his emphasis on the 'common man' throughout his work, he seems to ignore their tendencies in political thought and agency.

The American Revolution, therefore, is a multifaceted creature. On one hand, Wood's argument that, whilst the politics of liberation were limited to white men, the revolution was radical in its proclamation of equality for 'all', seems to succeed in the face of anachronistic criticism. On the other hand, the 'radical' revolution does not hold up when interrogated about the nature of societal and governmental continuity - one sees little change between the pre and post revolutionary US. Whilst the Revolution cannot be "the most radical and most far-reaching event in American history", it can lay claim to an amelioration of the common man such as had never been seen before, and in that sense, certainly, it is 'revolutionary'.

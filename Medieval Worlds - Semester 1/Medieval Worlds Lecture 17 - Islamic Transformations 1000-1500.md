### Two Rulers

- Mahmud of Ghazna (r. 998-1030)
	- Sultan of the Ghaznavid Empire in Central Asia, and controlled a large military empire from today's Iran to Afghanistan, Pakistan and India
	- Defeated the Saffarids, Samanids, and several Indian polities
	- Amassed huge wealth and resources through expeditions to the subcontinent
	- His capital, Ghazni, rivalled Baghdad with its political, cultural, and economic affluence
- Mansa Musa (r. 1312-1337)
	- 9th ruler of the Mali Empire, West Africa
	- Amassed great wealth from taxing the salt trade and gold
	- Made a pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324-25 - he had a large entourage during this, from 8000-60,000 people; 500 slaves carried 900kg of gold
	- His stay in Cairo led to the decline of the value of gold in Arabia (oversaturation of gold in the market)

Both of these rulers represent the changed political dynamic of the Islamic world after 1000CE. Both come from non-Arab origin, which challenged the narrative of Arab supremacy. Mahmud was of Mamluk Turkic origin, and Musa was from Ghana. The decline of the 'Abbasids was clearly underway by this point.

</br>

### Fall of Baghdad and the Mongol conquest

This was an episode with huge cultural and political memories in the Islamic world. It consisted of Mongol invasions across the Silk Route, beginning with Genghis Khan. The fall of Baghdad was orchestrated by Hulegu Khan (Ghengis' grandson), killing the 'Abbasid caliph, al-Mu'tasim in 1258. This marked the end of the 'Abbasid empire

</br>

### Political Transformations

**For context, also see [[Medieval Worlds Lecture 12 - The Formation of the Islamic World#Other major political powers]]**

Minor dynasties became major dynasties. The 'Abbasid empire disintegrated and splintered before collapsing entirely, which allowed new regimes to consolidate their power. This saw the emergence of new centres of power, instead of frontiers. There was a drastic shift in the centre of power to non-Arab lands.

New power dynamics evolved, such as the rise of *ajam* (non-Arabs/Persians) and *mawali* (clients) as major political powers. New paradigms and dynamics were created in terms of gender, ethnicity, and language. There were also various slave dynasties, such as the **Mamluks** of Egypt and India.

</br>

### Intellectual traditions

Political expansions continued, as did doctrinal divisions, primarily in the form of the three major Islamic sects, the Sunni, Shi'a, and Khawarij. This influenced the development of Islamic intellectual and textual traditions.

There was scientific exploration beyond internal division. This was the era of translations (e.g. Toledo translation school), and there was a book revolution in the 9th and 10th centuries, largely due to a fall in the price of paper and the spread of new cultural practices.

Scholasticism spread from the Islamic world to Europe, but declined in the Islamic world. However, there was a renewal of the use of commentaries and philosophical/anti-philosophical debates.

</br>

### Standardisation of Religious Sects

- Sunnis
	- Four major schools of Islamic Law
	- Two theological schools
- Shi'a
	- Many internal divisions
	- Three major schools of Law
- Khawarij
	- One school of law
	- Prominent in Oman, but also in North Africa

</br>

Among Sunnis, there was four major schools of Islamic law: Hanafism, Malikism, Shafi'ism and Hanbalism. These were largely standardised in the 13th century. There were two major theological schools: Ash'arism and Maturidism.

## The Third-Century Crisis

Between 235AD and 284AD, the empire had a crisis on both its eastern and western frontiers. Various civil wars took place, the Antonine plague returned, religious tensions heightened, the currency was devalued as a result of too many coins in the economy, and major geopolitical shifts took place. 22 emperors ruled during this period.

There is a major problem with sourcing the information about the Third-Century Crisis, largely due to the fact that the main source is the *Historia Augusta*, a collection of imperial biographies written after 370AD which pretends to have been written by six different authors, and muddles fact and fiction.

The amount of silver in a Denarius and the amount of gold in an aueri, antoniniani, and denarii all decreased over the period, causing more coins to be minted and thus causing inflation to occur.

## Diocletian

- Born in Dalmatia as *Gaius Valerius Diocles* to a middle0ckass family
- Had a successful military career, and reached the post of commander of the imperial bodyguard
- Promoted under the Emperor Carus
- Assumes power and changes his name after the death of Numerian in November 284
- Defeated Numerian's brother Carinus and becomes the sole ruler of the empire in July 285

### Reforms

#### Governance and Succession

**First stage**
- July 285AD: Diocletian appoints Maximian as Caesar (prospective heir). Maximian was a long-term friend and comrade-in-arms of Diocletian, and remained absolutely loyal to Diocletian throughout his life
- Diocletian keeps the Eastern part of the Empire for himself to rule, and left Maximian to rule the west
- April 286AD: Maximian is promoted to Augustus (full emperor), but always subordinated to Diocletian as the senior emperor
- **There is an alternative reconstruction:** Maximian may have been made Augustus on 13th December 285AD without being Caesar beforehand

</br>

**Second stage**
- March 293AD: Diocletian appoints two younger comrades, Constantius and Galerius, as Caesars
- Constantius resided in the West and worked with Maximian, and Galerius resided in the East and worked with Diocletain
- Constantius married Maximian's step-daughter, and Galerius married Diocletian's daughter

</br>

#### Provincial Administration

- The existing provinces were divided into much smaller units
- Italy lost its privileged status of fiscal exemption and was also divided into smaller units
- By the early fourth century, the provinces were grouped into 12-15 *dioceses*, managed by a *vicarius*
- Later in the fourth century, the entire empire was divided into three/four prefectures, under a *praetorian prefect*
- Provincial governors were no longer military officials and were now only responsible for administration of justice and collection of taxes
- Military officials were separated from the civil administration, having authority in several adjacent provinces
- The emperors were extremely mobile in this period, residing in a 'residence city' near the frontiers (Antioch, Nicomedia, Sirmium, Milan, Trier) and moving quickly whenever needed
- The central administration of the government travelled with the emperor
- The senate in Rome embodied the unity of imperial governance

</br>

#### Economic Policy

- Inflation was a major problem and was caused by a scarcity of precious metals during the period
- Romans coins had an intrinsic value lower than their nominal value as a result, and thus sellers had to raise prices
- For those citizens who had a fixed salary, this was an issue
- To solve the problem, Diocletian attempted to put a *price edict* in place
- Diocletian introduced a more rational tax system, the *iugatio et capitatio*, centred around the extension of taxable land and the quantity of labourers necessary to keep it productive

</br>

#### Religious policy


- There was a new focus on religious uniformity, which was perceived as essential for the stability of the Empire
- Special preference was given to imperially sponsored cults
- There was an increased persecution of religious rivals, e.g. the Manichaeans were persecuted as a result of their perceived towards the Persians
- The 'Great Persecution' of the Christians took place between 303-311AD, with punishments including exile, confiscation of property, imprisonment, torture, and execution
- Christianity was officially persecuted, although certain officials would decide not to uphold imperial policies

# The Julio-Claudians

</br>

- Belonged to the Julian or Claudian family (or both)
</br>

- Augustus: r. 27 BCE - 14 CE
	- Great-Nephew and adopted son of J. Caesar
-  Tiberius: r. 14 - 37 CE
	- Stepson and adopted son of Augustus
- Gaius (Caligula): r. 37 - 41 CE
	- Great-nephew and adopted son of Tiberius; great-grandson of Augustus
- Claudius: r. 41 - 54 CE
	- Uncle of Caligula; nephew of Tiberius
- Nero: r. 54 - 68 CE
	- Great-nephew, stepson, and adopted son of Claudius; great-great-grandson of Augustus

</br>

### Main Historical Sources

- Augustus: *Res Gestae* ('Things done')
- Philo of Alexandria, c. 20 BCE - c. 50 CE: *Embassy to Gaius*
- Tacitus, c. 56 - c. 120 CE: *Annals* and *Histories*
- Suetonius, c.69 - after 122 CE: *Lives of the Caesars*
- Dio Cassius c. 165 - c. 235 CE: *Roman History*

Historical sources largely present the Julio-Claudians in a negative light (Augustus comes out best, Caligula and Nero come off worst). The writers of these sources often came from the Roman senatorial class (e.g. Tacitus and Seutonius), whose power had been diminished in favour of giving greater power to the emperor; thus these writings are tinged with disdain for the emperor, and in particular imperial women (wives and the like).

</br>

### Succession security

There was no expectation of longevity from Augustus - he began to build his mausoleum before he took the title 'Augustus' and before he become *imperator*. He continuously definined the people who would succeed him:

- Marcus Claudius Marcellus (Augustus' nephew and son-in-law)
- Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa (Augustus' general and son-in-law)
- Gaius and Lucius Caesar (Augustus' grandsons and adopted sons; sons of Agrippa); given the title '*principes iuventutis*', 'leader of the youth'
- **Tiberius Claudius Nero (Augustus' stepson, son-in-law, and adopted son)**
- Agrippa Postumus (Augustus' grandson and adopted son; son of Agrippa)

Augustus would often marry off his daughter (Julia the Elder) to a potential successor (hence the plethora of son-in-laws). Likewise, successors were also often marked out on the reverse-side of coinage, had their names inscribed on major buildings, or had gladiatorial games held in their names. Each of these successors died before Augustus, aside from Tiberius Claudius Nero, who would eventually succeed him; and Agrippa Postumus (who was banished to an island and summarily murdered once Tiberius came to power).

</br>

### Augustus' Legacy

Augustus wished to leave behind a legacy and set an example to future generations. Thus, in penning the *Res Gestae* (and subsequently placing it in his mausoleum), he was able to preserve his legacy **as he wished to portray it**. The *Res Gestae* was also circulated around the empire (hence its preservation). Further, he wanted to create a template for successful imperial rule - the *Res Gestae* can be seen as guidebook of sorts for future rulers.

Similarly to how Augustus portrayed successors on coinage, so too did his successors (in reverse) - they would put an image of Augustus on the reverse-side to emphasise their lineage and their right of succession.

Even those writers who had disdain for the principate (one-man rule) such as Tacitus portrayed Augustus as a relatively benign and model ruler. He accepts that the Empire remained stable as long as Augustus was in charge: there was little war, and most looked to the sovereign with 'no immediate misgivings'.

</br>

### Tiberius

At the beginning of Tiberius' reign, he generally followed the example set by Augustus: he turned down honours, refused to allow temples to be decreed to him, nor did he allow statues to be built unless he gave his prior consent. He refused the title *imperator* and would not use the title 'Augustus' unless writing to kings or princes.

Tiberius introduced the facade of a free state, referring most public issues to the senate (even though the senate had very little power in reality).

However, Tiberius soon began change his attitude: he would not change appointments to various offices, would not host gladiatorial games, allowed territory to be ceded, completed no public works, etc.

</br>

### Caligula

Caligula too began to follow the example of Augustus, emphasising family devotion, publishing imperial accounts, allowing magistrates freedom in the judiciary, attempting to restore elections, etc. He also gave gladiatorial games, put on theatre, and threw gifts upon the population.

Caligula had a fall from grace, however. He began to give himself new titles and epithets, and came very close to turning the principate into a monarchy. When he became aware that he had risen higher than any other king, he began to claim that he was worthy of Godliness.

</br>

### Claudius

Once Claudius came to power, he removed any dissent towards the principate (some wanted to restore the republic). Similarly to his predecessors, he made out that he was similar to Augustus, by promoting magnificent public works, taking responsibility for the economy and output of Rome, holding games, giving out presents, etc.

He did, however, act as a servant rather than an emperor. He was at the mercy of the freedmen and his wives, on whose behalf he largely acted.

</br>

### Nero

Nero declared that he would model himself entirely on Augustus - he spared nothing in demonstrating these qualities. Yet again, however, Nero failed to live up to the Augustan example: he did not extend the empire, nor did he want to; he abolished the old names of various locations and things; he also had a desire to glorify himself, wanting to call April 'Neronius' and the city of Rome 'Neropolis'.

</br>

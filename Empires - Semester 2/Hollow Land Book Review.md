# Hollow Land Book Review


</br>

*Hollow Land* is Eyal Weizman's investigation into the Israeli regime of occupation, apartheid, and imperial expansion, with a specific focus on Weizman's speciality of architecture and urban planning. The book details how the Israeli regime has utilised architecture to maintain and expand their occupation and domination of Palestine (alternatively referred to as the Occupied Territories). Weizman defines architecture in both a physical sense and also in a sense which transcends the physical, placing heavy emphasis on the role of generals, politicians, and elites as the builders of a constructed reality. The focus of the book, therefore, is two-fold: Weizman details the physical architecture of the occupation, such as the barrier walls, checkpoints, and the architecture of the city of Jerusalem; but also allocates a great deal of the book to more ideological and theoretical 'architecture', including the urban warfare tactics of the Israeli Occupation Forces (IOF), the use of settlements as a medium of expansion, the 2005 evacuation of Gaza and the militarisation of Palestinian airspace.[^1]

It is important to note the temporal context of the book, as it was published in 2007 in the aftermath of the second Intifada. Therefore, it does not take into account the Gaza War of 2008-9, nor of 2014, and it cannot account for the genocide undertaken by the Israeli government since October 7th 2023, during which much of the architecture which Weizman details has not been in use. As such, some of the information within the book has now been rendered inaccurate. However, this by no means limits it as a source nor as a medium for the discussion of imperialism.

In writing *Hollow Land*, Weizman was in a position of privilege as an Israeli - when curating his sources, he was able to access Israeli politicians and generals (the Venn diagram of the two is effectively a circle), who were forthcoming with information about such things as the 'walking through walls' methods of the IOF's urban warfare strategy and the ideology of targeted assassinations.[^2] Where a Palestinian writer would not be able to access these sources, as an Israeli Weizman is able to provide insight into the inner workings of the occupation and the theory behind it, evidencing this with information directly from the mouths of the orchestrators. Furthermore, Weizman was involved with the Palestinian Ministry of Planning, as well as various human rights organisations, and thus has firsthand, on-the-ground experience.[^3] The sources that Weizman is able to draw on are unique and give his arguments significant weight compared with others in the field.

Weizman's primary intervention, which he refers to throughout *Hollow Land*, is that of the 'politics of verticality'.[^4] He argues that the Israeli occupation should not be viewed through a purely cartographical, 2-dimensional lens, but instead that a third dimension must be added to a view of the occupation in order to truly understand the Israeli imperial project as one that utilises topography as well. This 3-dimensional understanding encompasses the tunnels used by the Palestinian Resistance, disparity in water accessibility, the use of sewage systems to forcibly displace communities, the vertical apartheid within Jerusalem and across the Occupied Territories, and the airborne occupation enforced after the evacuation of Gaza. The 'politics of verticality' can be viewed as mapping value onto vertical space, and the consistent depiction of Israel and its citizens as physically higher is a metaphor for their alleged superiority over Palestinians. In Jerusalem, Jewish residents live on the higher floors of houses, accessible by secure rooftop pathways, whilst Palestinians live below on the streets;[^5] similarly, the majority of Israeli settlements in the Occupied Territories are built on hilltops, ensuring that, in the words of Ariel Sharon, "Arabs... see Jewish lights every night from 500 metres".[^6] We can also see verticality at a subterranean level: one of the most regularly repeated Zionist propaganda points regarding the Jewish right to Palestine is that the land was promised to them by divine mandate in Genesis 12:1 - Israel therefore pursues archaeological digs with a fervour, believing that the remains of Jewish settlements from millennia beforehand legitimises their claim to the Occupied Territories.[^7] Whether this claim is legitimate or not, such a metaphor is important for the Israeli regime in maintaining their sense of superiority over Palestinians and thereby justifying their illegal occupation. Not only is verticality a useful metaphor for Israel, but also serves a more physical purpose. The Israeli government builds roads above Palestinian territory, to reduce the chances of Palestinian 'interference' in the day to day lives of Israelis, whilst insisting that any Palestinian crossing between the Gaza strip and the West Bank be subterranean.[^8] Weizman's concept of the 'politics of verticality' is a new foray into the Israeli occupation, moving beyond the two-dimensional 'borders' of previous Israeli annexations which has characterised much of the discourse surrounding the occupation.

Not only does Weizman introduce 'politics of verticality' to the academic arena, he also makes the argument that Israeli architecture has been politicised as a discipline. He argues that, whilst many of his Israeli colleagues might not think of their job as a political one, it is in fact deeply entrenched in the politics of the occupation. The architect designing an Israeli settlement often abides by a manual written by the Israeli government in 1984, entitled '*Building and Development in the Mountain Regions*' - this set of guidelines encourages that bedrooms should be built facing inwards towards the settlement to foster a sense of community, and that living rooms should face toward the landscape below.[^9] This may not seem political or imperialist to the architect, but this design serves a sinister purpose: Israeli settlers are able to observe and inform on Palestinian 'insurgents' in the territory surrounding the settlement. Architecture is used by the Israeli government to turn regular civilians into the eyes and ears of the state, and it therefore becomes inherently political. The same politicisation can be observed in the construction the road bridges over the Occupied Territories, and in such small details as the red roofs of Israeli settlements, which whilst seemingly harmless, conveniently allow Israeli airborne attackers to identify 'friendly' settlements as opposed to their Palestinian targets.[^10] Perhaps the best example of 'inadvertent' use of architecture in the politics of the occupation is 'Jerusalem Stone' in building and cladding new properties in Jerusalem. The stone carries imagery of Jerusalem as a 'holy city', with local law mandating its use on all new buildings, which, as a result, extends the city's 'holiness' and sense of heritage that is so important to the Israeli regime to newly built properties.[^11] Maintaining this image 'legitimises' Israel's claim to the land. Even those who attempt to utilise architecture to alleviate the suffering of the Palestinian people are dragged into politics: the widening of roads by UNRWA in the Jenin refugee camp, for example, sought to prevent further destruction carried out by the IOF, but ended up allowing Israeli tanks to drive the streets with ease, whilst also reducing the amount of hiding places and alleyways that were being used by Palestinian resistance fighters.[^12] Weizman presents a truth that is unsettling and uncomfortable for those Israeli architects who maintain that their profession is not political.

In *Hollow Land*, Eyal Weizman presents a highly convincing argument - the Israeli occupation wields architecture as its primary tool in maintaining an apartheid regime, an architecture that is uniquely three-dimensional. Not only this, but it politicises architecture to the point where one is inextricably linked to the other. Weizman's arguments succeed categorically, a claim which is best evidenced by the fact that the Israeli Association of Architects prevented the project on which *Hollow Land* is based from being shown at the 2002 UIA Berlin Architectural Congress, destroying all 5000 copies of the research - this act can be taken as an admission of guilt on the part of the Israeli architects who are so instrumental in maintaining Israel's occupation.[^13] When one also considers the provenance of Weizman's sources, which can be considered to be highly accurate as mentioned previously, his arguments are further strengthened. As a result of Weizman's arguments, *Hollow Land* is considered to be a seminal text in discourse on the Israeli occupation of Palestine.

*Hollow Land* is Eyal Weizman's investigation into the Israeli regime of occupation, apartheid, and imperial expansion, with a specific focus on Weizman's speciality of architecture and urban planning. The book details how the Israeli regime has utilised architecture to maintain and expand their occupation of Palestine (henceforth referred to as the Occupied Territories), as well as their domination of the Palestinian people. Wiezman elaborates on the five architectural components which are the foundational tenets of the occupation: the contested capital of Jerusalem, Israeli settlements, 'border' fortifications, checkpoints between Israel and the Gaza strip/West Bank, the Wall, the evacuation of Gaza, and finally, targeted assassinations.

It is important to first note the temporal context of the book. The content within was published in 2007, in the immediate aftermath of the second Intifada. Therefore, it does not take into account the Gaza War of 2008-9, nor of 2014, and it cannot account for the genocide undertaken by the Israeli government since October 7th 2023, during which much of the architecture which Weizman details has not been in use or has been abandoned completely. As such, some of the information within the book has been rendered inaccurate in the present day. However, this by no means limits the book as a historical source nor as a medium for the discussion of imperialism.

One of Weizman's main focuses is on his concept of a 'politics/occupation of verticality', a theme which he introduces at the beginning of the book and continues to revisit throughout. 'Politics of verticality' is the term that Weizman uses to describe how the Israeli government and the IOF (Israeli Occupation Force) are able to maintain their occupation both by utilising the topography of the landscape and also through man-made Israeli architecture. Weizman gives a great many striking examples of this. One of the primary examples that he covers over multiple chapters is the expansion of Israeli settlements into Palestinian territory. Often, these settlements are built on hilltops above Palestinian territory. This not only ensures that the settlement is defensible to an attack from Palestinian resistance movements, but also serves a purpose for the IOF: the architecture of the settlements and the urban planning by which they are designed allow the IOF to use regular Israeli citizens as eyes of the state. Living rooms intentionally face towards the landscape below, so that settlers can then inform on any 'suspicious activity' by Palestinian 'terrorists' ('suspicious' activity can be as innocuous as looking at a settlement with a pair of binoculars - such a 'crime' allows the IOF to legally shoot and kil the accused on sight. As well as this the use of sewage and hygiene demonises the Palestinian population as 'dirty' and therefore 'beneath' the Israeli public, and this has been well-documented. Often, raw sewage will be allowed to drain down from Israeli hilltop settlements into Palestinian villages, thereby flooding them - there is also evidence that the Israeli regime intentionally damages overground sewage pipes to forcibly evict communities by making their land unliveable. In the eyes of the Israeli public, this creates an image of the Palestinian as 'a ‘defiled’ substance within the Israeli landscape'. Weizman goes on to discuss the disparity between Israelis and Palestinians in their access to water. Subterranean aquifers are a major source of water for the Occupied Territories, and pumps must therefore be set up to extract this water. Israeli pumps have no limit on how deep they are allowed to penetrate; Palestinian pumps, by contrast, have strict 'legal' limits on their ability to pump water from the ground. As a result, the vast majority of water is allocated to the Israeli state, rather than those in the Occupied Territories, who arguably are in more desperate need of it. Weizman also regularly references bridges and tunnels as indicators of power and control. For example, during the Camp David negotiations in 2000, when discussing access to the West Bank from Gaza and vice-versa, the Palestinian delegation favoured bridges, whereas the Israeli delegation strongly favoured the use of tunnels. Weizman argues that this is the result of the Israeli desire for the Palestinian people to remain as hidden from view as possible from Israelis, preferring for them to operate in the shadows as if they were never occupied in the first place. The Palestinian desire, as is to be expected, is the opposite. Not only this, but when building roads over Palestinian land to connect Israeli cities, the Israeli government has historically had a strong preference for using bridges. This maintains their vertical 'superiority' over the Occupied Territories, and allegedly prevents Palestinian interference in key Israeli infrastructure. The 'politics of verticality' can even be seen in occupied Jerusalem: Israeli properties are often accessed by paths that travel over the roofs of houses, which are subject to security checkpoints and identity checks, whilst Palestinians live in the tier below, both literally and figuratively. The politics/occupation of verticality is Weizman's key demonstration of how Israel maintains its control of the Occupied Territories.

*Hollow Land* is a text with a marked difference to many other books on the Israel/Palestine conflict in that it focuses primarily on architecture and planning over the brutality of the IOF and the events of the first and second Intifadas which other books tend to maintain a focus on. There is a place for these events in Weizman's book, and atrocities are regularly highlighted - however, it is the design, bureaucracy, legal systems, and physical properties of the Israeli apartheid state that Weizman prefers to focus on. Whilst IOF raids and targeted assassinations are in themselves a tool of occupation (and horrifying ones at that), Weizman always ties back the violence experienced in the Occupied Territories to Israel's methodology in constructing its settlements and expanding past its 'borders'. For example, urban warfare is discussed through an architectural lens, highlighting the IOF tactic of 'walking through walls' and the planning of refugee camps inadvertently facilitating Israeli military intervention; the attempt at maintaining Jerusalem's strict ethnicity ratios are, according to Weizman, an architectural endeavour in forced overcrowding; architecture even plays a large factor in Israeli internal and external propaganda both in characterising Jerusalem as a 'holy city' by using 'Jerusalem stone' to build and clad new buildings and in maintaining the image of Israeli superiority through topographical means - 'higher' and 'lower' not only used as an indicator of elevation, but also of ethnic status. Weizman emphasises that imperialism cannot be achieved merely by pure violence towards a subjugated population (although in the present day, the state of Israel seems to have abandoned that idea); an imperial power must, at its heart, be a complex beast comprised of many different mediums through which to enforce an occupation. Israel's method of choice is their architecture.

What makes Weizman's book stand out even further is his unique position in both Israeli society and the Palestinian liberation movement. As an Israeli, Weizman was able to access Israeli sources who were, more often than not, complicit in the occupation. Israeli politicians and military generals (often one and the same) were forthcoming in their methodology and strategy, and as such *Hollow Land* is written with information that many other authors are not able to ascertain (Palestinian authors, for example, would never be granted an audience with such powerful Israeli figures).

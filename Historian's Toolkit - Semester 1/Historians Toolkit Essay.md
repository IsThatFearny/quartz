### # Do we distort LGBTQ+ history by treating the Stonewall Riots as a moment of radical change?

The Stonewall Riots of 1969 are widely agreed upon by leading historians to be a 'moment of radical change' in the trajectory of the LGBTQ+ rights movement.[^1] However, there is debate over whether, by treating the event as such, we 'distort' the history of what came before. This essay shall argue that treating Stonewall as a moment of radical change, does not necessarily distort pre-stonewall LGBTQ+ history, but we must avoid defining Stonewall[^2] as the 'beginning' of the LGBTQ+ rights movement as is the tendency of some of the literature.

There is clear evidence of Stonewall's transformative impact upon the LGBTQ+ rights movement. On the whole, the movement saw a major shift from the homophile movement's approach of legal challenges and peaceful demonstrations with an emphasis on single-issue politics, to the gay liberation movement's tactics of direct action (largely adopted from various other civil rights movements of the 1960s) and the politicisation of sexuality and 'coming out'.[^3] Being gay became a highly politically charged identity for many, and the movement began to become involved with various other civil rights movements, such as the Black Panthers (with Lesbians becoming involved in the feminist movement).[^4] This change in the methodology of gay activism was marked by an explosion in the number of organisations post-Stonewall: just before Stonewall occurred, there were around fifty LGBTQ+ rights organisations in the US; By 1973, this had grown to over eight-hundred.[^5] Various victories were won, such as out gays running for office and in some cases even winning, as well as the representation (although still relatively rare) of gay characters in media.[^6] Despite great hostility still existing throughout the nation, the raised awareness of same-sex relationships led to a humanisation of the queer community, as evidenced by a shift in language: before Stonewall, the conception of a 'homosexual' had largely been based on sexual activity rather than romantic relationships, whereas after Stonewall there was much more focus on the homosexual as a romantic rather than a purely sexual being; likewise, the use of 'normal' in direct opposition to 'homosexual' fell out of use post-Stonewall, deferring to the use of 'gay' and 'straight' and going some way to eliminate the inherent stigma in pre-Stonewall language.[^7] The pre-Stonewall homophile movement could claim no such success in raising awareness or in bringing about legal change - for all their talk of integration and acceptance, from World War II to 1969 very little *de jure* or *de facto* progress had been made.[^8] 

One can see how some historians see Stonewall and its offshoots (such as the Gay Liberation Front, henceforth GLF) as the beginning of the 'real' LGBTQ+ rights movement.[^9] The riot itself *was* considered to be the first gay riot of such intensity in history.[^10] On the surface, the post-Stonewall movement seems to have been significantly more successful. However, this interpretation is a mistake. It was, of course, convenient for those involved in post-Stonewall activism to see it this way - indeed, as Stein says, "What better way for post-Stonewall activists to represent themselves as having broken with the past than to deny that lesbian and gay politics even had a past?".[^11] It is *this* interpretation that, intentionally or not, distorts LGBTQ+ history. For example, it erases the contribution made by those of the homophile movement who were 'out' before Stonewall, and Ada Bello (founder of the Philadelphia chapter of the Daughters of Bilitis, a major Lesbian homophile movement), who herself viewed Stonewall as a "watershed", pointed out that "The new militancy and radical approaches are the logical result of long years of laborious battles".[^12] The homophile movement was not entirely ineffective as it is often claimed - it simply existed in a socially conservative space in which it was necessary to constantly be on the defensive.[^13] The achievements of the homophile movement were far from their own goals, as they did envisage a radical movement such as the post-Stonewall movement; their aims were far less conservative than their achievements.[^14] Thus, its success cannot be measured in comparison to the relatively liberal atmosphere of the early 1970s, as it came in a completely different form. The homophile movement succeeded in bringing the queer community together in a way that would not have otherwise been possible. Isolation was one of the most difficult things about being gay, and homophile publications were vital for many in maintaining a connection with an identity which otherwise necessitated repression.[^15] The view that Stonewall was the beginning of the LGBTQ+ rights movement does indeed distort pre-Stonewall LGBTQ+ history, banalising and often erasing the role and success of the homophile movement.

The alternative view, which is prevalent in the majority of modern scholarship, is that Stonewall marked a radical change from the previous methodology of the homophile movement, whilst still acknowledging the work of pre-Stonewall activists. The reconciliation between the two involves no contradiction. Although organisations such as the GLF defined themselves in opposition to the 'old guard' of the homophile movement, the community that it provided formed the basis for the networks that the GLF and other groups such as the Radicalesbians would utilise in growing their membership base.[^16] Even prominent contemporary activists (such as Ada Bello above, and her contemporaries) broke with the trend of denying the homophile movement's involvement in pre-Stonewall activism. They closely associated Stonewall and the foundations upon which it was built. There is also a much greater focus on the part that Lesbians played in the homophile movement as a result of this acknowledgement of the importance of the movement: there is evidence of gay men defining themselves in terms of their relationships with Lesbians, and the Lesbian identification with the plight of the women's rights movement allowed them to mobilise tens of thousands of Lesbians across the US.[^17] This perspective as a whole emphasises that Stonewall did indeed catalyse a new dawn for the the LGBTQ+ rights movement, but maintains that it could not have occurred without the pre-Stonewall homophile movement having first built the foundations and created the conditions in which such an explosion of discontent could take place and in which the GLF and similar organisations could be founded and bring about change.

This essay has presented two opposing views that both current and previous scholarship have both taken. The former treats Stonewall as the beginning of the LGBTQ+ rights movement, and often erases the contribution that pre-Stonewall activists made in laying the groundwork for post-Stonewall organisations, especially in the case of Lesbian organisations such as the Daughters of Bilitis. The latter perspective, meanwhile, is able to reconcile 'pre-Stonewall' and 'post-Stonewall', demonstrating that rightfully defining Stonewall as a moment of 'radical change' does not necessarily entail the distortion of LGBTQ+ history, as contemporary activists may have wished to do. One may thus conclude that we do not distort LGBTQ+ history by treating Stonewall as a moment of radical change - after all, there is clear evidence of it as such - as long as we acknowledge that the pre-Stonewall homophile movement was essential in creating the conditions required for the progress that the post-Stonewall movement made.

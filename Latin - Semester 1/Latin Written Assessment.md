2  ipsorum lingua Celtae, nostra Galli appellantur. Hi omnes lingua, institutis, legibus inter se differunt.  
3  Gallos ab Aquitanis Garumna flumen, a Belgis Matrona et Sequana dividit. Horum omnium fortissimi

The three given translations (AI-Generated, Edwards, and Hanford) each interpret the original Latin in their own different ways. Whilst often the 'gist' of the text remains, each has strengths and weaknesses in terms of vocabulary choice, fluency in English, interpretation of word order, etc. This essay shall identify these differences and and provide comparison between each text.

The primary difference between these translation is usually choice of vocabulary. There are some stark contrasts which make a great difference to how the text is interpreted. For example, the AI translates '*fortissimi*' (line 3) as 'strongest', whereas Edwards and Hanford translate it as 'bravest' or 'most courageous'. 'Strongest' seems to imply military might, especially when put in the context of the Belgae as adversaries. However, 'bravest' and 'most courageous' do not necessarily entail power or strength; the Belgae could be very brave or courageous without much military might to back it up. When put into context by the next sentence, it would seem that the latter two translations are more accurate. In said sentence there is also a glaring contrast between translations. The AI translates '*ad effeminandos animos pertinent important*' as 'to soften their warlike spirit'; Edwards translates it as 'that make for effeminacy'; and Hanford translates it as 'enervating luxuries for sale'. It is immediately clear that Hanford has taken some liberties with this translation - a Latin equivalent of his translation cannot be found in the original text - but there is something to be said for the difference between the other two translations. Whilst the gist remains the same, 'effeminacy' could be interpreted to include '*animos*' (perhaps an 'effeminate spirit'?). The AI chooses English vocabulary that is not present in the latin, such as 'soften' and 'warlike', which, again, have the same gist but are perhaps not as accurate to the original. A third major difference in vocabulary choice can be seen throughout the text in Hanford's use of the word 'Celts' as opposed to the AI and Edwards' use of 'Gauls'. The literal translation of the various forms of '*Gallus*' is, of course, 'Gauls' or 'Gallic'. Hanford, however, chooses to use the word 'Celts', which was the endonym for this people. It seems unlikely that Caesar would refer to them as 'Celts' throughout, considering that he had previously stated that the Romans called them 'Gauls'. Hanford's choice is understandable, but one would argue that 'Gauls' is the better choice in this circumstance.

There are also various instances of variations in the translation of certain cases. For example, when translating '*humanitate provinciae longissime absunt*', in which '*provinciae*' is in the Genitive, Edwards and Hanford translate it correctly ('*of* the province') whereas the AI translation implies no such possession. In a similar fashion, Edwards seems to ignore the ablative of separation in '*cum aut suis finibus...*', translating it as 'keep them out of' rather than 'keep them from'. Whilst both are correct, the more literal translation here is the latter.

Occasional differences in translating the passive vs the active are also prevalent throughout the translations. For example, Edwards and Hanford translate '*incolunt*' as being in the passive voice ('is inhabited *by* the Belgae'), whereas it is in fact in the active voice. If it were in the passive, then '*incolunt*' would become '*incoluntur*'. Likewise, none translate '*appelantur*' as in the passive voice. This may, however, be a result of the passive feeling rather clunky in English: the sentence would translate to 'they are being called Gauls' which is not proper English. It may therefore be acceptable to translate it as such. There is a third occasion on which the passive is not translated correctly: '*oriuntur*' is in the passive ('they are being arisen') and only the AI translation includes any form of the word, and it is in the active voice.

Throughout each translation various omissions are also present, whether by negligence or for convenience. For example, Hanford omits '*Horum*' (i.e. 'these') and instead replaces it with 'three' - this may help with clarity when reading the English as it reminds the reader that there are three peoples. Likewise, the AI translation omits '*obtinere*', although this does not cause the sentence to lose meaning. There are, however, cases in which the omission of certain Latin words either prevent the sentence from making sense in English or change the meaning of the sentence. A simple example of this is the omission of '*que*' at the end of '*minimeque*'. The lack of 'and' in Hanford's translation means that there is no coordinating conjunction between clauses, and therefore no connection between their 'courage' and the lack of merchant activity, as Caesar wishes to imply.

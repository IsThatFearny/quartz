The three given translations (AI-Generated, Edwards, and Hanford) each interpret the original Latin in their own different ways. Whilst often the 'gist' of the text remains, each has strengths and weaknesses in terms of vocabulary choice, fluency in English, interpretation of word order, etc. This essay shall identify these differences and and provide comparison between each text.

The primary difference between these translation is usually choice of vocabulary. There are some stark contrasts which make a great difference to how the text is interpreted. For example, the AI translates '*fortissimi*' (line 3) as 'strongest', whereas Edwards and Hanford translate it as 'bravest' or 'most courageous'. 'Strongest' seems to imply military might, especially when put in the context of the Belgae as adversaries. However, 'bravest' and 'most courageous' do not necessarily entail power or strength; the Belgae could be very brave or courageous without much military might to back it up. When put into context by the next sentence, it would seem that the latter two translations are more accurate. In said sentence there is also a glaring contrast between translations. The AI translates '*ad effeminandos animos pertinent important*' (line 5) as 'to soften their warlike spirit'; Edwards translates it as 'that make for effeminacy'; and Hanford translates it as 'enervating luxuries for sale'. It is immediately clear that Hanford has taken some liberties with this translation - a Latin equivalent of his translation cannot be found in the original text - but there is something to be said for the difference between the other two translations. Whilst the gist remains the same, 'effeminacy' could be interpreted to include '*animos*' (perhaps an 'effeminate spirit'?). The AI chooses English vocabulary that is not present in the latin, such as 'soften' and 'warlike', which, again, have the same gist but are perhaps not as accurate to the original. A third major difference in vocabulary choice can be seen throughout the text in Hanford's use of the word 'Celts' as opposed to the AI and Edwards' use of 'Gauls'. The literal translation of the various forms of '*Gallus*' is, of course, 'Gauls' or 'Gallic'. Hanford, however, chooses to use the word 'Celts', which was the endonym for this people. It seems unlikely that Caesar would refer to them as 'Celts' throughout, considering that he had previously stated that the Romans called them 'Gauls'. Hanford's choice is understandable, but one would argue that 'Gauls' is the better choice in this circumstance.

There are also various instances of variations in the translation of certain cases. For example, when translating '*humanitate provinciae longissime absunt*' (line 4), in which '*provinciae*' is in the Genitive, Edwards and Hanford translate it correctly ('*of* the province') whereas the AI translation implies no such possession. In a similar fashion, Edwards seems to ignore the ablative of separation in '*cum aut suis finibus...*' (line 8), translating it as 'keep them out of' rather than 'keep them from'. Whilst both are correct, the more literal translation here is the latter.

Occasional differences in translating the passive vs the active are also prevalent throughout the translations. For example, Edwards and Hanford translate '*incolunt*' (line 1) as being in the passive voice ('is inhabited *by* the Belgae'), whereas it is in fact in the active voice. If it were in the passive, then '*incolunt*' would become '*incoluntur*'. Likewise, none translate '*appellantur*' (line 2) as in the passive voice. This may, however, be a result of the passive feeling rather clunky in English: the sentence would translate to 'they are being called Gauls' which is not proper English. It may therefore be acceptable to translate it as such. There is a third occasion on which the passive is not translated correctly: '*oriuntur*' (line 11) is in the passive ('they are being arisen') and only the AI translation includes any form of the word, and it is in the active voice - this use of active voice may, however, be correct for the sake of clarity.

Throughout each translation various omissions are also present, whether by negligence or for convenience. For example, Hanford omits '*Horum*' (line 3) (i.e. 'these') and instead replaces it with 'three' - this may help with clarity when reading the English as it reminds the reader that there are three peoples. Likewise, the AI translation omits '*obtinere*' (line 9), although this does not cause the sentence to lose meaning. There are, however, cases in which the omission of certain Latin words either prevent the sentence from making sense in English or change the meaning of the sentence. A simple example of this is the omission of '*que*' at the end of '*minimeque*' (line 4). The lack of 'and' in Hanford's translation means that there is no coordinating conjunction between clauses, and therefore no connection between their 'courage' and the lack of merchant activity, as it seems Caesar wishes to imply.

Throughout each translation, one can identify recurring stylistic choices that the translator has made. The AI-generated translation tends to translate very literally (and generally does not use synonyms) and avoiding including words that are not present in the original Latin. For example, the AI correctly identifies '*gerunt*' (line 6) as 'they wage [war]' - the other two translations do not, but substitute it for 'they *are at* [war]'; similarly, the AI translates '*trans*' (line 6) as 'across' instead of 'beyond' (as Edwards does). Where Edwards and Hanford add 'people' to the sentence, deviating from the original, the AI does not; likewise, the AI tends to maintain the punctuation in the original. These tendencies do not necessarily mean that the AI translation is any better than those of Edwards and Hanford however: there are multiple occasions on which its literal translation style does not do justice to the original text. For example, in lines 9-11 ('*Eorum uni... septentriones*') feels to the reader significantly more clunky than the translations of Edwards and Hanford. Another example is the AI's translation of lines 13-14 ('*spectat... septentriones*'), which, whilst accurate and stylistically true to Caesar's original, may not at first glance be the most clear to a reader.

Edwards takes some more liberties in translating, especially with his choice of vocabulary, although often a sacrifice of literality is made up for in stylistic variation. These characterise his translation. For example, although both translations of '*lingua*' (line 2) are correct, his choice of 'tongue' instead of 'language' is perhaps a more elegant choice of wording. Likewise, Edwards' description of the ocean being 'by' Spain as opposed to 'facing' is certainly a lot more clear in terms of placing the region geographically. A third example can be seen in Edwards' translation of '*praecedunt*' (line 7): he translates it as 'they excel', rather than 'surpass' - either is acceptable of course, but Edwards' choice seems to have been made in favour of more 'flowery' language.

Hanford's translation is largely characterised by his choice to add context for the reader in various places, especially when it comes to geography. Examples of this can be seen in Hanford's replacement of 'ocean' with 'Atlantic'; 'Roman province' rather than just 'province'; and the use of 'Switzerland' and 'Germany'. Whilst on occasion this added context may be helpful, in certain cases such as the latter, one could argue that the use of modern day states to describe ancient territories is problematic in that a modern reader does not get an accurate picture of the territory in which the event is taking place, imagining the territory as confined by modern day borders - of course, this was not the case. This is not to say that there is no place for added context when translating, but that one should be careful not to create more confusion when attempting to reduce it - Hanford does not succeed in this. He also makes various mistakes throughout and introduces unnecessary words into the translation that are not present in the latin.

The three translations of the passage each have their own distinctive characteristics, flaws and advantages. Overall, the AI translation is the most literal with the least stylistic flair; Edwards translates with a view to provide a translation that, whilst occasionally departing from literality, makes important vocabulary choices that enhance the text's flow and ease of understanding; and the use of added context in Hanford's translation, whilst often successful, can prove perilous to the meaning of a sentence. The fact that the AI is too literal, and Hanford makes various mistakes throughout, means that if one had to conclude the 'best' translation in terms of a combination of accuracy and finesse, Edwards seems to combine the two in the best manner. However, the AI translation seems to overall have an edge over Hanford' published translation in accuracy, style, and clarity.

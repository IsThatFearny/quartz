Whilst often the 'gist' of the text remains, each has strengths and weaknesses in terms of vocabulary choice, fluency in English, interpretation of word order, etc. This essay shall identify these differences and and provide comparison between each text.

The thing that differs most between these translation is usually choice of vocabulary. There are some stark contrasts which make a great difference to how the text is interpreted. For example, the AI translates '*fortissimi*' as 'strongest', whereas Edwards and Hanford translate it as 'bravest' or 'most courageous'. 'Strongest' seems to imply military might, especially when put in the context of the Belgae as adversaries. However, 'bravest' and 'most courageous' do not necessarily entail power or strength; the Belgae could be very brave or courageous without much military might to back it up. When put into context by the next sentence, it would seem that the latter two translations are more accurate. In said sentence there is also a glaring contrast between translations. The AI translates '*ad effeminandos animos pertinent important*' as 'to soften their warlike spirit'; Edwards translates it as 'that make for effeminacy'; and Hanford translates it as 'enervating luxuries for sale'. It is immediately clear that Hanford has taken some liberties with this translation - a Latin equivalent of his translation cannot be found in the original text - but there is something to be said for the difference between the other two translations. Whilst the gist remains the same, 'effeminacy' could be interpreted to include '*animos*' (perhaps an 'effeminate spirit'?). The AI chooses English vocabulary that is not present in the latin, such as 'soften' and 'warlike', which, again, have the same gist but are perhaps not as accurate to the original. A third major difference in vocabulary choice can be seen throughout the text in Hanford's use of the word 'Celts' as opposed to the AI and Edwards' use of 'Gauls'. The literal translation of the various forms of '*Gallus*' is, of course, 'Gauls' or 'Gallic'. Hanford, however, chooses to use the word 'Celts', which was the endonym for this people. It seems unlikely that Caesar would refer to them as 'Celts' throughout, considering that he had previously stated that the Romans called them 'Gauls'. Hanford's choice is understandable, but one would argue that 'Gauls' is the better choice in this circumstance.

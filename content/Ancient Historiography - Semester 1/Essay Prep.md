## Hebrew and Greek Sources in the Speeches of Josephus' "Jewish War"

"The concept that God is on the side of the Romans probably originated in a pragmatic application of Jewish historical interpretation - what could be called divine determinism - to the contemporary situation. The speech of Josephus in Bk. V clearly illustrates this: those whom the Jews were able to defeat throughout the whole course of their history were believed to have been defeated by God. Therefore, those who have defeated the Jews obviously did so with at least the permission, if not the active help of God. Josephus' projection of the idea that God is actively on the side of the Roman seems, moreover, to be connected to his interpretation of the impious action of the 'rebels' which require a serious punishment" p. 329

"The panegyric on the Roman Empire in Bk. II. 362-364 not only contains the assertion that the Romans are the greatest power on earth but because of the Hebrew literary device employed, it seems to contain a teleological idea reminiscent of the popular Roman concept of the order of the Roman Empire being parallel to the orderly universe. In other words, it appears that the writer has given this concept a Jewish form." p. 331

"The second part of the concept of *philanthropia* is applied to Josephus to the Romans, specifically as a concern on their part for the preservation of the laws and customs of the Jews and the of the center of their religion, the Temple. In this way he is able to align it to the rabbinic idea that the Jews should submit to a ruling power as long as it does not demand that they should disobey the law. ## The Empire Writes Back: Josephan Rhetoric in Flavian Rome

“we may watch an accomplished writer handle the complexities of unequal power-relations, in which an elite foreigner in Rome carefully shapes his discourse in order to win maximal advantage for himself and for his people, within the constraints of his social and political environment” (Barclay, 2005, p. 315)

“a subtly Romanized piece of argumentation, which transposes Jewish thematics into a specifically Roman key” (Barclay, 2005, p. 316)

“Josephus' rhetoric is moulded to appeal to Roman standards of honour, and his discourse is variously confined or developed according to the cultural presumptions of his environment.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 316)

“Josephus dares to present his own version of the War, and then, at great length, his own account of Jewish history and the place of Jews within the world-history ofthe Hellenistic and early Roman periods.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 319)

“changes the character of Judaism but also contributes to the ever-changing discussion of what it means to be 'Roman'” (Barclay, 2005, p. 320)

“His position in Rome, and his desire to reach and persuade a Roman or Romanized elite make it impossible for him to voice overt criticism of Roman policy to the Jews.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 320)

“Given such constraints, we should expect Josephus' most effective advocacy for the Jews to emerge not in confrontation with Roman cultural values, but in the ways he turns and shapes those values to his own interests.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 320)

“It was thus equally possible for a non-Roman like Josephus to turn that complex Roman tradition to the interests of his own cultural tradition.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 321)

“In Against Apion we can see Josephus working on Roman terms, but manipulating Roman values and norms to include, as exemplars, the Jews themselves—appropriating Roman cultural symbols, sometimes subtly redefined, for the greater glory of Judaism” (Barclay, 2005, p. 321)

“Although his use of the Roman tradition in some respects consolidates the legitimacy of Roman discourse, it also empowers him to find a persuasive medium in which to re-express Judaism.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 321)

By insisting on the extreme antiquity of Judaism and the originality of Moses' constitution (which has been imitated and envied by all other peoples), and by inserting under Roman moral categories his own Jewish customs (e.g. the Jewish ban on abortion, Ap. 2. 202), Josephus, as it were, infiltrates Roman discourse with his own distinctively Jewish traditions.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 321)

“In this light, we might be open to consider whether Josephus uses ambiguous or veiled statements which could suggest to some readers a counter-current to his own public deference towards the Romans.” (Barclay, 2005, p. 321)

## Josephus's Jewish War

“Josephus’s extant oeuvre consists of four works:2 (1) the Jewish War ( Bellum judaicum), a seven-volume narrative of the background, events, and aftermath of the First Roman-Jewish War (66–73 CE), for which Josephus is our primary extant source;3 (2) the Jewish Antiquities (Antiquitates judaicae), a massive, twentyvolume account of Jewish history prior to the war whose first eleven books offer a detailed recapitulation of the contents of the Hebrew Scriptures; (3) the Life, an autobiographical supplement to the Antiquities that primarily serves as an apologia for Josephus’s conduct during the early stages of the war with Rome;” (Duncan, 2023, p. 728)

“and (4) Against Apion, a polemical and apologetic treatise in which Josephus defends the venerable character of the Jews’ history and the Mosaic “constitution” against Greek critics who had purportedly responded to the publication of his Antiquities by disparaging the Jews.” (Duncan, 2023, p. 729)

“we possess considerably more information concerning Josephus’s personal history than is typical in the case of other ancient historians” (Duncan, 2023, p. 729)

“In the mid-60s he undertook a diplomatic errand to Rome, returning to Jerusalem shortly before the outbreak of the Jewish revolt against Roman rule, which he sought in vain to forestall” (Duncan, 2023, p. 729)

“self-portrait of Josephus as one who—in common with much of the Jerusalem aristocracy—opposed war with Rome, yet quickly found himself compelled to align himself with the revolutionary cause in order to preserve his safety, while nonetheless hoping that a full-scale military confrontation might ultimately be averted” (Duncan, 2023, p. 730)

“avoiding any further unnecessary provocation of the Romans, while simultaneously preparing the region’s cities and inhabitants to withstand a Roman invasion should war prove inevitable” (Duncan, 2023, p. 730)

“Following the conclusion of the war, Josephus took up residence at Rome, where he embarked upon the task of authoring his history of the conflict” (Duncan, 2023, p. 730)

“Vespasian bestowed Roman citizenship upon him (Vita 423), and he continued thereafter to enjoy the favor of the Flavian emperors” (Duncan, 2023, p. 730)

“According to his own testimony, then, Josephus occupied a profoundly ambiguous, liminal space between the Jewish and Roman worlds, serving successively as a native opponent to the Jewish revolt against Rome, a general of Jewish forces at war with Rome, a prisoner of the Romans, a trusted Roman liaison to the recalcitrant Jewish revolutionaries, and an author penning accounts of the history and laws of the Jews under Roman patronage.” (Duncan, 2023, p. 731)

“a distinctive blend of Jewish piety and Greco-Roman rhetorical and historiographical conventions,” (Duncan, 2023, p. 731)

“in accordance with his aim of producing a work that is “written for lovers of the truth [τὴν ἀλήθειαν] and not to gratify my readers”” (Duncan, 2023, p. 736)

## Josephus in the Diaspora

“We observe, from his earliest work, a sense of the unity of the Jewish ethnos, which for him comprised both the Jewish people in their own land and those everywhere else. ## Josephus as a Roman Historian

“The mere fact that he was personally known to the imperial father and son would have meant that he had a better social position than the vast majority of Romans.” (Mason, 2016, p. 89)

“Much could be said about the substance of Josephus’s Judean War as a work written first for certain circles in Rome: about the emphases of the prologue’s prospectus (1.17–30) in contrast to what the later narrative actually includes, the contrast between assumed knowledge of Roman realia and the need to explain Judean affairs, and the programmatic comparison between Roman and Judean civil war with their respective tyrants (1.4, esp. 585–663; 7.157).” (Mason, 2016, p. 97)

“Another feature of Josephus’s historiographical perspective points to an accommodation with his Roman context: his treatment of “the Greeks.”” (Mason, 2016, p. 101)

“hen we consider that Josephus was writing in Rome, and that his attacks on Greek pretension highlight their love of rhetoric at the expense of truth, as well as their ignorance of non‐sea faring nations (such as Judea and Rome), and that these happened to be familiar sore points in Roman‐Greek cultural relations (e.g., Dionysius, Ant. rom. 9.5.1; Cicero, De orat. 1.102; Pliny, NH pr. 24–28; 3.2; Wardman 2002, 110–134), it is hard to avoid the conclusion that Josephus bonds his laconic Judean compatriots with the citizens of his adopted home city, over against the prevailing Greek culture (Apion 1.60–66 with Barclay 2007 ad loc.).” (Mason, 2016, p. 101)

“We have no reason to think that he was an advisor or close friend of the ruling family” (Mason, 2016, p. 103)

“we find coherent indications that he produced his work in the normal sort of way. Josephus envisaged and had a local public in Rome, and he wrote for them in the first instance. He expected such audiences not only to get past, but to savour, Judean War’s opening 264‐word period (1.1–6). He wrote for those interested in war, and more generally in polis leadership, civil strife, tragedy, cultic pollution and purification, political constitutions, ethnography, laws, and moral philosophy. Although we know little about his first audiences, we may be confident that he had a public in Flavian Rome.” (Mason, 2016, p. 103)

“When we seek to understand Josephus’s work, to use it for Judean history, it matters that he wrote neither some kind of neutral facts in a vacuum nor in free suspension above the Mediterranean. He chose his material and language first of all as a communicative bridge between his Judean values and those of his Greek‐educated public in Flavian Rome.” (Mason, 2016, p. 103)

## **Part I: Tacitus’ Account of the Trial of Piso (Ann. 3.10–19)**

### **1. What picture does Tacitus give us of the downfall of Piso?**

Tacitus presents Piso’s downfall as **tragic, politically charged, and morally ambiguous**.  
He portrays Piso as:

- **Initially confident**, relying on Tiberius’ supposed protection.
    
- **Gradually isolated**, abandoned by allies (even his wife Plancina) and betrayed by political expediency.
    
- **Broken by despair**, committing suicide (though Tacitus leaves open the possibility of murder).
    

Tacitus’ tone is **tragic and ironic**: Piso is neither innocent nor purely villainous — he is destroyed by the system he served. His fall exposes the **hypocrisy and corruption of imperial justice**, where appearances of legality mask political manipulation.

> “Tiberius, pitiless and angerless, barred and bolted against the ingress of any human emotion” (3.15)  
> → Tacitus’ moral portrait of tyranny disguised as restraint.

**Key themes:**

- _Downfall through isolation_ — Piso loses every support (Plancina, Sejanus, even Tiberius’ sympathy).
    
- _Ambiguity of guilt_ — Tacitus refuses to say clearly whether Piso poisoned Germanicus.
    
- _Political theatre_ — The trial becomes a public spectacle of loyalty to the emperor, not a search for truth.
    
- _Suicide as moral commentary_ — Common in Tacitus, suicide becomes both an act of desperation and an implicit accusation of the regime.
    

---

### **2. How are the main protagonists characterized?**

|Character|Tacitus’ Portrayal|Implications|
|---|---|---|
|**Piso**|Proud, rash, overconfident; possibly guilty, but also victim of imperial politics.|Tacitus uses him as a tragic figure illustrating the dangers of ambition and Tiberian duplicity.|
|**Plancina**|Ambitious, unscrupulous, protected by Livia. Her abandonment of Piso marks moral collapse.|Tacitus uses her to show female influence at court (Livia’s role) and the corruption of justice.|
|**Germanicus** (in retrospect)|Noble, popular, virtuous; his death symbolizes the lost ideal of the Republic’s moral order.|Germanicus’ ghost haunts the narrative — his death exposes the moral decay of Tiberius’ regime.|
|**Tiberius**|Controlled, cold, calculating; master of appearances. He speaks with _moderatio_ but manipulates the outcome.|Tacitus contrasts his outward moderation with inward hypocrisy — a model of _dissimulatio_.|
|**Livia (Augusta)**|Powerful, meddling, associated with Plancina’s acquittal.|Symbol of matriarchal interference and moral decay.|
|**Senate**|Weak, compliant, theatrical; unable to act independently.|Tacitus’ cynicism: the Senate performs loyalty rather than delivering justice.|

In essence, **Tacitus turns the trial into a moral drama** about power, hypocrisy, and corruption under Tiberius.

---

### **3. How accurate do you think the narrative is?**

Tacitus was writing almost a century later (in the 110s CE), using senatorial records and oral traditions, but shaping them through his **moral and rhetorical agenda**.

**What’s accurate:**

- The trial really occurred in AD 20.
    
- Piso and Plancina were indeed prosecuted, Piso died before sentencing, and an official decree (SCPP) was issued afterward.
    
- The senatorial procedures and political dynamics (imperial intervention, accusations of poisoning, senatorial judgment) are broadly plausible.
    

**What’s tendentious:**

- Tacitus **moralizes and dramatizes** events. His narrative structure (Piso’s despair, Livia’s influence, Tiberius’ restraint) serves literary contrast rather than legal accuracy.
    
- His sources were **hostile to Tiberius**; he inherited a tradition that vilified the emperor and idealized Germanicus.
    
- Tacitus’ interpretation of motives — e.g., Tiberius’ hypocrisy or Livia’s guilt — is **impossible to verify**, but serves his theme of imperial corruption.
    

Thus, the narrative is **not “accurate” in a modern historical sense**, but **truthful in a moral and rhetorical sense**: it captures what Tacitus believed to be the inner truth (_veritas_) of imperial politics.

---

### **4. Chronological problems in Tacitus’ narrative**

Tacitus’ **chronology of the trial is problematic**, partly because he shapes events for dramatic effect.

**Issues:**

- He telescopes events that actually took place over several months (from Germanicus’ death in AD 19 to Piso’s trial and suicide in AD 20).
    
- The sequence of Piso’s return, the senatorial proceedings, and Drusus’ movements don’t align perfectly with other evidence (especially the SCPP).
    
- Tacitus implies the trial and Piso’s death occur close together, but the SCPP suggests a more extended and procedural process.
    

**Why this matters:**

- The **compressed chronology heightens drama** — Piso’s downfall feels inevitable, swift, and tragic.
    
- It also **reveals Tacitus’ narrative technique**: history is arranged for moral and psychological impact, not precise chronology.
    
- For historians, it complicates using Tacitus as a factual source: we must disentangle **literary structure from historical sequence**.

## **Part II: The _Senatus consultum de Cn. Pisone patre (SCPP)_**

### **1. Basics**

- **What:** An official senatorial decree recording the Senate’s judgment on Piso after his trial.
    
- **Where found:** At Baetica (southern Spain) in the town of _Baetica (Hispalis/Seville area)_.
    
- **Date:** 10 December AD 20.
    
- **Nature:** A **public document** published across the empire — celebrating justice, loyalty to Tiberius, and condemnation of Piso.
    

---

### **2. How does the language compare to Tacitus’?**

|Tacitus|SCPP|
|---|---|
|Literary, moralizing, ironic.|Bureaucratic, formulaic, self-congratulatory.|
|Emphasizes corruption, hypocrisy, and tragedy.|Emphasizes harmony, justice, and imperial clemency.|
|Uses moral vocabulary (_moderatio, pietas, scelus_).|Uses political vocabulary (_fides, concordia, pietas_).|

Tacitus’ tone is **critical and tragic**, the SCPP’s tone is **official and celebratory**.  
The SCPP is propaganda: it insists the Senate acted freely and justly, under the wise guidance of Tiberius. Tacitus sees that very claim as the mask of tyranny.

---

### **3. Do they share the same values?**

Superficially yes — both praise _pietas_, _fides_, _moderatio_.  
But Tacitus uses these values **ironically**; the SCPP uses them **sincerely**.

- Both claim to uphold **Roman moral ideals**.
    
- Tacitus shows that these ideals have become **rhetorical tools** of imperial control.
    
- The SCPP enacts what Tacitus critiques: the moral vocabulary of empire as a façade of virtue over coercion.
    

---

### **4. How do they compare? Which is more trustworthy?**

|Tacitus|SCPP|
|---|---|
|A retrospective literary account with moral interpretation.|A contemporary, official document reflecting the government’s position.|
|Shows suspicion, irony, hidden meanings.|Shows the _official ideology_ of Tiberius’ regime.|

**Historically:**  
The SCPP is more “accurate” in recording _what the Senate decided_, but **not impartial** — it’s imperial propaganda.  
Tacitus, though biased, offers a more revealing **psychological and moral truth** about how power worked.

**Thus:**

- The _SCPP_ is valuable for reconstructing procedure and ideology.
    
- _Tacitus_ is valuable for understanding perception, tension, and moral atmosphere.

## **Part III: Broader Questions**

### **Historiographical Implications**

- Tacitus’ account is a **literary reconstruction** — shaped by themes of dissimulation, tyranny, and moral decay.
    
- The SCPP is a **performative document** — it asserts senatorial _concordia_ and imperial justice.
    

Together they reveal the **dual language of the Principate**:

- _Tacitus_: “appearance versus reality.”
    
- _SCPP_: “appearance as reality.”
    

---

### **What both tell us about Tiberius and imperial discourse**

- **Tiberius’ image:** Tacitus shows him as cold and manipulative; the SCPP portrays him as just, merciful, and moderate.
    
- **Imperial power:** Both sources show how the emperor controlled the moral and legal vocabulary of Rome.
    
- **The Senate:** A body performing moral rhetoric while enacting imperial will — emblematic of the _new political culture_ of the Principate.
    

---

### **Does the inscription change how we view Tacitus?**

Yes — it forces us to recognize that **Tacitus was shaping his sources** dramatically.  
The SCPP shows a complex bureaucratic and ritualized process, not simply the moral theatre Tacitus describes.

Yet the SCPP also **confirms Tacitus’ central insight**: the empire’s moral and political language was performative, concealing coercion beneath virtue. Tacitus, far from being unfair, exposes what the SCPP tries to disguise.

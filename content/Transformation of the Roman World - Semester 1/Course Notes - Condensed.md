## **1. Crisis and Reorganisation, 3rd–4th Centuries**

### **The Third-Century Crisis (235–284)**

- Empire faces simultaneous crises: **external invasions** (Sasanian Persia; Germanic/Gothic groups), **internal instability** (rapid emperor turnover, civil war), **economic collapse**, **plague**, and **religious tensions**.
    
- **Economic breakdown**: debasement of silver and gold coinage → sharp inflation; salaried workers suffer.
    
- The “soldier emperors” stabilise frontier pressures temporarily but cannot solve structural weakness.
    

### **Diocletian (r. 284–305): A New Imperial Architecture**

- **Tetrarchy** (293): Two Augusti (Diocletian East; Maximian West) + two Caesars (Galerius; Constantius). Intended to solve succession problems and increase strategic responsiveness.
    
- **Administrative reforms**:
    
    - Provinces subdivided into c. 120 small units → grouped into 12–15 **dioceses** → under 3–4 **praetorian prefectures**.
        
    - **Split of civil/military authority** to prevent provincial rebellion.
        
    - Italy loses fiscal exemptions; more uniform taxation.
        
- **Mobility of emperors**: imperial courts centred on frontier zones; comitatus forms a mobile administrative core.
    
- **Economic policy**: systematic censuses; land and labour units assessed every 5 years; **Edict on Maximum Prices** fails to halt inflation.
    
- **Religious policy**: state-sponsored cults emphasised; persecution of Manichaeans and, episodically, Christians.

## **2. Constantine and the Christian Empire**

### **Rise of Constantine (306–324)**

- Power struggle after Diocletian’s retirement.
    
- 312: Constantine defeats Maxentius at **Milvian Bridge**, supported by vision narratives (“in hoc signo”).
    
- 324: Defeats Licinius and reunifies the Empire.
    

### **Constantine’s Reforms**

- **Foundation of Constantinople (324–330)**:
    
    - Geopolitical pivot: defendable, near strategic frontiers, positioned on east–west trade nexus.
        
    - Ideologically a “Second Rome”: Senate, grain supply, major monuments, imperial palace; Christian urban topography emphasised via relics and churches.
        
- **Army**:
    
    - Expansion to c. 600,000 effective troops.
        
    - Creation of new high-command posts and **mobile field armies** (comitatenses) complementing limitanei.
        
- **Fiscal/Monetary policy**:
    
    - **Solidus** becomes stable gold currency → long-term concentration of wealth among elites; bronze coinage continues to inflate.
        
- **Christianisation** (post-312):
    
    - Legal advantages to clergy; bishops gain judicial authority; Sunday laws.
        
    - Restrictions on pagan sacrifice; crucifixion banned.
        
- **Succession failures**:
    
    - Rivalries among Constantine’s sons lead to civil war, murders, and brief reigns of Gallus and Julian (who attempted a Hellenic religious revival but failed).

## **3. Late Fourth-Century Empire and the Gothic Crisis**

### **Valentinianic and Theodosian Dynasties**

- **Valentinian I** rules West (cap. Trier); his brother **Valens** rules East (cap. Antioch).
    
- Rise of imperial households and comitatus officers as key power brokers.
    

### **Goths and the Battle of Adrianople (378)**

- 376: Gothic groups request resettlement inside Danube frontier; mismanagement → famine → revolt.
    
- 378: Valens defeated and killed at **Adrianople**.
    
- **382 settlement**: Gothic groups allowed to settle on Roman soil with internal autonomy; they provide troops but retain leaders—**foederati** status becomes precedent for later barbarian polities.
    

### **Religious Developments**

- **Edict of Thessalonica (380)**: Nicene Christianity becomes privileged norm.
    
- **Council of Constantinople (381)**: reinforces orthodoxy; reorganises church authority.
    

### **Permanent Division (395)**

- Theodosius I dies; sons Arcadius (East) and Honorius (West) rule separately → final end of unified governance.
    

---

## **4. Fifth-Century West: Disintegration and Transformation**

### **Court Rivalries and Military Weakness**

- East–West distrust (e.g. conflicts over Illyricum).
    
- Western court struggles with multiple usurpers; reliance on generals like **Stilicho** and **Aetius**.
    
- **Alaric** demands position and pay → repeated negotiations and breakdowns.
    

### **Visigoths and the Sack of Rome (410)**

- Alaric’s Visigoths invade Italy (401–410).
    
- Sack of Rome (410): limited physical destruction but immense psychological impact.
    

### **Provincial Collapse**

- **Britain**: Roman withdrawal by 410 → disappearance of coinage, urban building, and villa economy; reversion to local power structures.
    
- **Gaul and Spain**: Vandals, Alans, Sueves spread; imperial authority fragmented.
    
- **North Africa**:
    
    - Vandals cross in 429; capture Carthage in 439.
        
    - 442 treaty cedes richest tax base → cripples Western state capacity.
        

### **The End of the Western Empire (476–480)**

- Odoacer deposes Romulus Augustulus in 476; rules Italy nominally under Eastern emperor.
    
- Death of Julius Nepos in 480 ends the line of Western emperors; West governed through Germanic kings under theoretical Eastern supremacy.

## **5. Constantinople and Urban Transformation**

### **City Development**

- Modelled on Rome: Senate, praefectus urbi, free grain supply.
    
- Monumental Christian landscape (Hagia Sophia; Holy Apostles; relic cult).
    
- Becomes principal imperial residence under Theodosius I and II; Pandidakterion (425) founded.
    

### **The First Siege (626)**

- Avar–Slav coalition and Persians threaten the city; repelled through walls + naval superiority + religious mobilisation (Theotokos cult).
    
- Reinforces mythic status of Constantinople as **protected by divine power**.
    

---

## **6. Society, Law, and Religion in Late Antiquity**

### **Law Codes**

- **Theodosian Code (438)**: laws since Constantine; book 16 emphasises religious legislation.
    
- Focus on suppressing pagan practice (sacrifice, temple use), restricting pagan elites’ roles; but temples often repurposed rather than destroyed.
    

### **Social Structure**

- Enormous fortunes (e.g. Melania, Pinianus) dominate landholding.
    
- Curiales (town councillors) squeezed between obligations and shrinking municipal revenues.
    
- Large-scale slavery persists; debate over decline inconclusive.
    

### **Christianisation**

- Spread through urban churches, bishops, relics; but in the West, increasingly through **rural monasteries** and negotiated local conversion.
    
- “Paganism” = catch-all term imposed by Christian authors, often for rural ritual practice.

## **7. Justinian and the Reconfiguration of the Mediterranean (6th Century)**

### **Triumphs and Limits of Reconquest**

- **Vandal War (533–534)**: rapid reconquest of North Africa.
    
- **Gothic War (535–554)**: prolonged conflict; Totila’s resurgence; enormous destruction of Italy; collapse of senatorial class; depopulation of Rome.
    
- **Spania (552)**: foothold in SE Iberia.
    

### **Consequences**

- Short-lived imperial revival; ultimately strained finances, weakened military, facilitated Lombard invasion (568).
    
- Deepens East–West cultural divergence.
    

---

## **8. Persia, Heraclius, and the Rise of Islam**

### **Persian Wars (602–628)**

- Rise of Phokas after mutiny → Persian exploitation (607–619): capture Jerusalem, Egypt, Syria.
    
- **Heraclius** reforms army and finances; launches counter-offensive (622–628).
    
- Victories in Armenia and Mesopotamia; recovery of True Cross.
    
- Empire emerges victorious but **exhausted**, financially ruined, and demographically weakened.
    

### **Early Islamic Expansion (630s–660s)**

- Arabs conquer Syria, Egypt, North Africa; massive naval battle at **Battle of the Masts (655)**.
    
- Byzantium retreats to Anatolian plateau; frontier stabilises along Taurus mountains.
    
- Umayyad siege of Constantinople (674–678) fails, but shows new existential threat.
    

### **Administrative Transformation**

- Empire reorganised into **themes** (military districts) with combined civil-military authority.
    
- Ruralisation of society; decline of classical urban culture; rise of smaller towns and fortified centres.
    

---

## **9. Slavs, Avars, Bulgars, and the Transformation of the Balkans**

### **Slavic Migration and Settlement**

- Slavs appear in sources from 6th century; initially raiders but gradually settle Balkan regions.
    
- Archaeology shows mixed populations, not total displacement.
    
- Many regions depopulated; collapse of urban life; rise of small agricultural villages.
    

### **Avars**

- Steppe confederacy dominating Carpathian basin; pressure on Byzantine positions → joint Slavic–Avar raids.
    

### **Bulgar State (681 onwards)**

- Emerges from Turkic groups north of Danube; 681 peace treaty recognises their kingdom.
    
- Bulgar khans interact with Byzantine culture; political centralisation accelerates in 8th–9th centuries.
    
- Major frontier rival: victories and defeats shape imperial strategy.
    

### **Byzantine Countermeasures**

- Population transfers (Asia Minor → Balkans; Balkans → Asia Minor).
    
- Campaigns in 8th–9th centuries gradually reassert control, esp. in Greece.

## **10. Iconoclasm and Byzantine Reorientation (8th–9th Centuries)**

### **Iconoclasm Phases**

1. **First phase (c. 730–787)** under Leo III and Constantine V.
    
2. **Second phase (815–843)** ending with the Triumph of Orthodoxy.
    

### **Causes and Interpretation**

- Earlier accounts blamed Islamic influence or a 726 edict (unlikely).
    
- More plausibly tied to:
    
    - struggle between imperial and monastic authority;
        
    - desire to discipline church wealth;
        
    - military failures in the East provoking religious introspection.
        

### **Constantine V**

- Systematises iconoclasm via Council of Hieria (754).
    
- Strengthens tagmata; breaks large themes into smaller units to weaken rebellious elites.
    

### **Restoration (787, 843)**

- Empress Irene restores icons (787).
    
- Final restoration in 843 under Theodora → ideological unification around icon veneration and Chalcedonian Orthodoxy.

## **11. Abbasid World and Transformation of the Mediterranean**

### **From Umayyads to Abbasids**

- Umayyads create vast “jihad state” with Arabicisation of administration, Islamic coinage, Dome of the Rock.
    
- Tension between Arab military elites and non-Arab converts (mawali).
    
- Abbasid Revolution (747–750) led by **Abu Muslim** → new dynasty.
    

### **The Abbasid Empire**

- Capital at **Baghdad (762)** marks Persian cultural ascendancy.
    
- State becomes highly bureaucratic (viziers; emirs).
    
- Intellectual golden age: translation of Greek, Persian, Syriac texts; advances in medicine, mathematics, astronomy.
    
- Caliphate less oriented toward frontier warfare with Byzantium; more cosmopolitan.
    

---

## **12. Post-Roman West: Kingship, Law, and Christianisation**

### **Successor States**

- **Franks** dominate Gaul; expand aggressively.
    
- **Visigoths** unify Iberia; conversion of Reccared (589) → Catholic identity.
    
- **Lombards** form smaller but well-governed kingdom in Italy.
    

### **Law Codes and Identity**

- Law codes (Salic Law, Edict of Rothari, Visigothic Code) combine Roman procedure with Germanic wergild traditions.
    
- Laws express **ethnic and religious ideology** (e.g., Visigothic law after conversion).
    
- Lombard royal laws show close interaction with cities and petitioners.
    

### **Christianisation of Europe**

- Not a simple top-down process; largely **local, negotiated**, often blending older customs.
    
- Monasteries become engines of literacy, landholding, and cultural production.
    
- Major saints’ lives show shifting gender norms and new social ideals.

# **Overall Interpretive Themes**

- The “Fall of Rome” = **long, uneven transformation**, not single event.
    
- **East survives** through fiscal strength, urban continuity, and administrative adaptability.
    
- **West fragments** into successor kingdoms with hybrid Roman–barbarian identities.
    
- Christianity becomes the dominant ideological framework shaping politics, law, and society.
    
- By c. 800, three heirs of Rome exist:
    
    - **Byzantium** (Orthodox Roman Empire),
        
    - **Carolingian West**

	- **Abbasid Caliphate** — all products of Late Antique transformation

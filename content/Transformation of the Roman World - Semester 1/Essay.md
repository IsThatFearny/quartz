## How Roman were the post-Roman ‘barbarian kingdoms’ in the West?

The 'barbarian kingdoms' which were established throughout and after the fall of the Western Roman Empire endured quite different processes of political and economic development and power consolidation. However, each shared common ground: various aspects of society remained 'Roman', whether that have been in terms of culture, administration, architecture, or religion. Whether these kingdoms were true 'successor states' is debatable, but there is very little doubt that elements 'Romanness' remained commonplace throughout the Visigothic, Ostrogothic, Lombard, and Frankish kingdoms despite the supposed 'fall' of the Western Empire.

One of the primary lasting features of the former Western Empire was Christianity, which was widely practised in the majority of 'barbarian' territory (until the Muslim conquests in the 7th century). A religion popularised by the Roman Empire, it is perhaps no surprise that it remained the dominant religion for the barbarian kingdoms. 'Romanness' from the 4th-century onwards was characterised largely by Christianity, and indeed after the fall of the Western Empire, much of Rome's authority was derived from its status as a Christian city, having been the stage for significant apistolic events and various martyrdoms. The Church, whether in its Arian or Catholic form, survived the collapse of the Western Empire, and its survival also protected its inherent moral authority, as well as preserving the Latin language (if only usually in ecclesiastical contexts). Theoderic, in particular, was a devout Christian, as was Clovis. The local populations of their kingdoms, largely former Roman citizens, remained true to their Christian faith, and thus the rulers of newly acquired ex-Roman territories saw adopting Christianity as a relatively simple way to both legitimise their authority and power, to create a link between their rule and divine will, and to connect themselves to the population over which they now had command. This meant that many existing structures of the Roman church such as Bishops, monasticism, and the papacy were allowed to remain in the post-Roman world, both centrally and provincially. Christianity was a binding force in barbarian rulers' attempts to integrate themselves and the ethnic groups associated with them with native populations. 'Romanness', when characterised by Christianity, can be seen to have been maintained throughout much of the former Western Empire. However, whilst Christianity remained, the form in which it did so varied significantly. Former Roman citizens largely adhered to Catholicism, whereas their new rulers were usually Arian Christians. In Ostrogothic Italy, this was of little concern: Theoderic promulgated Arian Christianity, but did not persecute Catholics; however, in some areas of the former Western Empire, anti-Catholic policies were actively pursued. North Africa, ruled by the Vandals, was a prime example of this. Under Thrasamund and Geiseric, practising Nicean (Catholic) Christians could face deposition, exile, and various other penalties, in a blatant attempt to bring the Vandal kingdom under one form of Christianity. It must be argued, then, that whilst Christian Romanness persisted in some regions of the former Western Empire, this did not apply to all kingdoms, where different forms of Christianity took precedent.

As well as religion, another main element of 'Romanness' that remained in the newly established 'barbarian' kingdoms was Roman bureaucracy and the use of Roman administrators to manage finances, logistics, and the day-to-day business of running the kingdom. In order for the kingdom to continue operating smoothly, new rulers needed former Roman citizens to operate the bureaucratic systems which they had chosen to retain, and many aristocratic Romans were more than content to work under their new masters in similar roles to those which they had formerly occupied whilst the Western Empire was still operational. The new rulers were happy to share some of their authority with the occupied natives not only because it allowed them to maintain their kingdom, but also because involving occupied Romans in governmental systems played a large role in placating them and suppressing any potential resistance to the new regime. The division of power was not, as one might assume, an immutable line between Frank, Goth, or Vandal, and the Roman citizenry - the divide was blurred to a significant extent. A certain level of 'Romanness' was certainly maintained in provincial and central governance of the barbarian kingdoms, not only in terms of the systems through which it operated but also in terms of the workforce that manned it. The same applied to the military: the former Roman army often remained unchanged, aside from their fealty to a new leader. There was little point in dismantling systems which had operated effectively and smoothly for centuries, especially as many Roman soldiers were more than happy to remain in the army. However, there were caveats to this 'Romanness'. Firstly, Roman citizens were assigned much lower value than members of the ruling race. For example, those who had a place in the Frankish court and were of Frankish descent were valued at exactly twice the value of their Roman counterpart. Similarly, almost all military and governmental power remained in the hands of the Goths, Vandals and Franks - it would be a mistake to ascribe power of any significance to those Romans who remained involved in the management of the kingdom. Thus one must conclude that, whilst the bureaucracy and military remained 'Roman', it does not follow that the kingdom itself remained as such.

In a similar vein, the legal codes of the 'barbarian' kingdoms were often simply Roman legal codes re-written to suit the new regime, another aspect of Romanness which survived the fall of the Western Empire. For example, what little remains of the contents of the *Codex Euricianus*, a Gothic lawbook (possibly written by Euric of the Visigoths), bears a striking resemblance to Roman laws, as does the *Edictum Theoderic* Likewise, Siagrius, a Gallo-Roman general, is referred to by Sidonius as "a new Solon of the Burgundians in discussing the laws", which implies that Siagrius was writing laws for the Burgundians in a form that was stylistically Roman (Siagrus had previously been a Roman senator). The continuation of Roman law is further demonstrated by the fact that legal systems which had been introduced for the protection of land-owners were allowed to remain in place in order to maintain property rights throughout the former empire. The legal codes of the Western Empire were so deeply ingrained into society that, even in the post-Roman era, deconstructing them would have introduced the risk of an administrative collapse: it was simply easier to keep the Roman systems in place. Legal systems are one of the clearest ways in which one can identify 'romanness' in the post-Roman 'barbarian' kingdoms, and it must be concluded from this that, in this aspect, the post-Roman kingdoms indeed retained some 'romanness'.

Often, this was used as a tactic to consolidate power (as with the adoption of Christianity), and a method with which to relate to the newly conquered former Roman population.

# Long Essay

**How have modern attitudes to empire affected the study of imperialism in the**  
**ancient world? Is it right that they should do so?**

---

The study of imperialism in the ancient world (largely that of the Greeks and Romans) often suffers from anachronistic scholarship, imposing modern views which are often formed from our modern conception of 'imperialism' onto societies which had no such concepts. Modern attitudes towards empire include the introduction of 'colonialism' as a lens through which to study imperialism, and the notion of empire as a mission with the aim of dominion over another population. Neither of these modern attitudes accurately reflect imperialism in the ancient world, and thus this essay shall argue that, as a result of said inaccuracy, it is not right that contemporary academia should treat ancient empire as one and the same as the imperialism in more recent years (such as that of the British Empire). Modern concepts such as colonialism, globalisation, and the like can illuminate power structures, settlements patterns, and broader characteristics of ancient imperialism, however they should not be the sole lens through which empire in the ancient world is studied in modern times.

To analyse how contemporary attitudes towards empire negatively affect the study of ancient imperialism, one must first note the major differences between the ancient world and more recent empires. One of the most important distinctions is the development of the modern nation-state. Imperialism in the Peloponnese and the wider Greek world was not based on the dominance of one particular 'state', but rather operated through the *polis*, 'city-states' which had their own laws and customs.[^1] These poleis, whilst often under Spartan or Athenian hegemony, did not answer directly to a centralised governmental structure as has been the case with more modern empires, but instead were often subject to a form of imperialism by consent, with a recognition that this would be the best arrangement if the *polis* were to remain financially and militarily efficient.[^2] The territories of the Roman Empire, whilst adhering to a much stricter form of centralised government with power vested in the Emperor, still did not fall under the modern definition of 'nation-states', as much of them were made up of autonomous townships (albeit with varying degrees of independence from Roman rule). Modern scholars of empire often define imperialism as the dominance of one nation-state (such as Britain) over another (such as India), a model which clearly does not fit with the forms which ancient empires took.[^3]
 
The first modern view that tends to be impressed onto the ancient empires of Greece and Rome is that of 'colonialism'. It is often assumed in modern scholarship that 'colonialism' necessarily entails mass-migration and European/Western domination over non-European regions.[^4] This term can be successfully applied to many modern empires, the British Empire most obviously. However, the Roman Empire and Greek imperialism do not fall into such a category. The expanse of the Roman empire at its height was largely European (with the exception of North Africa and Western Asia) and mass migration was not thought of in the same sense as it is in the modern era: the concept of national identity had not emerged, and as such the racialised element behind the concept of much modern migration (which biases contemporary scholars when engaged in discourse over ancient imperialism) simply did not exist in the Roman world.[^5]  Whilst Greek imperialism did largely push eastward, it is very important to note that the sense of a 'European identity' was not yet formed. Scholars too often characterise Ancient Greece, for example, as white, European, colonisers, and perhaps as a result Europe often defines itself in terms of the Ancient Greek world - however the Ancient Greeks certainly did not define themselves by European terms or by European characteristics.[^6] This means that colonialism when characterised as a 'European vs non-European' endeavour cannot fit into the ancient model of imperialism.  The concept of 'colonialism' as it is interpreted in contemporary academia must be applied carefully and with nuance to ancient empires and ancient imperialism; application of such a concept too generally becomes highly problematic for our understanding of these empires. The marked increase in and emphasis on the study of 'colonialism' in contemporary scholarship also gives rise to study through a 'postcolonial' lens. Postcolonial studies often have a tendency to fall into the trap of characterising empires as a conflict between 'European' and 'barbarian', or "the west and the rest", but Greek imperialism had no such conflict: as previously referenced, the *poleis* under Athenian and Spartan hegemony saw themselves as self-governing city-states, and those in Athens and Sparta who administrated taxation and tribute, amongst other aspects of the politics of the *polis*, often did not see the *poleis* as 'other'.[^7] The 'us and them' mentality of future colonial projects was not present in the Greek world. Indeed, Ancient Greece and even the imperialist subdivisions of the Mediterranean should not be seen as a single political entity.[^8] It is a failure of modern post-colonial perspectives to apply such binary thinking to societies which had no such concept.

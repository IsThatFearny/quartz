# Medieval Worlds Lecture 18 (That I've attended...) - Eurasian Connections on the 'Silk Roads'


- The term 'silk road' was popularised largely in the 1980s, although coined by Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877
- The original silk roads (as largely attested to by the archive at Donhuang) ran through central Asia, i.e. from the Middle East through Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, etc. and to the central east coast of China
- The UNESCO Silk Roads program implies that the Silk Roads stretched to western Europe (i.e. France and Spain), to the Indian ocean, through North and North-East Africa, and throughout much of eastern Europe

This begs the question of whether the 'silk roads' should be defined as the former or the latter - the latter is the way in which the term is used today, especially in popular history - this use can be helpful in showing how the medieval world was interconnected, however one risks overgeneralisation, a lack of geographical specificity, and a loss of personal agency.

</br>

### The 'Silk Roads' in popular imagination

- The **primary role** of the Silk Roads is understood as connecting East Asia with the lands to the west, especially Europe; the direction of travel is generally understood to be **East --> West**
- Largely imagined as a space for private merchants, who travel through a sort of 'no-man's land' to connect civilisations; this view erases central Eurasians and their culture, causing them to be depicted as 'way-stations'
- The Silk Road 'opens' and 'closes' depending on the period

</br>

### Reframing the 'Silk Roads'

Although the term might be over-romanticised, the term provides an entry point for thinking about the connections throughout Eurasia. However, the term requires **fundamental reframing**, especially to assert the central Eurasian place in history.

Eurasian connections are social processes, relying on physical infrastructure, institutions and knowledge. This means that we need to flesh out our idea of central Eurasia as a collection of oasis cities with a rich set of state bureaucracy and religious institutions. These state institutions were vital to forming these connections. Many early figures of the 'Silk Roads' were diplomats, although the line between diplomacy and commerce is very blurred.

</br>

### Why the *Silk* roads?

- Silk was a form of currency (especially in paying soldier's salaries) and taxation in many east Asian states
- It was integral in diplomacy and trade with various nomadic empires

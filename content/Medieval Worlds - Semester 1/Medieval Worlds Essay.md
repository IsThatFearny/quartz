### Was long-distance travel basically a matter of setting out into the unknown during the Middle Ages?

There are various accounts of long-distance travel throughout the Middle Ages, ranging from pilgrimages, to missionary work, to diplomacy. Long-distance travel was certainly an arduous task, with many challenges and climes to combat; but whether it was a matter of 'setting out into the unknown' is greatly debated. The traditional conception of the people of the Middle Ages as "confined within limited geographical horizons", whilst mostly considered to be inaccurate, still proliferates, perhaps even subconsciously, amongst scholarship.[^1] It is true to an extent that long-distance travel was off-limits to the general populous, but there are many exceptions to this rule. Geographical scholarship, including cartography and cosmography, was relatively common, and medieval travellers did have access to 'guidebooks' of a sort, as well as some quite accurate maps in the latter half of the period. This essay shall utilise this evidence in arguing that Medieval long-distance travel was not 'a matter of setting out into the unknown', but rather much information was available to the traveller if they so desired.

By far the most common accounts that we have of long-distance travel in the Middle Ages are those that describe pilgrimages.[^2] These journeys were largely undertaken by Christians to major religious sites, with destinations reaching from the Iberian Peninsula to the Holy Lands in the Middle East.

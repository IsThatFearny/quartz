### Was long-distance travel basically a matter of setting out into the unknown during the Middle Ages?

There are various accounts of long-distance travel throughout the Middle Ages, ranging from pilgrimages, to missionary work, to diplomacy. Long-distance travel was certainly an arduous task, with many challenges and climes to combat; but whether it was a matter of 'setting out into the unknown' is greatly debated. The traditional conception of the people of the Middle Ages as "confined within limited geographical horizons", whilst mostly considered to be inaccurate, still proliferates, perhaps even subconsciously, amongst scholarship.[^1] It is true to an extent that long-distance travel was off-limits to the general populous, but there are many exceptions to this rule. Geographical scholarship, including cartography and cosmography, was relatively common, and medieval travellers did have access to 'guidebooks' of a sort, as well as some quite accurate maps in the latter half of the period. This essay shall utilise this evidence in arguing that Medieval long-distance travel was not 'a matter of setting out into the unknown', but rather much information was available to the traveller if they so desired.

By far the most common accounts that we have of long-distance travel in the Middle Ages are those that describe pilgrimages.[^2] These journeys were largely undertaken by Christians to major religious sites, with destinations reaching from the Iberian Peninsula to the Holy Lands in the Middle East. The journeys of individual pilgrims such as Egeria and Arculf, both of whom made the pilgrimage to the Holy Land in the early Middle Ages. A description of the travels of each was written: in Egeria's case, she penned the *Peregrenatio*; Arculf's travels were described by Adamnan, the abbot with whom Arculf stayed for a period, in *De locis sanctis*.[^3] We turn to the *Peregrenatio*. Egeria gives descriptions of physical landmarks and of the peoples who inhabit these regions. In the *itinerarium*, Egeria gives a list of places through which she passed on her journey. These places are, of course, all included as a result of their importance to her pilgrimage and are thus primarily religious sites. The *itinerarium* does not just give names but also provides a rough spatial location for each site, describing the distance between each site. In this way the reader can map out Egeria's route (albeit roughly) and thus follow her pilgrimage.[^4] Such a list forms a sort of 'guidebook' for pilgrims wishing to make a devotional journey to the Holy Land. Many such 'guidebooks' were created, and used for pilgrimages to various places across Afro-Eurasia. Pilgrimages were perhaps the journey for which travellers had the most information on where and how to travel, and on the sights and inhabitants of such far-flung locations. In this sense, at least, travellers were not 'setting out into the unknown'.

Although primitive, navigational aids were used widely amongst long-distance travellers, especially those who were educated in navigation and cosmography. Maps were one such aid. Although some maps were not always intended for practical navigation (for example, the Hereford map is widely agreed to have been used as a religious/historical guide instead of a system to navigate by), others were, and were surprisingly accurate.[^5] Take, for example, the Catalan Atlas. Produced in the 1370s (the same century that the Hereford Map was created), its accuracy in mapping western Europe to the Middle East (with special attention paid to the Iberian peninsula) was unprecedented, and its thoroughness in its depiction of places of interest (especially religious sites) made it an incredibly helpful guide to the long-distance traveller.[^6] Who exactly used the atlas is unknown, but there is evidence of the Aragonese royal house commissioning similar maps for their long-distance journeys.[^7] In a similar fashion, various other instruments were used by travellers: there is evidence for the use of the magnetic compass in the late 12th century in the writings of Alexander Neckham, although it is theorised that it could have been in use even earlier; and the continuous development of the astrolabe significantly aided those who were able to navigate by the stars.[^8] Not only did these developments help with navigation at sea, but could also help during land journeys as well. This demonstrates that navigation was possible in the Middle Ages, albeit only for those capable of using such devices.

In stark contrast to 'setting out into the unknown', many medieval travellers such as Ibn Battutah found their journey made significantly easier by the fact that they shared a common religion: Islam. Ibn Battutah's *Rihla* (Book of Travels) gives a thorough description of major Islamic cities throughout much of Eurasia and elsewhere in the Islamic world. The reader of the *Rihla* gets the strong impression that Ibn Battutah was well looked after throughout his travels: he keeps the company of regional leaders such as the Sultan Muhammad Uzbak Khan and the Khatun Bayalun and is able to use his connections in North Africa to afford himself certain luxuries.[^9] Being able to speak Arabic also seems to have helped significantly. When a member of one of the most geographically widespread religions in the Medieval world, and sharing a common language, the connections that one could make and the attitude towards such a traveller were much improved. A major difference can be seen between the travels of Ibn Battutah and another Islamic traveller, Usama ibn Munqidh. Usama travelled to the Frankish kingdoms, and this seems to have ingrained hostility towards the Franks in him. When one reads his account in his *Book of Contemplation*, it is clearly shown that his treatment is not of a calibre equal to that of Ibn Battutah - this is perhaps on account of his differing religion (there certainly seems to be hostility towards Muslims as demonstrated in the account of the capture of a ship carrying Muslim pilgrims).[^10] Having something such as religion or language in common with the peoples that a traveller was likely to encounter allowed them to have knowledge of their geographical location, the laws of the land, and the regional political hierarchy, and thus for Islamic travellers long-distance travel was likely less about 'setting out into the unknown' than it may have been for those venturing into new lands such as Usama Ibn Munqidh (or indeed William of Rubruck in his journey to the Mongols).

Throughout the Middle Ages, there was a clear development in the geographic literary record. At the beginning of the Medieval millennium, antique sources were the primary method of gaining knowledge about the world and its geography.[^11] Ancient writers such as Pliny, Aristotle, and Ptolemy provided much of the reference material for those wishing to travel, and the Bible was also widely used as a source of information (especially for early pilgrimages).[^12] Very often, these sources were wildly inaccurate, filled with apocryphal tales of monsters and demons in far-off lands, and more concerned with story-telling than geographical accuracy.[^14] The Bible especially was relatively unhelpful, as it unsurprisingly viewed geography through a religious myopia. Not only this, but the process of transmission from these ancient authors to those in the early Medieval period was far from direct, especially as the majority of the populous was illiterate.[^13] Thus, those travelling in the early Middle Ages were indeed often 'setting out into the unknown'. As the millennium progressed, 'modern' travellers such as those mentioned above began to provide more accurate, comprehensive, and contemporary geographical information, and their works soon began to take precedence over ancient authors, however respected they may have been.[^15] Such an increase in accuracy meant that those travellers who lived in the latter stages of the Medieval period were vastly more knowledgeable about the world around them, their journeys, and their destinations than their predecessors.
 
The answer question of whether long-distance travel was a matter of 'setting out into the unknown' in the Middle Ages depends largely on circumstance. The religion, tongue, education, and temporal context of the traveller would determine their knowledge of the world and its geography. Those such as Ibn Battutah, who was well-educated, came from a wealthy family, spoke Arabic, was Muslim, and lived in the latter half of the Medieval millennium was at a clear advantage to those who did not have the same educational opportunities, nor shared a common tongue, nor had the good fortune to live in the late Middle Ages. Overall, however, one can safely conclude that long-distance travel - whilst still full of surprises for those who undertook it - was, for many, not a matter of 'stepping out into the unknown'.

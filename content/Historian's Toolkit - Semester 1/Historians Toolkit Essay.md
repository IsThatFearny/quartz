### # Do we distort LGBTQ+ history by treating the Stonewall Riots as a moment of radical change?

The Stonewall Riots of 1969 are widely agreed upon by leading historians to be a 'moment of radical change' in the trajectory of the LGBTQ+ rights movement.[^1] However, there is debate over whether, by treating the event as such, we 'distort' the history of what came before. This essay shall argue that treating Stonewall as a moment of radical change, does not necessarily distort pre-stonewall LGBTQ+ history, but we must avoid defining Stonewall as the 'beginning' of the LGBTQ+ rights movement as is the tendency of some of the literature.

There is clear evidence of Stonewall's transformative impact upon the LGBTQ+ rights movement. On the whole, the movement saw a major shift from the homophile movement's approach of legal challenges and peaceful demonstrations with an emphasis on single-issue politics, to the gay liberation movement's tactics of direct action (largely adopted from various other civil rights movements of the 1960s) and the politicisation of sexuality and 'coming out'.[^2] This change was marked by an explosion in the number of organisations post-Stonewall: just before Stonewall occurred, there were around fifty LGBTQ+ rights organisations in the US; By 1973, this had grown to over eight-hundred.[^3] Various victories were won, such as out gays running for office and in some cases even winning, as well as the representation (although still relatively rare) of gay characters in media.[^4] The pre-Stonewall homophile movement could claim no such success - for tall their talk of integration and acceptance, from World War II to 1969 very little *de facto* progress had been made.[^5] One can see how some historians see Stonewall and its offshoots (such as the Gay Liberation Front) as the beginning of the 'real' LGBTQ+ rights movement.[^6] 

However, this interpretation is a mistake. It was, of course, convenient for those involved in post-Stonewall activism to see it this way - indeed, as Stein says, "What better way for post-Stonewall activists to represent themselves as having broken with the past than to deny that lesbian and gay politics even had a past?".[^7] It is *this* interpretation that, intentionally or not, distorts LGBTQ+ history. For example, it removes any mention of the contribution made by those of the homophile movement who were 'out' before Stonewall, and Ada Bello (founder of the Philadelphia chapter of the Daughters of Bilitis, a major Lesbian homophile movement), who herself viewed Stonewall as a "turning point", pointed out that "The new militancy and radical approaches are the logical result of long years of laborious battles".[^8]

### Why does Eddins argue that 'organic acts of reproductive justice' by enslaved women should be part of how we think about the history of the Haitian Revolution?

Eddins argues that the desire to commit such acts of 'reproductive justice' may have contributed more to the Haitian Revolution than previous scholarship has shown. She shows that some African women were willing to commit 'maronnage' and live with other, un-enslaved communities (such as the Maniel community) in order to enjoy greater reproductive freedom, and thus contributed to an increase in the population of these unenslaved, radical communities, whose networks of radicals and revolutionaries were largely responsible for the orchestration of the rebellion. Thus, the 'organic acts of reproductive justice' that Eddins refers to seem to have had a large role to play in the Haitain revolution, even if the women involved did not directly contribute to the revolution itself as a result.

*Hollow Land* is Eyal Weizman's investigation into the Israeli regime of occupation, apartheid, and imperial expansion, with a specific focus on Weizman's speciality of architecture and urban planning. The book details how the Israeli regime has utilised architecture to maintain and expand their occupation of Palestine (henceforth referred to as the Occupied Territories), as well as their domination of the Palestinian people. Weizman takes care to define architecture as a practise includes the physical sense and also that which transcends it, placing heavy emphasis on the role of generals, politicians, and elites as the 'builders' of a 'constructed reality'. The focus of the book, therefore, is two-fold: Weizman details the physical architecture of the occupation, such as the barrier walls, checkpoints, and the architecture of the city of Jerusalem; but also allocates a great deal of the book to more ideological and theoretical 'architecture', including the urban warfare tactics of the Israeli Occupation Forces (IOF), the use of settlements as the Israeli methodology of expansion and observation of the Occupied Territories, the 2005 evacuation of Gaza and the militarisation of Palestinian airspace in the form of targeted assassinations.[^1]

It is important to note the temporal context of the book. The content within was published in 2007, in the immediate aftermath of the second Intifada. Therefore, it does not take into account the Gaza War of 2008-9, nor of 2014, and it cannot account for the genocide undertaken by the Israeli government since October 7th 2023, during which much of the architecture which Weizman details has not been in use or has been abandoned completely. As such, some of the information within the book has been rendered inaccurate in the present day. However, this by no means limits the book as a historical source nor as a medium for the discussion of imperialism.

In writing *Hollow Land*, Weizman was in a position of privilege as an Israeli - when curating his sources, he was able to access Israeli politicians and generals (the venn diagram of the two is effectively a circle), who were allegedly very forthcoming with information about such things as the 'walking through walls' methods of the IOF's urban warfare strategy and the ideology of targeted assassinations. Where a Palestinian writer would not be able to access these sources as a result of the apartheid of the Israeli regime, as an Israeli himself Weizman is able to provide a unique insight into the inner workings of the occupation and the theory behind it, directly from the mouths of the men who orchestrated it.

Weizman's primary intervention, which he refers to throughout *Hollow Land*, is that of the 'politics of verticality'. He argues that the Israeli occupation should not be viewed through a purely cartographical, 2-dimensional lens, but instead that a third dimension must be added to a view of the occupation in order to truly understand the Israeli imperial project as one that utilises topography to great effect. This 3-dimensional understanding encompasses the tunnels used by the Palestinian Resistance, disparity in water accessibility, the use of sewage systems to forcibly displace communities, the vertical apartheid within Jerusalem and across the Occupied Territories, and the airborne occupation enforced after the evacuation of Gaza. The 'politics of verticality' can be viewed as mapping vertical space onto value, and the consistent depiction of Israel and its citizens as physically higher is an metaphor for their alleged ideological superiority over Palestinians. In Jerusalem, for example, Jewish residents live on the higher floors of houses, accessible by secure rooftop pathways, whilst Palestinians live below on the streets; in a similar vein, the majority of Israeli settlements in the Occupied Territories are built on hilltops, ensuring that the Palestinians who live below can always see the lights of an Israeli settlement at night. We can also see this verticality at a subterranean level: one of the most regularly repeated Zionist propaganda points regarding the Jewish right to Palestine is that the land was promised to them by divine mandate in Genesis 12:1 (among other verses), and therefore Israel pursues archaeological digs with a fervour, believing that the remains of Jewish settlements from millennia beforehand will legitimise their claim to Jerusalem and the Occupied Territories. Whether this claim is legitimate or not, such a metaphor is important for the Israeli regime in maintaining their sense of superiority over Palestinians and thereby justifying their illegal occupation. Not only is verticality a useful metaphor for Israel, but also serves strategic purposes in a more physical sense. For example, the civilians who live in settlements are used as government puppets of observation, able to survey the surrounding territory from their vantage point and inform on any 'insurgent' activity, their living rooms intentionally facing outward towards Palestinian territory; and settlements often double as military bases, so that the IOF are able to mount attacks on Palestinian villages and refugee camps from the high ground. Weizman's concept of the 'politics of verticality' is a new foray into the methodology of the Israeli occupation, moving beyond the two-dimensional 'borders' of previous annexations of the Palestinian state which has characterised much of the discourse surrounding the Israeli occupation.

Not only does Weizman introduce his 'politics of verticality' to the academic arena, he also makes the argument that Israeli architecture has been politicised as a discipline. He makes the point that, whilst many of his Israeli colleagues might not think of their job as a political one, it is in fact deeply entrenched in the politics of the occupation.

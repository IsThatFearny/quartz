Frontier Architecture

**Jerusalem**

- Jerusalem: outer circle of settlements termed an ‘organic’ wall, without of which is the actual separation wall; ‘metropolitan Jerusalem *is* the occupation’ - Jeff Halper; new buildings have to be built with ‘Jerusalem stone’, giving the impression of unity and a ‘connection’ to the Holy City - ‘stone embodied biblical tradition’. Stone was also too expensive for local inhabitants, pricing them out of the city despite it being the alleged vernacular. Many new buildings are merely clad with stone and provisions have been put in place to make sure that such cladding ‘conveys an emotional message’ even at extra cost. The perceived ‘holiness’ of this material ensured that Jerusalem was characterised as a ‘holy city’ in itself, and the further it expanded, the more land was made ‘holy’
- Colonel Ronald Storrs (Military gov. of Jerusalem in 1917): the Zionist enterprisewas ‘forming for england a little loyal Jewish Ulster in a sea of potentially hostile Arabism’
- Archaeology: A method of tying the state of Israel to the land; often carried out by secularists with nationalist ambitions (as opposed to religious ones). Archaeological and historical sites designated as ‘nations and cultural property’, ‘amounting to a de facto annexation of the ground beneath the Occupied Territories. In some cases, upper storeys of new homes would be built on their archaeological foundations. Ram Karmi: ‘the search for nations identity must be conducted through architecture’
- Jerusalem Old City: ‘Rather than a multicultural city centre the Jewish Quarter might be better described as an artificial, ethnically homogenous, gated neighbourhood, who construction was made possible by the forced displacements of its inhabitants. It is a ‘biblical’ theme park, sending out further tentacles of Jewish housing enclaves and religious study-centres into the Muslim quarter to which it is connected above street level via protected and exclusive roof paths. The separation of this enclave from its surroundings is further enforced by the fact that all entrances and exits to the Jewish quarter are guarded by the border police, providing access, after body and bag scans, only to Jewish residents/settlers, tourists, and the Israeli army and police’
- Jerusalem demographics: Arab 28%, Jew 72% - this target is achieved by housing development and urban planning e.g. causing overcrowding in Palestinian areas and thereby forcing house prices up - by leaving the city, Palestinians lose ‘Israeli residency’ which allows them access to state service and healthcare
- Kollek: ‘the primary purpose of defining Shuafat ridge as a green area was the prevent arab building until the time was ripe to build a new Jewish neighbourhood’
- Palestinian neighbourhoods are prevented from expanding outward or upward under the pretext of preservation - permitted floor area ratio is kept low to prevent more building on an owned plot of land
    

**Fortifications**

- Original 1949 cease-fire lines never seen as permanent by either party, despite international legitimacy, and so very little border defence was constructed. 1967 borders (Israel as a state with borders marked by the Suez, Jordan, and the Golan Heights) perceived as completion and so were fortified more
- The frontier is presented as ‘a labaratory for the creation of a “new Jew”’, a ‘mythical space that shaped the character and institutions of the nation’
- Special Commando Unit 101: created for the sole purpose of frontier raids; a geopolitically rogue, minimally hierarchical, and often illegal force led by Ariel Sharon. Mythic status cultivated in the minds of Israeli youth. Moshe Dayan (mentor to the unit and to Ariel Sharon): Unit 101 was a “workshop for the creation of a new generation of [Hebrew] warriors
- Transgression of borders signified that Israeli borders were liquid and permeable, presenting its settler-colonial project as unfinished
- Government had plausible deniability into Unit 101’s activities allowing Israel to plead innocence when civilians were slaughtered
- Unit 101 was seen as the model for the IDF in 1953 - until 1973 war, IDF was seen as a central to Israeli identity
- Israeli military frontier strategy was dynamic and flexible - rather than fortifying a specific area with a wall or munitions (especially on the suez), the military would occupy hilltops and vantage points, just behind the banks of the canal, allowing them to move quickly
- Attackers are allowed to gain space in exchange for extra time for the IDF; IDF is then able to flank - no specific defensive points
- Sharon viewed the armed conflict between the Palestinian resistance and the IDF as an urban problem - rapid expansion of refugee camps would later become known as the ‘jihad of building’. PLO-backed and PLO splinter groups based themselves in the network of refugee camps, creating a series of armed enclaves. They would then engage in military operations against occupying forces and suspected Palestinian collaborators
- Occupying forces rarely entered refugee camps to carry out administrative tasks
- Sharon ordered a shoot-to-kill policy of suspected insurgents, carried out by assassination squads (1000+ killed)
- Military bulldozers ordered to carve roads through three of the largest refugee camps to make it easier for infantry to access smaller communities, plus creating a ‘security line’ around the camps preventing anyone from leaving or entering without being noticed
- Jewish settlements wedged into Gaza like ‘fingers’ in order to separate Palestinian settlements from each other
- Israeli government moved Sharon around once each ‘task’ had been completed, attempting to disassociate his brutality from state-sponsored actions in Gaza snd the Sinai
- 1973, Yom Kippur War: Suez canal lines breached by conventional military tactics utilised by a joint Egyptian-Syrian force; dug themselves in a few kilometres past the suez with a garrison of 100,000; massive IDF defeat when tanks intending to break Egyptian lines were halted by Sager anti-tank missiles. This breach of the Bar Lev line had a massive effect of military and civilian morale, seen as ‘breaching the city walls’
- Sharon as Minister for Settlement: Proposed a series of Jewish settlements - over 100 points to be inhabited by suburban, urban, and industrial settlements on the mountain ridges of the West Bank, with larger settlements supplementing smaller ones. These settlements were intended to function not only as dwellings, but as barriers to envelope the Palestinian-populated mountain regions, an imposition of a new network upon a previously existing one, isolating Palestinian settlements from each other by Israeli controlled roads, barriers, and military bases. This replaced the necessity of IDF forces within the now immobilised Palestinian cities, allowing Oslo accords to be adhered to without losing control
- Sharon: ‘there was no place that was built without a reason’; some military, some production, some major traffic arteries… *all* strategically placed
- Visibility: important for Israel that Palestinians can see their complete and utter domination; Sharon: “Arabs should see Jewish lights every night from 500 metres”
- Settlements began as very mobile outposts - caravans, tents, mobile homes, etc. could be moved in quietly, under cover of night, and without the need for roads (helicopters were often used)
- Settlements were propagated to a traumatised Israeli population as a defence against a possible repeat of the Yom Kippur war perpetrated by the Egyptians, Syrians, or Jordanians
    

**Settlements: Battle for the Hilltops**

- Settlement attempts outside of Allon plan borders were deemed illegal by the Israeli government, although this did not deter some settlers, who (often with the support of Ariel Sharon) would try and settle an area with Rabbis, academics, and Knesset members with them (called ‘ascents’)
- Propagates of these ascents lobbied the Knesset to grant them permission to settle - often military bases would be offered as settlements with the implicit understanding that these settlements would far outgrow the bases. It became evident quickly that the government could be pressured into allowing ‘illegal’ settlements (as evidenced by the success of groups such as Gush Emunim)
- Settlement project was a ‘strategy without a strategist’, largely formed out of ‘ad hoc improvisation, opportunistic actions, and conflicts between different politicians’- not a coherent ideology of the state. Discuss individualistic agents of imperialism? Must be emphasised that settler-colonialism was still official state policy, although somewhat dictated by manufactured ‘facts on the ground’
- Settlement plans were inherently also partition plans
- Settlement proposals often included using Palestinians as cheap labour
- Appearance of settlements being out of control allowed the state to achieve their objectives without claiming responsibility for illegal actions - Israel afforded international absolution by characterising settlement initiatives as carried out by ‘rogue citizens’, and enabled Egyptian/American/Israeli negotiations to take place seemingly in good faith
- At first asking, the High Court of Israel accepted arguments that new settlements could reasonably be considered as legitimate security measures as set out in the Hague Convention, and thus settlers had grounds to construct a community there to create a wedge between Palestinian towns. This set the tone for the High Court’s future rulings on settlement matters
- ‘the architecture of the Zionist rural settlement was shaped not only by methods of agricultural production and in response to its socio-ideological organisation, but also according to tactical considerations dictated by a militarised logic
- The ‘kibbutz’ form of community (cooperative settlement sharing the means of production) valuable for Israel, playing a valuable part in the 1948 war. Allon: “no less value than a military unit, and may even surpass it”
- Settlements organised according to military strategy (e.g. houses in close proximity to each other, roads forming star shapes to maximise flanking fire, etc.)
- Settlements of civilians are allegedly trained and armed to repel attacks and aid the IDF
- Israeli case law dictates that ‘permanent settlement’ is a relative term - deeds are valid for the standard 49 years, but it is emphasised that they hold as long as the IDF maintain a presence on the ground. The persistence of violence ‘justifies’ the settlements’ continued existence. Absolute security for the state of Israel is therefore not the final goal, as that would remove said justification
- ‘Temporary’ also exists in the context of a conflict which always seems on the edge of ‘resolution’ as a result of the international initiatives to resolve the conflict
- Adi Ophir and Ariella Azoulay claim that ‘the entire logic of military rule in the West Bank and Gaza relies on the principle of ‘temporariness’, an that is is the very definition of the ‘temporariness’ of the state of conflict that allows it to continue indefinitely’
- The use of the term ‘occupation’ as a transitional phase implies such temporariness, and so its use may be complicit in maintaining this military control on which the system of settlement rests
- Justice Vitkon of the HCJ ruled that ‘security’ and ‘defence’ were different terminology, and only ‘security’ was a valid excuse for the establishment of a settlement (‘defense’ being pre-emptive building of fortifications and borders, with a clear distinction between ‘inside’ and ‘outside’ the state; ‘security’ making the assumption that some sort of subversive threat already exists within the state); security remained a legitimate consideration for the HCJ as they believed the Palestinian ‘problem’ to be more urgent than the threat posed by external Arab states
- Use of Palestinian land often went unchallenged because the landowners did not have the means to appeal to the HCJ
    

**Settlements: Optical Urbanism**

- 1979 Elon Moreh Case: Settlers criticised the ‘security’ defence of building settlements and the HCJ had no choice but to strike down the whole concept
- Any land that Palestinians could not prove was a) privately owned or b) actively in use, was land that Israel declared ‘state land’ which could then be leased to Israeli and Jewish settlers
- If a farmer failed to cultivate land for three years, under 1858 Ottoman Land Law, the sovereign was able to take control of the land - Israel seized this land and as such many settlements lay above a certain elevation (because this land was difficult to cultivate)
- Some orchards became Palestinian enclaves within Israeli settlements, but after the Second Intifada (2000) those farmers were no longer able to enter settlements and so were unable to cultivate the land for three years, rendering it ‘state land’
- Dayan: “people still had good memories of German colonial rule before WW1… Israel… should follow the example of benevolent colonialism”
- Under Dayan, Israel actively encouraged Palestinian agriculture, increasing agricultural productivity - this became counterproductive to Israel’s settlement aims in 1979 and the program was halted
- Despite legal barriers to building on disputed land, Israeli settlers have continued to do so
- JNF (Jewish National Fund) planted trees around Jerusalem in a ‘green belt’ in order to prevent Palestinian planting (and therefore cultivation); not only this, but they deliberately chose pine trees (the needles of which are acidic and prevent vegetation from growing in the subsoi) in order to prevent Palestinian shepherds from using the land in future
- Immigration to settlements in the West Bank has been encouraged by both economic and ideological incentives (free housing in low demand areas for example). As a result there has been a significant increase in emigration from urban centres, allowing Israel to consolidate their control through sheer population alone
- ‘Community settlements’ were conceived in the 1980s as a new type of settlement. Essentially, they are private villages, only open to members who have been vetted. All residents must conform to community regulations (i.e. social, religious, and ideological rules) and can be expelled from the village if they do not. This avoids the possibility of Palestinian citizens of Israel making their homes in these settlements
- Ownership of state land was often transferred to private (and US-based) entities which claim to represent ‘wild Jewry’, are funded by non-Israeli Jews, and get tax exemptions under US charity law; thereby the state could circumvent their own legal system - these entities then transfer cooperative ownership to settlers once the community has been established.
- Certain architectural features (red roofs for example) make it clear, even from a distance, that a particular settlement is Jewish/Israeli - this serves both as an orientation point and as a security function: the military are able to identify Israeli settlements from the ground and the air
- Living rooms of Israeli houses oriented toward the view of the surrounding area, and sleeping quarters inwards toward shared public space; the latter promotes an ‘unconscious policing’ on acceptable community behaviour and communal coherence, whilst the former echoes the security function of the settlements: the civilian population is enlisted as a mass monitoring device of Palestinians, able to inform the authorities of any ‘suspicious’ activity
- The IDF has standing orders (dating back to 2003) to shoot to kill any Palestinian caught observing settlements in any ‘suspicious’ manner (e.g. with binoculars’)
- Israeli settlements are ubiquitous in Palestine - it is nigh impossible to look from a Palestinian house and not see the red roofs of a settlement
- Ideological motivations are not the primary concern for architects and residents - rather, the ‘security’ excuse is a useful one to use when wanting to expand an architect’s project, and immigrants are drawn to the settlements due to high living standards or being closer to nature, rather than with the sole purpose of being part of the Israeli security apparatus
    

**Checkpoints**

- Article X of Oslo 1: defined the bureaucracy through which Palestinians could cross borders. By all appearances for those travelling, the terminals are Palestinian-run, with Palestinian insignia throughout and seemingly run by Palestinian officials. In reality, Israeli officers sit behind a one-way mirror and check Palestinian documents; they are able to emerge from this in an ‘emergency situation’ (this ‘exception’ was made permanent throughout the second intifada) and as a ‘last resort’ are permitted to use their firearms. Israel has the final say on regulating movement through the Occupied Territories
- The PA (Palestinian Authority) has relieved Israel of its international obligations as an occupying force by taking over day-to-day governance of the enclaves which they ‘control’
- Oslo sought to replace ‘occupation’ with ‘management’
- Checkpoints were regularly shut on Jewish and Muslim festivals, at times of higher ‘risk’, or during international events (this will have changed since 2023)
- Israeli border officers randomise their tactics to make every border crossing uncertain (e.g. detaining every 9th person, every man called Mohammed being detained on one day, forcing travellers to wait for no reason, and closing the border without cause)
- Checkpoints are so ubiquitous that Israel is referred to as the ‘owner of checkpoints’, Palestine as the ‘land of checkpoints’, etc.
- The ‘checkpoint economy’ whereby vendors sell goods and food to Palestinian travellers has become ‘almost the only functioning Palestinian public space’
- US complicity in the occupation as a result of Zionist congress - in 2005, $200 million USD was intended for Palestinian aid - $50m went towards the construction of new ‘closure’ checkpoints
- Spiegel report, advocating for more ‘humanitarian’ treatment of checkpoint crossers - may have briefly demilitarised checkpoints in 2005 (this is now not the case) but increased number of physical barriers throughout the West Bank
- Turnstiles reduced from standard Israeli government size, designed to press against Palestinians - small details which are key infrastructure
- ‘Humanitarian’ rhetoric of the occupation part of an attempt to normalise it (changed?) - Israel seeks to ‘improve itself’ (viz. its international image) by undertaking these ‘humanitarian reforms’
- Israel able to keep the Rafah crossing closed most of the time by preventing European observers from accessing the control room and then closing the border, forcing the Egyptians to close their side too
- 4th Geneva Convention Article 55 requires that an occupying force ensures the food and medical supplies of the populations it subjugates - Israel wants to absolve itself of this responsibility so as to reduce the cost of occupation, whilst simultaneously maintaining security control; the PA assuming a level of autonomy allows Israel to dominate them with their ‘security concerns’
- Many costs of the humanitarian crisis created by Israel are put onto the international community - Israel do not have to take the financial burden of supporting Palestinians; and they know that they can indiscriminately bomb Gaza and the international community will oblige in picking up the pieces
- Israel must keep the situation in Gaza from ‘total collapse’ to prevent UN peacekeeping involvement of international outcry (now too late!!)
- Perhaps, then, the abolition of the PA would be in the Palestinian interest to force Israel’s hand in recognising its obligations under international law/ending the occupation
- The architecture an Israeli control of the checkpoints is clear to Palestinians; there is no illusion to anyone but the international community

**The Wall**

- Wall’s lack of aesthetic considered by some to be the main reason for international outcry
- Has been characterised as a ‘political seismograph gone mad’; the wall’s geography has been dictated by various groups throughout its construction (e.g. Palestinian farmers’ associations, Israeli real estate developers, settler lobbies, human rights groups, etc.)
- Israeli public opinion overwhelmingly in favour of the ideology and politics of the wall, although different parts of the political spectrum would like to see the wall in different places (the closer to the green line, the more left leaning the proposal
- Opposition to the wall was largely from settlers who viewed it as a restriction on their expansion ambitions - they have radicalised the project to make it more invasive
- Israel has claimed that the wall is designed so as to ‘dominate’ the surrounding area, allowing armed patrols to control the high ground; also helps to hide military operations behind the wall
- Do more research into HCJ. Seem to be quite law-abiding so to speak
- Often the wall would cut through Palestinian villages in the interest of ‘security’, cutting residents off from public facilities
- Regime behind the construction of the wall has been somewhat legitimised by the HCJ - 'improvements' suggested to alleviate the suffering of Palestinians make the regime look better in the eyes of international onlookers
- Counter-petitioners have managed to appeal to the HCJ to divert the wall in their interests, against the interests of Palestinians whom the HCJ had previously ruled to protect
- 'Depth barriers' created to enclose Israeli settlements in Palestinian space - disconnected from the main wall and so largely free from international criticism. The areas encircled by these barriers have been declared by the IDF as 'special security zones' and the surrounding area described as 'sterile', giving the IDF a pretext to shoot-to-kill Palestinians who wander into this area
- The wall is seen by many international onlookers as a defined 'border'; it is actually a perpetually shifting frontier
- Highways and roads under Israeli control but built above Palestinian land - Tirza argued that this would reduce the 'friction' between settlers and Palestinians, significantly reducing the chance of an 'encounter' with one another

**Urban Warfare**

- Operational Theory Research Institute (OTRI) established as a think-tank to reconceptualise responses to 'low-intensity' wars in the occupied territories - US confirmed collaboration with OTRI
- Conflict since the second Intifada has had a distinctly urban aspect to it - targets of both the IDF and Palestinian resistance movements were the cities of the other
- West Bank has become a laboratory in urban warfare
- Urban warfare strategy cannot be centralised or planned - it is based on probability, chance, and opportunity. Civilians become fighters, and fighters can become civilians. 'Swarm' tactics are often used, replacing orders from a central command with communication between individual 'autarkic' units. This allows units to respond to emergent situations more quickly and counteract unpredictable warfare
- Military capabilities adapt to techniques used by guerrilla fighters, who respond in turn to the advancements made by the military
- Diffusion of Palestinian resistance forces minimises the effect that Israeli attacks can have
- 'Walking through walls' became the primary method of urban warfare: IDF soldiers would break through walls of settlements rather than using the conventional methods of moving around an urban area (doors, streets, etc.) in order to a) maintain the element of surprise on targets, and b) to prevent guerrilla fighters from anticipating troop movements. Using this method, a group of soldiers gathers next to a wall, blows it up, and takes the often unsuspecting Palestinian civilians hostage in their own house without food, water, or sanitation, often for several days until the operation is concluded. Soldiers never have to leave buildings to get to their target
- UNRWA widened Jenin camp's roads to prevent houses being demolished in the event of another Israeli attack, but ended up allowing tanks into the camp more easily and reduced the amount of alleys and side streets for resistance fighters to hide in and move through
- Attacks on poorly armed resistance fighters and terrified civilians are characterised by the IDF and government as 'battles'- this is not the case when the 'battle' is incredibly one-sided

**Evacuations**

- Evacuated settlements were offered back to Palestinians for a price - this was rejected by the PA in favour of the removal of the settlements (a move that was interpreted by Israel as a double bluff). The proposal was that the majority of the land be redeveloped for agriculture
- Architecture was widely understood to be one of the instruments of Israeli occupation, and as such Palestinians would not live in Gaza settlements in which their oppressors once lived
- Proposals were made to build high-rise (and therefore permanent) housing for refugees. The removal of the temporary camps in favour of permanent structures raised the concern that it would eliminate the urgency of the Palestinian claim to the land. Palestinian political organisations wanted to maintain the sense of temporariness and kept infrastructure to a bare minimum to reinforce this
- The 'refugee problem' was seen by Israeli politicians as central and a precondition to the conflict, with the belief that many problems could be solved by turning refugees into city or village dwellers - this reduced political problems into socio-economic/urban ones. Dayan believed that if Palestinians were to move out of refugee camps, then they might forget their claim to their land and form a new attachment to their new 'home'

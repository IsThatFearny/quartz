### Terminology

- The endonym for the assassins was the *nizari isma’ilis* or *nizariyya*
- Exonyms included *batinis, batiniyya* (people of the hidden knowledge) or *kuffar* and *malahida* (infidels, deviations, heretics)
- The origin of the word ‘assassin’ is the term for the assassins used by those in Syria, *hashishiyyun* (I.e. those who consume hashish, as assassins were believed to be under the influence of hashish whilst carrying out assassinations)


</br>

### Origins of the Nizari Isma’ili

When the Fatimid caliph, al-Mustansir, died in 1094, his vizier and various generals declared his younger son al-Mustali caliph, instead of the eldest son Abu Mansur  Nizar. Nizar’s supporters separated from the fatimids and establish the Nizari Isma’ili sect of Shi’ism in Alaum, Iran. They were under the leadership of Hasan-I Sabbah:

**Hassan-I Sabbah**

- Born in the mid 1050s in Iran to a Twelver Shiite family
- Converted to Isma’ilism and subsequently becomes a *da’i* (he who summons), a religious missionary, in 1071-72.
- Visited Egypt from 1078-81
- From 1081-1090 he was responsible for propagating Isma’ilism on behalf of the Fatimids in Iran

Hassan-I Sabbah took control of a mountain fortress at Alamut in 1090, which becomes a centre against the Seljuq Sunni establishment. In 1092, the Seljuq vizier Nizam al-Mulk was murdered, perhaps the first ‘assassination’. Alamut supported Nizar when the schism in the family occurred and broke off relations with the Fatimids entirely.

Hassan-I Sabbah wished to impose his own version of Shiism on the Seljuq empire, writing (in Persian) about *da’wa jadida* (a new message) with a focus on the teaching of the Isma’ili Imam - only the Imam can possess knowledge of God. Hassan-I Sabbah was criticised as a heretic by some, but praised by others for his religious knowledge and piety.

</br>

### The Assassinations

- c. 50 assassinations reported from 1095-1124, although after Hassan’s death a few more took place
- The victims tended to be high-profile individuals, such as government officials, military commanders, judges, etc.
- The assassins usually killed in public places such as Mosques, working in pairs and using daggers
- The assassins did not seek martyrdom, but were ready to die for the cause

</br>

### Seljuqs and the Nizari Isma’ilis

In 1118, Great Seljuq troops laid siege to Alamut (largely rumoured to be impenetrable). After the death of Hassan-I Sabbah in 1124, the Nizari Isma’ilis ended their revolt against the Seljuqs. The Seljuqs ultimately failed to take the Nixari fortresses and did not undertake any large scale offensives afterwards.

</br>

### Abolition of Shari’a Law

The 3rd successor of Hassan-I Sabbah, Hassan II, declared in 1164 that he is not simply a *da’i* but also the *khalifa* (successor) and therefore the deputy of the hidden imam. He proclaimed a new doctrine of *qiyama* (resurrection) and shari’a law is abolished.

Hasan II was murdered in 1166, being succeeded by his son Muhammad II who continued his doctrine. Hasan III succeeded Muhammad, and publicly condemned the doctrine, ordering all Nizari Isma’ilis to accept Sunni shari’a law.

</br>

### The Nizari Isma’ilis in Syria

Rashid al-Din Sinan, also know as ‘The Old Man of the Mountain’ was born to a Shiite family near Basra, converting to Nizari Isma’ilism whilst young and staying in Alamut. The Nizari Isma’ilis infiltrated Syria from 1100 onwards, having little success in major cities such as Damascus and Aleppo, and thus withdrew to the mountainous regions of northern Syria. Rashid al-Din Sinan was sent by Hasan II to Masyaf in 1162, and in 1169 Rashid became the leader of the Syrian Nizari Isma’ilis.

</br>
### Saladin

Nur al-Din sent expeditions to the weakened Fatimid caliphate, led by a Kurdi general Shirkuh, who was replaced after his death by his nephew Saladin. Saladin abolished the Isma’ili Fatimid caliphate, restoring Egypt to Sunni Islam in 1171. He won the battle of Hartin in 1187 against the crusaders, with Acre becoming the new crusader state capital. In response to the fall of Jerusalem, the third crusade was begun, but Richard the Lionheart failed to retake Jerusalem and thus left the holy land.

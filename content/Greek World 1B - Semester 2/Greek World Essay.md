# Greek World Essay

In 399 BCE, Socrates stood trial before a jury of 500 to defend himself against charges of 'impiety' and 'corrupting the youth' which had been brought against him. It was argued by the prosecutor, Meletus, that Socrates did not believe in the traditional Athenian gods, and that he was thus indoctrinating the young people of Athens with his teachings.[^1] In much scholarship, these charges are interpreted as charges of 'atheism'. However, whether Socrates was an atheist (in the modern sense) is a matter of much debate: there is evidence to suggest that Socrates simply redefined Greek religion and divinity, rather than refuting the concept of religion in its entirety. This is the argument that this essay shall make: Socrates was not an atheist. A monotheist perhaps, 'impious' by Athenian standards maybe, but he certainly believed in the supernatural and divine powers, even if he did not attribute these powers to traditional Greek polytheistic views. Our evidence for Socrates' piety derives from three main sources: the *Apology of Socrates* by Plato, Socrates' student; Xenophon's *Memorabilia*; and Plato's *Euthyphro*. It is these sources that this essay shall analyse in proving Socrates' piety.

Before this argument can be set forth, two clarifications are necessary. Firstly, 'atheism' is somewhat of an anachronistic term when applied to Socrates and his contemporaries. Religion was so pervasive in the ancient world that there was no atheist movement, but rather those individuals who questioned the nature of the gods, and even these 'disbelievers' would never put their philosophies into practise.[^2] Bremmer terms this 'soft atheism', as opposed to the modern conception of the word, i.e. someone who entirely discredits the concept of deities.[^3] However, no matter whether one uses the modern, anachronistic term or the classical conception of atheism, neither applies to Socrates.

Latterly, the conception of Socrates as an atheist stems largely from the satirist Aristophanes' *Clouds*, in which Socrates is portrayed as 'contemplating the sun' and 'walking on air', and his character often discusses natural forces which would typically ascribe to the gods along scientific lines, contrary to traditional Hellenistic theology.[^4] Aristophanes' Socrates is interested in cosmology, natural science, physics, and the like, and this interest is used to portray him as a committed atheist. Indeed, Plato's Socrates in the *Apology* directly refers to *Clouds* as one of the sources of the rumours which have caused him to be charged with impiety.[^5] However, not only is *Clouds* quite clearly a work of satire, in which the character of Socrates is comprised of caricatures of various other philosophers, what little attempt at honesty Aristophanes may have tried to include is entirely misguided: Socrates was not an atheist as he is portrayed.[^6] The three texts by Plato and Xenophon provide a very different narrative.

Plato's *Apology* is a dialogue which re-tells the trial of Socrates for the aforementioned charges. Throughout the work, Socrates regularly refers to various deities explicitly, citing his belief in them. For example, he uses the testimony of the oracle at Delphi as evidence against the charges.[^7] The oracle, of course, was believed to be the earthly mouthpiece of Apollo, and therefore by Socrates' invocation of the oracle, he also affirms his belief in the word of Apollo. He goes on to explain that the cause of his actions was the command of Apollo. Socrates' piety thus extended at the very least to belief in the credibility of the oracle, and therefore the term 'atheist' is inaccurate in describing him. Elsewhere in the Apology, he exclaims 'by Hera!'[^8] and invokes the name of Zeus various times.[^9] Further to this, Socrates' makes a strong defence against the charge of impiety: he argues that he believes in the supernatural (in his words, 'spirits'), and says that, because Athenian custom is to believe that spirits are either Gods or the children of Gods (which Meletus concedes is true), Socrates must therefore believe in Gods.[^10] Socrates use of these names to strengthen his argument strongly suggest that he believes in the Gods to which he makes reference, and his subsequent appeal to his belief in 'spirits' which Meletus allows to be belief in 'Gods' establishes his position as a religious man. Even if he invokes the names of Gods purely for the sake of theatrics, his belief in spirits still rules out the use of the term 'atheist' when describing the nature of his religious beliefs. 

Xenophon's *Memorabilia* also provides a good account of Socrates' personal adherence to religion. The first two chapters of the text are a defence of Socrates from the charges against him, and the first chapter specifically deals with the charge of impiety and introducing new divinities. Xenophon claims that Socrates' "visibly sacrificed at home and often at the common altars of the city" and that he could be seen using divination.[^11] This is not sufficient evidence in and of itself, as Socrates' could be argued to have been keeping up appearances. However, Xenophon also references Socrates' *daimonion* (δαιμόνιον, 'divine sign'), which was a major part of Socrates' philosophical pursuits.[^12] Socrates claimed that this sign was a gift from the Gods (whether these were the Athenian gods is irrelevant), an 'inner voice' warning him against certain things and aiding him in his study of philosophy.[^13] He makes reference to this during his trial and cites it as evidence that he believes in deities and the supernatural, and is thus not an atheist. Xenophon makes the argument that many of Socrates' teachings "appeared to him as though from a God", and that his followers benefitted from these things, whilst those who ignored him tended to regret it. Socrates insistence that he had the gift of divine knowledge strongly supports the argument that he was no atheist, but was instead deeply religious and spiritual. Xenophon's account of Socratic piety is thus thoroughly convincing, providing substantial evidence for Socrates' devotion to the divine.

Plato's *Euthyphro* is the dialogue in which he attempts to provide the correct account of piety in relation to the gods, and within it one can infer many things about Socrates' religious experience and devotion. For example, Socrates focuses largely on pious acts, and argues that pious acts are acts that please the gods.[^14] Within this conclusion, there lies the premise that the gods exist (for if they don't, then the conclusion that pious acts are those which please the gods is based on a non sequitur fallacy). Thus, one can argue that Socrates' assumption that the gods exist is evidence for his belief in them. Similarly, Socrates develops the premise that piety is a service which assists the gods in fulfilling their *ergon* (piety is thus the *arete* of the gods' *ergon*).[^15] This premise represents a bona fide Socratic belief in the divine, contradicting any claims of his alleged atheism.[^16] Throughout the text, Euthyphro himself refuses to remove the gods from his world, whilst still acknowledging the place of reason and logic (the preserve of Socrates' philosophy).[^17] Socrates makes no objection to this and makes no attempt to disprove Euthyphro's belief. This should be interpreted as Socrates' acceptance of the existence of deities, again proving him to not be an atheist. The *Euthyphro* (and by extension, Socrates) makes the assumption that the gods exist in arguing for certain conclusions, and therefore Socrates' atheism is once again disproved.

It should be noted here that the accounts of Plato and Xenophon differ slightly: Plato's *Apology* and *Euthyphro* utilise an embellished form of Socrates, which is not necessarily an accurate characterisation of the real Socrates, but is instead intended to provide an accurate account of his philosophical method; Xenophon on the other hand attempts to give a more biographical account of Socrates, which is generally considered to be closer to the reality of Socrates' personality and beliefs.[^18] However, no matter which Socrates one analyses, the same conclusion will be reached: Socrates' atheism is an unfounded claim which has substantial evidence against it.

Despite Aristophanes' painting a vivid picture of Socrates as a devoted atheist and natural scientist, the works of Plato and Xenophon convey an entirely different character. Socrates' various references to the gods throughout the *Apology* and his belief in spirits (and therefore gods), his public and private religiosity as well as his *daimonion* as attested to by Xenophon, and the premises required for his conclusions in Plato's *Euthyphro* all point to the same conclusion. Socrates was not an atheist by any means. There is evidence for his introduction of alternative deities not of traditional Athenian theology, but there is none that convincingly portrays him as an atheist. Socrates was a religious and pious man, whether that be in Plato's dialogues or the biographical writings of Xenophon. Thus, we may conclude that Socrates was not an atheist in any sense of the word.

**Was Socrates an Atheist?**

- Whilst perhaps did not adhere to traditional Athenian religion, Socrates had a believe in the divine and in the supernatural, and therefore was not an 'atheist' in the modern sense
- Socrates makes assumptions in the Euthyphro that the Gods exist in order to discuss the matter of piety
- Are we discussing the historical Socrates or the Platonic conception of Socrates?

In 399 BCE, Socrates stood trial before a jury of 500 to defend himself against charges of 'impiety' and 'corrupting the youth' which had been brought against him. It was argued by the prosecutor, Meletus, that Socrates did not believe in the traditional Athenian gods, and that he was thus indoctrinating the young people of Athens with his teachings. In much scholarship, these charges are intepreted as charges of 'Atheism'. However, whether Socrates was an Atheist (in the modern sense) is a matter of much debate: there is evidence to suggest that Socrates simply redefined Greek religion and divinity, rather than refuting the concept of religion in its entirety. This is the argument that this essay shall make: Socrates was not an Atheist. A monotheist perhaps, 'impious' by Athenian standards maybe, but he certainly believed in the supernatural and divine powers, even if he did not attribute these powers to traditional Greek polytheistic views. Our evidence for Socrates' piety derives from three main sources: the *Apology* of Plato, Socrates' student; Xenophon's *Memoribilia*; and Socrates' own work, the *Euthyphro*. It is these sources that this essay shall analyse in proving Socrates' piety.

Plato's *Apology* is a dialogue which re-tells the trial of Socrates for the aforementioned charges. Throughout the work, Socrates regularly refers to various deities explicitly.

# Greek World Essay

In 399 BCE, Socrates stood trial before a jury of 500 to defend himself against charges of 'impiety' and 'corrupting the youth' which had been brought against him. It was argued by the prosecutor, Meletus, that Socrates did not believe in the traditional Athenian gods, and that he was thus indoctrinating the young people of Athens with his teachings. In much scholarship, these charges are intepreted as charges of 'Atheism'. However, whether Socrates was an Atheist (in the modern sense) is a matter of much debate: there is evidence to suggest that Socrates simply redefined Greek religion and divinity, rather than refuting the concept of religion in its entirety. This is the argument that this essay shall make: Socrates was not an Atheist. A monotheist perhaps, 'impious' by Athenian standards maybe, but he certainly believed in the supernatural and divine powers, even if he did not attribute these powers to traditional Greek polytheistic views. Our evidence for Socrates' piety derives from three main sources: the *Apology* of Plato, Socrates' student; Xenophon's *Memorabilia*; and Socrates' own work, the *Euthyphro*. It is these sources that this essay shall analyse in proving Socrates' piety.

Plato's *Apology* is a dialogue which re-tells the trial of Socrates for the aforementioned charges. Throughout the work, Socrates regularly refers to various deities explicitly, citing his belief in them. For example, he uses the testimony of the oracle at Delphi as evidence against the charges.[^1] The oracle, of course, was believed to be the earthly mouthpiece of Apollo, and therefore by Socrates' invocation of the oracle, he also affirms his belief in the word of Apollo. He goes on to explain that the cause of his actions was the command of Apollo. Socrates' piety thus extended at the very least to belief in the legitimacy of the oracle, and therefore the term 'atheist' is inaccurate in describing him. Elsewhere in the Apology, he exclaims 'by Hera!'[^2] and invokes the name of Zeus various times.[^3] Further to this, Socrates' makes a strong defence against the charge of impiety: he argues that he believes in the supernatural (in his words, 'spirits'), and says that, because Athenian custom is to believe that spirits are either Gods or the children of Gods (which Meletus concedes is true), Socrates must therefore believe in Gods.[^4] Socrates use of these names to strengthen his argument strongly suggest that he believes in the Gods to which he makes reference, and his subsequent appeal to his belief in 'spirits' which Meletus allows to be belief in 'Gods' establishes his position as a religious man. Even if he invokes the names of Gods purely for the sake of theatrics, his belief in spirits still rules out the use of the term 'Atheist' when describing the nature of his religious beliefs. 

Xenophon's *Memorabilia* also provides a good account of Socrates' personal adherence to religion. The first two chapters of the text are a defence of Socrates from the charges against him, and the first chapter specifically deals with the charge of impiety and introducing new divinities. Xenophon claims that Socrates' "visibly sacrificed at home and often at the common altars of the city" and that he could be seen using divination.[^5] This is not sufficient evidence in of itself, as Socrates' could be argued to have been keeping up appearances. However, Xenophon also references Socrates' *daimonion* (δαιμόνιον, 'divine sign'), which was a major part of Socrates' philosophical pursuits.[^6] Socrates claimed that this sign was a gift from the Gods (whether these were the Athenian gods is irrelevant), an 'inner voice' warning him against certain things and aiding him in his study of philosophy.[^7] He makes reference to this during his trial and cites it as evidence that he believes in deities and the supernatural, and is thus not an Atheist. Xenophon makes the argument that many of Socrates' teachings "appeared to him as though from a God", and that his followers benefitted from these things, whilst those who ignored him tended to regret it. Socrates insistence that he had the gift of divine knowledge strongly supports the argument that he was no Atheist, but was instead deeply religious and spiritual. Xenophon's account of Socratic piety is thus thoroughly convincing, providing substantial evidence for Socrates' devotion to the divine.

Socrates' *Euthyphro* is the dialogue in which he attempts to provide the correct account of piety in relation to the gods, and within it one can infer many things about Socrates' religious experience and devotion. For example, Socrates focuses largely on pious acts, and argues that pious acts are acts that please the gods.[^8] Within this conclusion, there lies the premise that the gods exist (for if they don't, then the conclusion that pious acts are those which please the gods is based on a non sequitur fallacy). Thus, one can argue that Socrates' assumption that the gods exist is evidence for his belief in them. Similarly, Socrates develops the premise that piety is a service which assists the gods in fulfilling their *ergon* (piety is thus the *arete* of the gods' *ergon*).[^9] This premise represents a bona fide Socratic belief in the divine, contradicting any claims of his alleged atheism.[^10]

### What was a slave?


> [!quote] Homer, *Odyssey* 17.320-324
> "Slaves, when their masters lose their power, are no longer minded thereafter to do honest service: for Zeus, whose voice is borne afar, takes away half his worth from a man, when the day of slavery comes upon him"

Slaves were considered to be 'half a man' from the moment of the enslavement, even if their master were to die or lose control over the them. They would have slave status permanently.

</br>

> [!quote] Aristotle, *Politics* 1253b
> "The manager of a household must have his tools, and of tools some are lifeless and others living...; so also an article of property is a tool for the purpose of life, and property generally is a collection of tools, and a slave is a live article of property"

Slaves were a 'living tool' which a master could use at their will, even if one was not owned by another. Slaves were dehumanised and thus it was socially acceptable to use them in this way.

</br>

### Who was a slave?

The basic definition of a slave which Greek authors would largely adhere to was either: those who become slaves by conquest (i.e. those enslaved after the territory in which they lived was captured by a hostile force); or those barbarians who were enslaved and subsequently sold to slave owners

There were also occasions of enslavement of regular citizens as punishment:


> [!quote] Strabo 6.3.2, quoting Antiochus of Syracuse
> "After the Messenian war broke out, those of the Lacedaemonians who did not take part in the expedition were adjudged slaves and were named Helots, and all children who were born in the time of the expedition were called "Children of Virgins" and judicially deprived of the rights of citizenship"

</br>

**How many slaves?**


> [!quote] Ktesikles BNJ 245 frg. 1
> "Ktesikles in the third book of his chronicles states that a census of the Attic population was conducted in Athens by Demetrios of Phaleron during the 11[...]th Olympiad, counting 21,000 Athenian citizens, 10,000 metics, and **400,000 slaves** 

This is an embellishment, but speaks to the large amount of slaves that were present in Athens compared to those with citizenship. In any case, we can be sure that there was a very large contingent of slaves in Greece. A better estimate may be around 50,000-100,000 in Athens alone, although of course there is still a sizeable margin of error.

</br>

### Who owned slaves?

Slave owners, of course, tended to be those well-off enough to own them. Often, entrepreneurs owned many slaves in order to maintain a strong workforce that was cheap to maintain. Alternatively, certain slave owners are known to bought slaves on a large scale and then rented them out to those who required their labour. Plato imagines a man who has fifty or more slaves when giving an example in his *Republic*, implying that this is an entirely realistic scenario. 

Those who were poor were also able to own slaves - in Aristophanes' *Wealth*, a poor man complains to his 'most trustworthy' slave of his financial situation, implying that the man owns *multiple* slaves, despite his alleged poverty.

The terms of the Helots' enslavement was slightly different: it was not permitted to sell them outside of the country, nor to set them free. The state had control over the Helots to an extent not experienced by privately owned slaves, assigning them to specific settlements and giving them specific tasks to perform. Some Helots were privately owned, but most were state-owned. The Helots were considered inferior above all else - a 'rite of passage' for young men was to go by night and kill Helots travelling along highways, or to go into fields where Helots were working and slaughter them.

</br>

### What did slaves do?

Slaves were employed for a variety of purposes: they could be used in enterprise (such as in the mines or fields); used as simple servants for their masters; used as entertainment for noble audiences; etc. They were also used in construction (e.g. slaves were used in the construction of buildings at the Acropolis). Often they cost money to use, but this money would go to their masters instead of the slaves themselves

</br>

### The treatment of slaves

In Athens, physical violence against slaves was not permitted. However, this was not for the sake of the slaves, but for the sake of the citizens - one did not want to strike a free man under the illusion that he was a slave. However, this does show that the slaves were well treated:


> [!quote] Xenophon, *Athenian Constitution* 1.10
> "For the people there are no better dressed than the slaves and metics, nor are they any more handsome”


</br>

Likewise, there is evidence that slaves were not unequivocally treated with violence:


> [!quote] Lysias 1.18
>"So it is open to you,’ I said, ‘to choose as you please between two things,—either to be whipped and thrown into a mill, and to be irrevocably immersed in that sort of misery, or else to speak out the whole truth and, instead of suffering any harm, obtain my pardon for your transgressions. Tell no lies, but speak the whole truth'"

</br>

However, of course, slaves were punished for transgressions as one might expect, largely through lashings. They were also often sexually exploited, and whilst the rape of a slave would carry a fine of five drachmas, this was significantly less (approx. 100 times less) than the fine for the rape of a free man/woman, which was 100 staters.

</br>

### Slave emancipation

Slaves could be privately freed by their masters (although proper documentation was needed). Likewise, a slave could be freed in public, which would annul the need for documentation and allow that man to be considered free by all.

In wartime, slaves could also be freed if they were useful in the war effort. This largely served to motivate slaves into doing difficult tasks which free men did not want to do.

Manumission did not give one full citizenship of a region, but rather made the freed man a metic (i.e. a permanent resident of that area).

</br>

### Alternatives to slavery

The abolition of slavery was not an option for the Greeks - Aristotle and Krates both write that slavery could only be ended if all static implements were made capable of moving.

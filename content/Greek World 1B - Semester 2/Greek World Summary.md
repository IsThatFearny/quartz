# Greek World Summary

### **Lecture 1 Summary: The Origins and Structure of the Athenian Empire**

#### **Main Sources**

This lecture draws upon a range of primary sources:

- _Thucydides_, esp. Book 1 on the _pentekontaetia_ (the fifty-year period between the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars)
    
- _Herodotus_, _Xenophon_, _Aristophanes_
    
- _Athenian Constitution_ (by the School of Aristotle and the 'Old Oligarch')
    
- Inscriptions and tribute lists from Athens
    

#### **Formation of the Athenian Empire**

- Post-Persian War, Athens rebuilt and fortified its city and ports (esp. the _Peiraeus_) under Themistocles, enabling naval dominance.
    
- Spartan retreat from leadership allowed Athens to assume command of the Greek alliance, determining how allied cities contributed to the war effort (money or ships).
    
- Aristides facilitated Ionian cities leaving the Spartan-led alliance to join Athens.
    
- Athens established the _Delian League_ in 478 BC with nominal anti-Persian aims, evolving from an alliance of equals to an imperial force under Athenian control.
    
- The _Hellenotamiai_ managed tribute payments (_phoros_), initially based on Delos but moved to Athens by mid-century.
    

#### **Key Chronology**

- **478 BC** – Delian League founded
    
- **470s–460s BC** – Early military campaigns under Cimon
    
- **461–429 BC** – Periclean consolidation and imperial expansion
    
- **420s BC** – Cleon's leadership during the League's mature phase
    
- **404 BC** – Athens defeated, League dissolved
    

#### **Imperial Administration and Finance**

- Tribute began at ~460 talents; after 454 BC, tribute lists show payments and _aparchai_ (offerings to Athena).
    
- These lists are key evidence of the league's extent—330 allies recorded.
    
- In 413 BC, tribute was replaced with a 5% trade tax.
    
- Tax collectors enforced compliance, including punitive measures for shortfalls.
    

#### **Relations with Allies**

- Athens varied in its treatment of allies—some were treated leniently (e.g., legal rights for Phaselites), others more harshly (e.g., Chalkis swearing sole allegiance to Athens).
    
- Councillors in allied cities swore loyalty to Athens, solidifying its political dominance.
    

#### **Cleruchies**

- _Cleruchies_ were Athenian settlements on allied or conquered land.
    
- Unlike colonies, cleruchs retained Athenian citizenship and lived under Athenian laws.
    
- Lands for cleruchies were taken from local populations, reinforcing imperial control.

### **Lecture 2 Summary: The Athenian Empire and Democracy**

#### **When Did the Delian League Become an Empire?**

- **Harold Mattingly** argues the shift occurred between **Pericles and Cleon**, with Cleon marking a more overtly imperialist phase.
    
- Language in **Athenian decrees** evolved: from “the alliance” → “Athens and her allies” → “the cities that the Athenians rule.”
    
- The **Three Bar Sigma** theory (used to date inscriptions pre-445 BC) was disproven in 1990, showing that **epigraphy alone is not reliable for dating**.
    
- Many imperialist actions (loyalty oaths, interventions, suppressions) already existed under Pericles, indicating **imperial structures were in place earlier than once thought**.
    
- As Athens became more oppressive, **Sparta gained sympathy** among disaffected allies. Athens could not retreat from empire without backlash.
    

---

### **Athenian Democracy**

#### **Key Sources**

- _Athenian Constitution_ (School of Aristotle)
    
- _Old Oligarch_’s account
    
- Attic orators and inscriptions
    

#### **Development Timeline**

- **594 BC**: Solon’s archonship – beginnings of democratic thought
    
- **510–508 BC**: Cleisthenes’ reforms – foundation of democratic structures
    
- **488 BC**: Introduction of **ostracism** to counter tyranny
    
- **462 BC**: Ephialtes reforms – curbing the oligarchic Areopagus
    
- **403 BC**: Post-civil war reconciliation and legal revision
    

---

#### **Key Democratic Reforms**

- **Cleisthenes**: Created the _Council of 500_, divided Athens into **10 tribes**, each with **3 trittyes** (city/coastal/inland), and **139 demes** (local communities).
    
- **Ostracism**: Democratic safety valve to remove individuals deemed a threat, passed by majority vote.
    
- **Ephialtes**: Diminished the _Areopagus_, moving more power to democratic institutions.
    
- **403 BC Reconciliation**: Temporary government of 20 revised laws using Solon’s and Draco’s codes; aimed at **political stability over revenge**.
    

---

#### **Democratic Procedures and Governance**

- **Prytanies**: Each tribe took turns (36 days each) setting the _agenda_ for the council.
    
- **Officials**: Mostly selected **by allotment**, except for **financial and military positions**, which were elected.
    
- **Logistai, euthynoi, and paredroi**: Oversight officers who **audited and investigated** official conduct.
    

---

#### **Judiciary**

- Solon’s reforms allowed **citizens to prosecute** and **appeal in court**.
    
- **Juries** were selected through an elaborate **lot-drawing system**.
    
- Trials involved **sworn oaths**, **timed speeches**, and **majority verdicts** (with ties favouring defendants).
    
- Penalties were typically **fines** or **loss of freedom**.
    

---

#### **Citizenship Criteria**

To be a citizen (_politēs_) one had to:

- Be **male**, of **two citizen parents**, **free**, **18+**, and **approved by deme members**.
    
- **Astoi** (citizens by birth) included women; **politai** held political rights.

</br>

### **Lecture 3 Summary: The Parthenon and Athenian Power**

#### **Delian League Treasury**

- In **454 BC**, the **Delian League treasury** was moved from **Delos** to **Athens**, located on the **Acropolis**.
    
- This move symbolised the shift from a mutual alliance to Athenian imperial dominance—Athens now directly controlled league finances.
    

---

### **The Parthenon (c. 447–432 BC)**

- A temple **presumed** to be dedicated to **Athena Parthenos**, although there's no concrete evidence for dedications or her altar.
    
- Part of a larger building program under **Pericles**, representing Athenian wealth and imperial prestige.
    

#### **Architecture**

- Designed by **Iktinos** and **Kallikrates**; sculptural work led by **Pheidias**.
    
- Made of **Pentelic marble** (Athenian origin).
    
- A **peripteral** structure (columns surrounding the building); features **peristyle columns**.
    
- Combines **Doric** (main structure) and **Ionic** (Adyton interior) orders—**unusual mix** for the time.
    
- Architecturally refined: **no straight lines**, curved floors and roofs, **inward-leaning columns** for visual harmony.
    

#### **Structure and Layout**

- Main room: **Cella**
    
- Rear room: **Adyton**
    
- Eastern porch: **Pronaos**; Western porch: **Opisthodomos**
    
- Ideal column layout: long side = 2x short side + 1
    

---

### **Parthenon Sculpture and Decoration**

#### **Metope Reliefs (Doric Frieze)**

- Uniquely, **every metope** was sculpted (rare among temples)
    
- **Themes by side**:
    
    - **East**: Athena vs. Giants (Gigantomachy)
        
    - **West**: Greeks vs. Amazons (Amazonomachy)
        
    - **North**: Sack of Troy
        
    - **South**: Centauromachy (Centaurs vs. Lapiths)
        
- South metopes are best preserved due to relative inaccessibility and less Ottoman damage
    

---

### **The Parthenon Frieze**

- A continuous **Ionic frieze** wrapped around the building’s inner exterior
    
- Originally painted and detailed with bronze elements
    
- Approx. **500 ft long**, with ~**20% missing**
    
- Begins at **south-west corner**; progresses from motion to stillness
    
- Early scenes: horsemen, animals in motion (sacrifice procession)
    
- Later scenes: pedestrians, increasing female presence, culminating in ritual stillness
    
- The **east frieze** features:
    
    - Presentation of the **peplos** (cloth) to Athena
        
    - **Gods depicted** seated with backs turned, identifiable by positioning
        
- Artistic principles: **restraint** (_“nothing in excess”_) and **self-awareness** (_“know thyself”_)
    

---

This lecture ties Athens’ architectural and religious achievements to its imperial ambitions. The **Parthenon** reflects **Athenian identity, power, and ideology**, projecting dominance through art and sacred architecture.

</br>

## **Lecture 4 Summary: Parthenon Sculpture & Other Acropolis Monuments**

### **Continuation of the Parthenon Frieze**

- **Athena** is closest to the central scene on the east frieze—**identified by the snake** coiled on her arm, a recurring symbol of her protective aspect.
    

---

### **The Pediments of the Parthenon**

Pediments are the triangular gables on either end of the Parthenon, filled with large-scale sculpture.

#### **West Pediment**:

- **Contest between Athena and Poseidon** to become patron deity of Athens.
    
- **Poseidon**: creates a salt spring.
    
- **Athena**: produces an **olive tree** (the chosen gift).
    
- Depicts **legends from Athens’ past** around the central scene.
    
- **Artemis** delivers the outcome—her figure is one of the best preserved, rendered in **wet drapery style** (thin, clinging fabric that both conceals and reveals the body).
    

#### **East Pediment**:

- **Birth of Athena** from Zeus’ head, split by **Hephaestus**.
    
- Severely damaged:
    
    - In **1687**, during a Venetian attack, a shell hit the building.
        
    - Altered during its conversion into a **church**.
        
- **Aphrodite** shown reclining, with ambiguous female companions.
    
    - Her **wet drapery** emphasises her **sexual allure**: fabric clings tightly at the abdomen and groin, highlighting her role as goddess of desire.
        

---

### **Statue of Athena Parthenos**

- Stood inside the **Parthenon**.
    
- Created by **Pheidias**, made from **ivory and gold** (12.2m tall).
    
- Some gold sourced from **Delian League** reserves—reclaimed in times of financial strain.
    
- **Shield (exterior)**: Battle of Amazons  
    **Shield (interior)**: Gigantomachy  
    **Sandals**: Centauromachy  
    **Base**: Birth of **Pandora** (symbolising the beginning of humanity—and female presence in myth)
    

> Statue no longer survives, but **Roman copies** and **textual sources** help reconstruct it.

---

## **Other Acropolis Monuments**

---

### **The Propylaia** (Gateway to the Acropolis)

- Built **c. 437–432 BC**, replacing an earlier gateway.
    
- **Architectural mix**:
    
    - **Doric**: outer columns
        
    - **Ionic**: interior columns (used to reduce tapering and increase height/space)
        
- Constructed using **Eleusinian limestone**.
    
- Serves both a functional and symbolic purpose as the grand entrance.
    

---

### **Temple of Athena Nike**

- Built **c. 427–424 BC**, south-west of the Propylaia.
    
- **Entirely Ionic**; small **tetrastyle amphiprostyle** temple (4 columns on front and back, not sides).
    
- Friezes on all four sides:
    
    - 3 depict **battles** (Greek vs. Greek and likely **Battle of Marathon** vs. Persians).
        
    - 1 shows the **divine assembly**.
        
- Possibly **revolutionary**: if the warriors depicted were contemporary, it broke tradition by showing **current events** in temple sculpture.
    

---

### **The Erechtheion**

- Located on the **north side** of the Acropolis.
    
- Built **c. 421–406 BC**, construction interrupted by the **Peloponnesian War**.
    
- Dedicated to **multiple deities**:
    
    - **Athena**, **Erechtheus**, **Poseidon**, **Hephaestus**, **Boutes**
        
- **Caryatids** (sculpted female figures) replace traditional columns, standing over the grave of **King Kekrops**.
    
- Asymmetrical plan due to integration of **older sanctuaries and altars**.
    
- **Frieze** present but in **poor condition**, making its subject unclear.
    
- Unique layout reflects both **sacred complexity** and **historical layering**.

</br>

## **Lecture 6 Summary: Theatre and Dionysian Worship in Athens**

### **Origins of Greek Theatre**

- **Thespis** (c. 530 BC): First known actor to step out of the chorus and introduce **dialogue**, transforming monologue-based performance into true drama.
    
- This innovation marked the **birth of theatre** as a distinct art form.
    

---

### **Dionysian Cult and the Theatre**

#### **Dionysos Eleuthereos (Dionysos the Liberator)**

- A temple dedicated to Dionysos was built on the **south slope of the Acropolis**, with a **cult statue** housed within (some remains still visible today).
    
- Associated with **ekstasis**—"standing outside oneself"—a core concept for both **religious experience** and **theatrical performance**.
    

#### **Theatre of Dionysos (First Version)**

- Built near the shrine—**rectangular in shape**, likely wooden construction.
    
- Evidence from inscriptions shows **seats were rented**, pointing to an organised and commercialised viewing experience.
    
- **Maximum of 3 actors** on stage (excluding the **chorus**).
    

---

### **The City Dionysia Festival**

A major **religious and cultural event** in Athens honouring Dionysos, featuring **theatrical competitions**.

- Lasted **4 days**, with **all-male casts** wearing **masks** for different roles.
    
- Events included:
    
    - **Tragic competition**: 3 playwrights each submitted **3 tragedies and 1 satyr play**.
        
        - Only complete surviving set: **Aeschylus’ _Oresteia_**
            
    - **Comedy competition**: 5 comic playwrights each submitted **1 play**.
        
    - **Choral competitions**: _Dithyrambs_ sung by **tribal choruses** (separated into men and boys, 50 per chorus).
        

> The festival served as both **civic celebration** and **dramatic contest**, reinforcing communal identity and values.

---

### **Later Theatre Developments (c. 330 BCE)**

- A **new semi-circular theatre** was built at the base of the Acropolis (seen today in Roman-period ruins).
    
- **Stone seating** replaced wood—made of **limestone**, holding **15,000–20,000** spectators.
    
- **Prohedria**: Front-row, marble seats reserved for **elite spectators**, with **inscriptions** and elaborate decoration.
    
    - Included a **throne for the priest of Dionysos**.
        
- The **skene**: a structure behind the stage functioning as **backstage/dressing area** and **scenic backdrop**.
    

---

### **The Choregos and Civic Patronage**

- Wealthy Athenians were required to perform **liturgies**—public financial contributions.
    
- A **choregos** funded a chorus/play, serving a role akin to a **producer**.
    
- They competed for **prestige** and could commemorate success with **choregic monuments**.
    
    - Example: **Monument of Lysikrates** in Athens.
        

> The _choregos_ system shows how **elite patronage** and **public entertainment** were deeply intertwined in democratic Athens.

</br>

## **Lecture 7 Summary: The City Dionysia in Practice and Greek Tragedy on Stage**

### **City Dionysia: Festival Programme**

Building on Lecture 6, this lecture focuses on the **structure and ritual** of the City Dionysia festival.

#### **Festival Events and Ritual Procession**

- **Proagon**: A preliminary event where **poets, actors, and chorus members introduced their productions**.
    
- A **cult statue of Dionysus** was:
    
    - **Garlanded** and **paraded** from the Acropolis to a sanctuary outside the city.
        
    - Sacrifices were made, and the statue returned to the city **by torchlight**.
        
- **Citywide participation**:
    
    - All public business was suspended.
        
    - Citizens **paraded with sacrificial items**, including the **main bull**.
        
    - **Hymns** were sung.
        
    - A **dithyramb competition** followed, before the main **dramatic contests**.
        

#### **Ritual Preceding the Plays**

1. **Theatre purified** for sacred use.
    
2. **Ten generals** offered **libations**.
    
3. **Public proclamations** were made.
    
4. **Tribute** from Athens' allies was **displayed**.
    
5. **Orphaned sons of war dead** were awarded a **ceremonial suit of armour**.
    
6. **Judges and performance order** chosen by lot.
    

> These rites emphasised the **religious, civic, and political unity** of Athens, reinforcing its identity before the performances began.

---

### **Dramaturgical Conventions**

#### **Performance Structure**

- **2–3 actors**, all **male**, with **masks and costumes**.
    
- **Chorus** of 10–15 members, participating in singing and dancing.
    
- Alternation between:
    
    - **Spoken verse** (usually **iambic trimeter**).
        
    - **Sung passages** by the chorus (sometimes antiphonal).
        

#### **Typical Structure of a Tragedy**

1. **Prologue**
    
2. **Parodos** (entry of the chorus)
    
3. **Episode (Act 1)**
    
4. **Stasimon (Choral Ode 1)**
    
5. Further **Episodes and Odes**
    
6. **Exodus** (final scene)
    

#### **Key Dramatic Techniques**

- **Agon**: formal debate between characters.
    
- **Messenger Speech**: vivid narration of off-stage events (often violent).
    
- **Stichomythia**: rapid-fire **alternating lines** between characters.
    
- **No on-stage violence**—all action happens **off-stage** and is reported.
    
- During odes, the **stage was often empty** of actors.
    

---

### **Stage Design and Machinery**

- **Elevated stage** with a **chorus area (orchestra)** below.
    
- **Skene** building with 1–3 doors and **painted scenery**.
    
- **Ekkuklēma**: a wheeled platform to **reveal off-stage scenes**.
    
- **Mechane**: a crane to raise actors—used to depict **gods (deus ex machina)**.
    
- Some action occurred on the **skene roof** or directly in the **orchestra**.
    

---

### **Aeschylus**

- **Life**: 524–456 BCE
    
- **Fought at Marathon** and other Persian War battles.
    
- First tragic victory in **484 BCE**.
    
- Wrote around **90 plays** in groups of **4 (trilogy + satyr play)**.
    
- Only **7 plays survive**.
    
    - Most famous: **_Oresteia_ trilogy** (the only full trilogy extant).
        

> Aeschylus elevated tragedy with **religious gravitas**, **thematic complexity**, and experimentation with **stagecraft**.

</br>

## **Lecture 9 Summary: Sparta – Reality and Mirage**

### **Sparta’s Aesthetic and the ‘Spartan Mirage’**

- **Lacked monumental architecture**: unlike Athens, Sparta had no grand buildings or temples.
    
    - _Thucydides_ notes that if Athens and Sparta were both deserted, ruins of Athens would exaggerate its true power, while Sparta’s modest appearance would understate its past strength.
        
- **Deliberate policy of secrecy**: Spartan strength was hidden from outsiders.
    
- The **Spartan Mirage** refers to how **Sparta projected strength beyond its reality**:
    
    - Rituals like **combing their hair before battle** created an illusion of calm confidence.
        
- Spartan education (the **agōgē**) shaped elite warriors perceived as **unflinching and fearsome**, reinforcing the mirage.
    
- Despite internal **political tensions**, Sparta maintained **domestic stability** for over **400 years**, enabling interference in foreign affairs.
    

---

### **Spartan Constitution and Lycurgus**

- **Lycurgus**, the mythical lawgiver:
    
    - Said to have received **constitutional reforms from the Oracle at Delphi**.
        
    - Inspired by **Cretan governance**, he reorganised **military and civic institutions**.
        
- Constitution **dates to at least the 7th century BCE**, though precise details are unclear.
    

---

### **Spartan Political Institutions**

|Institution|Description|
|---|---|
|**Kingship**|Two hereditary kings (from **Agiadai** and **Eurypontidai** families), said to descend from Herakles.|
|**Gerousia**|Council of Elders: 28 men aged **>60**, plus 2 kings. Members held seats **for life**.|
|**Apella**|Assembly of **Spartiates** aged **>18**; limited power.|
|**Ephors**|Five officials elected annually, with authority to **oversee the kings** and general public conduct.|

---

### **Social Structure of Sparta**

|Class|Description|
|---|---|
|**Spartiates / Homoioi**|"The Similars" – full citizens and elite warriors.|
|**Perioikoi**|“Dwellers around” – free but **non-citizen** inhabitants of the region. Lacked political rights.|
|**Helots**|Enslaved peoples from **Eurotas valley** and **Messenia**, often outnumbering the Spartiates.|

> **Sparta’s military society** depended on the **exploitation and suppression of helots**, contributing to constant internal tension.

---

### **Spartan Instability and Political Scandals**

Despite its conservative image, **Sparta faced internal turmoil**:

- **Demeratos** (king): exiled in **491 BCE**, fled to Persia.
    
- **Cleomenes**: exiled, recalled, and died **under suspicious circumstances** in 490 BCE.
    
- **Pausanias** (general): accused of **tyranny and Medism** (supporting Persia), died in disgrace.
    
- Similar issues persisted across Spartan history.
    

---

### **Military Insecurity at Home**

- Sparta never felt secure in its own territory, despite its strength:
    
    - Faced constant **conflict in the Peloponnese**:
        
        - Frequent warfare with **Arcadia**, **Argolis**, **Laconia**, and especially **Messenia**.
            
- The fear of **helot revolts** meant that Sparta always had to watch its rear, even while waging war abroad.

</br>

## **Lecture 10 Summary: The Peloponnesian War and Thucydides' Approach**

### **Phases of the Peloponnesian War**

The war is typically divided into three phases:

1. **The Archidamian War (431-421 BCE)**: The first phase of the conflict.
    
2. **Melos and the Sicilian Expedition (416-413 BCE)**: The second phase marked by major campaigns and a shift in Athenian strategy.
    
3. **The Decelean War (413-404 BCE)**: The final phase, culminating in Athens’ defeat.
    

Thucydides divides the history into these three sections, although he acknowledges the continuous nature of the conflict.

---

### **The Method of Thucydides**

- Thucydides admits the difficulty of **exactly reproducing speeches** but tries to capture the essence of what each speaker might have said.
    
- Exiled from Athens, he gained a more **objective perspective** on the war, especially in representing the **Peloponnesian** side.
    
- Thucydides saw his history as a **permanent legacy**, not just a contemporary record.
    
- His narrative follows a **chronological order**, often subdividing events by **seasons** rather than terms of office.
    

---

### **Causes of the War**

Several key incidents triggered the war:

- **Civil conflict in Epidamnos (435 BCE)**: A colony of Corcyra, torn between oligarchs and democrats.
    
    - **Corcyra** sought Athenian help; **Corinth** supported the oligarchs, leading to a clash.
        
- **Athens vs. Corinth (433 BCE)**: Athens demanded that **Potidaea** (a Corinthian colony) tear down its walls, resulting in Potidaea’s revolt.
    
    - Sparta and Corinth supported the rebellion, while Athens besieged the city.
        
- **Athenian embargo on Megara**: Megara, allied with Sparta, was banned from Athenian ports, aggravating tensions.
    

---

### **Spartan Demands**

Sparta made specific demands in 432 BCE:

- Expulsion of the descendants responsible for the **Kylon massacre** (from the 7th century BCE).
    
- Lifting the **siege of Potidaea**.
    
- Restoring **autonomy** to the Aeginetans.
    
- Reversing the **Megarian exclusion** from Athenian ports.
    

Sparta asserted that **no war** would occur if Athens complied with these demands.

---

### **Pericles’ Speeches**

**Pericles** delivered several key speeches during the war:

#### **First Speech (432 BCE)**: The Call to War

- **Athens** must not yield to Sparta, as doing so would lead to further demands.
    
- War is inevitable, and Athens should **fight on its own terms**.
    
- Pericles emphasized Athens’ **financial and naval superiority**, contrasting it with Sparta’s reliance on land-based power.
    

#### **Second Speech (431 BCE)**: The War Strategy

- A **funeral oration** for those who died in the first year of the war, with a focus on **Athenian values**:
    
    - **Merit-based society**, where individuals advance based on ability, not class.
        
    - **Individual freedom**, allowing Athenians to thrive both privately and publicly.
        
    - Despite its relaxed lifestyle, **Athens excelled in war**, and its **superiority made sacrifices worthwhile**.
        

#### **Third Speech (430 BCE)**: The Apology

- The war was going badly for Athens: **plague** and **invasions** of Attica had weakened the city.
    
- Pericles responded to accusations of **foolish leadership**, apologizing to the Athenians and reaffirming that Athens could not afford to abandon its empire.
    

---

### **Key Events in the War’s Progression**

- **428 BCE**: Revolt of **Mytilene**.
    
- **425 BCE**: Battles of **Pylos** and **Sphacteria**, key Athenian victories.
    
- **424-422 BCE**: Focus shifts north, with **Brasidas’ campaign** and the **capture of Amphipolis**.
    
- **422 BCE**: Deaths of **Brasidas** and **Cleon** at the **Second Battle of Amphipolis**.
    
- **421 BCE**: The **Peace of Nicias**, a brief truce between Athens and Sparta.
    

**Cleon** played a major role in the conflict, particularly during the earlier years, until his death in 422 BCE.

</br>

## **Lecture 11 Summary: The Peloponnesian War Continued and the Fall of Athens**

### **Pylos and Sphacteria (425 BCE)**

- **Demosthenes**, an Athenian general, led a naval campaign and fortified a position on **Pylos**, an island along the Peloponnese.
    
- In response, Sparta sent troops to **Sphacteria** to counter Athens’ actions, but the Athenians managed to capture **Spartan soldiers** on the island.
    
- **Cleon** took command, achieving a significant victory, capturing Spartan prisoners.
    

---

### **Brasidas’ Northern Campaign (424 BCE)**

- **Brasidas**, a Spartan general, launched a campaign in **Thrace** and **Macedonia** with the goal of weakening Athenian power in the northern regions.
    
- He successfully captured **Amphipolis**, an important Athenian colony. **Thucydides**, who was tasked with defending the city, arrived too late and was subsequently **exiled**.
    
- The **Second Battle of Amphipolis** occurred in **422 BCE**, where both **Cleon** and **Brasidas** were killed in battle, leading both sides to seek peace.
    

---

### **The Peace of Nicias (421 BCE)**

The Peace of Nicias marked a temporary cessation of hostilities between Athens and Sparta, with the following stipulations:

- A **fifty-year truce** between the two powers.
    
- All captured territories were to be returned to their original occupiers.
    
- Prisoners from both sides were to be released to their home city.
    
- Hostilities were to cease entirely, and future disputes were to be resolved through **diplomatic and judicial** means.
    

The peace lasted **only ten years** before tensions reignited.

---

### **War After the 'Peace' of Nicias**

- **416 BCE**: Athens sent an expedition to **Melos**, which resisted Athenian occupation. Upon surrender, the Athenians executed all male citizens and enslaved the women and children.
    
- **415-413 BCE**: The **Sicilian Expedition** proved disastrous for Athens. Athens underestimated the size of the force and the challenges of the island. The campaign ended in a crushing defeat for Athens, leading to the loss of much of their fleet.
    
- **413-404 BCE**: The **Ionian War** involved further battles and shifting alliances:
    
    - Sparta fortified **Decelea** in **Attica**, disrupting Athens’ grain supply.
        
    - With **Persian** financial aid, Sparta built a naval fleet to challenge Athens on the seas.
        

---

### **The Oligarchy of the Four Hundred (411 BCE)**

- **Peisander** led a coup in **Athens**, establishing an **oligarchic government** in an attempt to negotiate peace with Persia and Sparta.
    
- The new regime was marked by **violence** and **assassinations**, and its promises to dilute the oligarchy to five thousand citizens were never fulfilled.
    
- Meanwhile, **Athenian forces at Samos** supported the democratic faction, ultimately restoring **democracy** and recalling **Alcibiades** to gain Persian support.
    
- **Sparta** exploited the chaos by attacking **Euboea** and destroying an Athenian fleet, leading to the downfall of the Four Hundred. The **Five Thousand** oligarchy briefly took power but was short-lived, as **democracy** was restored after a victory at the **Battle of Kyzikos (410 BCE)**.
    

---

### **The Siege of Syracuse (415-413 BCE)**

- The Athenians attempted to **encircle Syracuse** by building walls around the city. However, the Syracusans received support from the **Spartans**, and after a disastrous battle, the Athenians suffered a crushing defeat.
    
- The generals **Nicias** and **Demosthenes** disagreed on the strategy, which delayed the Athenian response and played into Spartan hands. The Spartans dominated both land and sea.
    
- **40,000 Athenian soldiers** attempting to withdraw were massacred, and **7,000 survivors** were imprisoned in open-air quarries.
    

---

### **Thucydides to Xenophon**

- Thucydides' history stops just after the establishment of the **Five Thousand** oligarchy.
    
- **Xenophon’s _Hellenica_** picks up from this point, covering the years **411-362 BCE** in seven books.
    
- Scholars suggest that **Xenophon** may have continued Thucydides' work, as Book 1 of _Hellenica_ begins with "after this," and there is a noticeable change in prose style starting in Book 3.
    

---

### **The Final Phase of the War**

- Despite Athenian victory at the **Battle of Arginusae (406 BCE)**, where the Athenians defeated the Spartans, the commanders were executed for failing to rescue **drowning sailors**.
    
- Spartan leadership was taken over by **Lysander**, who utilized **Persian financial aid** to build a powerful fleet.
    
- Lysander’s strategy focused on cutting off Athenian supply lines in the **Hellespont** by attacking key Athenian strongholds.
    
- In **405 BCE**, the **Battle of Aegospotamoi** proved to be a decisive Spartan victory, effectively sealing Athens' fate.
    
- Athens was forced to **negotiate surrender**, leading to the dismantling of its walls and the end of the Peloponnesian War

</br>

### **Greek World Lecture 12: Socrates and His Philosophical Impact**

---

### **Socrates' Philosophical Background**

- **Cicero** famously said, "Socrates brought philosophy from heaven down to earth," emphasizing Socrates' transformation of philosophy into a more practical, human-centered pursuit.
    
- Socrates revolutionized **Greek philosophy** by focusing on **moral philosophy** and **ethics**, introducing questions about how humans ought to live, what constitutes justice, and how one should conduct their life.
    

---

### **The Warrior's Code**

- The **Greek concept of justice** was often debated, questioning whether it was part of the natural order or a matter of convention.
    
- **Archilochus** (fr. 126) expressed the view that revenge and injury were natural responses to wrongs, aligning with the idea that there were fixed, personal moral codes of retribution.
    
- The question Socrates posed in his teachings was: is **morality** and **justice** rooted in nature (i.e., divinely ordained), or is it a social construct created by humans? This leads to the central idea of whether ethical codes are universal or culturally relative.
    

---

### **Might vs Right**

- In **Thucydides' Melian Dialogue**, Athens, with its superior military power, threatens **Melos** with destruction unless they surrender. This brutal declaration reflects the idea that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must," a clear articulation of the **Might vs. Right** dilemma.
    
- Socrates was likely influenced by such concepts as he questioned the fairness and nature of justice in human societies, urging individuals to think beyond power struggles and aim for virtue and truth.
    

---

### **The Trial of Socrates**

- **Socrates** did not write any of his teachings down, and much of what we know of his philosophy comes from his students, particularly **Plato** and **Xenophon**.
    
- He was tried in **399 BCE** for charges of **impiety** (not believing in the city’s gods and introducing new deities) and **corrupting the youth**. His accuser, **Meletus**, sought the death penalty.
    
- The trial became a pivotal moment in the history of philosophy, as Socrates was condemned not only for his ideas but for his manner of questioning and challenging the status quo.
    

---

### **Socrates' Defense Speech (Apology)**

1. **Proemium (Introduction)**:  
    Socrates opens his defense by trying to win over the audience's goodwill. He argues that the accusations against him are based on falsehoods and requests that they focus on facts rather than the slanders he's been subjected to.
    
2. **Prothesis (Thesis)**:  
    He distinguishes between two sets of accusers: the **old accusers** (those who have long accused him of being a sophist) and the **new accusers** (the immediate charge of impiety and corruption). He then emphasizes the difficulty of defending himself against such broad accusations.
    
3. **The Defense**:
    
    - Against the **old accusers**: Socrates argues that he is not a sophist and never took payment for his teachings, unlike the typical sophists. He claims that the accusation is politically motivated, as many young aristocrats who mimicked his behavior irritated their parents.
        
    - Against the **new accusers**: Socrates dismisses the charge of atheism, stating that he believes in gods but in his own way, which may not align with the popular interpretations of the gods.
        
4. **Excursus (Digression)**:  
    Socrates refuses to stop his philosophy or his public questioning if this would lead to his release. He stresses that his purpose is to care for the city's moral well-being, revealing his commitment to the idea that philosophy serves the greater good.
    
5. **Epilogue (Conclusion)**:  
    Socrates, now convicted, proposes a counter-charge, suggesting that he should be rewarded for his service to Athens, not punished. However, he ultimately expresses a preference for death over living a life of falsehood, raising the question of the nature of death.
    

---

### **Was Socrates Guilty?**

- **Athenian guilt** was often judged not solely on legalistic grounds but on what was perceived as best for the **polis**. In this sense, Socrates' conviction could be seen as a measure taken to "purify" Athens by removing someone who was considered to be an **irreligious traitor** and a disruptor of traditional values.
    
- However, Socrates was not truly guilty in the sense of violating specific laws, and some argue that his unorthodox views on religion and social structures were seen as dangerous to Athenian society's stability. **Plato's portrayal of Socrates** in the **Apology** suggests that Socrates was more pious than many of his accusers, using the trial as a way to criticize the city of Athens for its intolerance of ideas that challenged the status quo.
    

---

### **The Legacy of Socrates**

- Socrates' method of questioning, known as the **Socratic Method**, involved asking probing questions to draw out truths from others, leading them to self-examine their beliefs.
    
- He laid the foundation for future **philosophical inquiry**, and his **ethical philosophy** greatly influenced subsequent thinkers like **Plato** and **Aristotle**, shaping Western thought for centuries.

</br>

### **Greek World Lecture 13: The Struggle for Hegemony**

---

### **Three Main Contenders for Greek Hegemony**

After the Peloponnesian War, three major city-states—**Sparta**, **Athens**, and **Thebes**—vied for control over the Greek world. While there were smaller contenders, these three were the dominant powers shaping Greek politics.

---

### **Sparta: Hegemony Attempts and Consolidation**

- **Post-War Power Grab**:  
    After its victory in the Peloponnesian War, Sparta sought to extend its influence by dismantling democratic regimes and replacing them with **oligarchic governments** favorable to Spartan ideology. They demanded tribute from conquered cities to fund their military operations and control over the Greek world.
    
- **Lysander’s Rise**:  
    The Spartan general **Lysander** became a key figure in these attempts at hegemony. After achieving a decisive victory in the war, he was elevated to a near-divine status by the Samians, who erected altars and made sacrifices in his name, calling their festival **Lysandreia** after him.
    
- **Persian Relations**:  
    Sparta also found itself tasked with defending the Greek cities in **Asia Minor** from potential Persian threats. Although Sparta attempted to sponsor a war against Persia, Athens and Thebes had no desire to engage in a conflict with the powerful Persian empire.
    
- **Succession Crisis**:  
    A succession struggle within Sparta followed, particularly after the prophecy warning against a "lame king." **Leotychides** and **Agesilaus** competed for the throne, with Agesilaus ultimately succeeding, though his claim was contested due to his lack of royal blood.
    
- **The Corinthian War (395–387 BCE)**:  
    A coalition of **Athens**, **Thebes**, **Corinth**, and **Argos** rose up against Sparta, seeking to overthrow its hegemony. Despite the large-scale battles, including the significant Battle of **Haliartus**, the war ended with **the King's Peace** (387 BCE). The Persian King **Artaxerxes** brokered peace, demanding that all Greek cities in Asia Minor remain free and that Sparta's influence in the region be recognized. This peace heavily favored Sparta, restricting Athens’ ability to form alliances.
    
- **Consolidation of Power**:  
    After the war, Sparta cracked down on those cities that had supported the anti-Spartan alliance, demanding that they dismantle their defenses and comply with Spartan military mandates. Spartan control over the region grew firmer, but at a cost.
    

---

### **Athens: Resurgence and Rebuilding**

- **The Resistance Movement**:  
    After the fall of Athens to Sparta and the imposition of the **Thirty Tyrants**, Athens eventually restored its democracy. This was done through a resistance movement that overthrew the tyrants with help from **Thebes**.
    
- **New Constitutional Changes**:  
    Athens made significant changes to its government. The **Council Leader** (originally one of the ten tribes’ elected leaders) no longer wielded as much power, with the **nine Prohedroi** taking over much of the leadership functions. Athens also began offering **financial incentives** to encourage participation in politics, increasing the pay for voting in the assembly from one obol to three.
    
- **Rebuilding the Walls**:  
    Athens began rebuilding its defensive walls, known as the **Walls of Konon**, named after the Athenian general **Konon**. This was in response to the destruction of their previous walls during the Peloponnesian War, securing Athens’ defensive strength once again.
    
- **Proposal for a New Persian War**:  
    Athens considered joining or leading another campaign against Persia, re-establishing their previous role in the **Persian Wars**. As **Isocrates** notes in his _Panegyricus_, Athens saw the Persian empire as a significant adversary worth fighting against, instead of continuing internal Greek conflicts.
    
- **A New Alliance**:  
    Athens proposed forming a new alliance with former allies and other cities, but with different terms. Unlike the earlier empire-building days, this new alliance would require no tribute and allow cities to remain autonomous, suggesting a more peaceful and cooperative vision for Greek unity.
    

---

### **Thebes: Rise to Power**

- **Boeotian Federalism**:  
    Thebes led the **Boeotian League**, a federation of cities in the region of **Boeotia**. The region was organized into divisions, each sending a **boeotarch** (leader) to represent them in a federal council. Thebes claimed the right to swear oaths on behalf of all Boeotians, but issues arose with Sparta over the lack of clarity in agreements regarding Boeotian autonomy.
    
- **The Boeotian War**:  
    As Sparta’s dominance grew, conflict with Thebes escalated, leading to the **Boeotian War**. Sparta attempted to force Thebes into submission, but tensions over the terms of peace agreements led to continued hostilities. In the pivotal **Battle of Leuktra** (371 BCE), **Thebes** achieved a decisive victory over Sparta, dealing a blow to Spartan military superiority and signaling the decline of Spartan hegemony.
    
- **Thebes and Persia**:  
    With aspirations of Greek hegemony, Thebes turned to Persia, seeking an alliance to solidify its power in Greece. However, while Thebes had become the most powerful Greek state after its victory at Leuktra, it was still involved in complex negotiations with other city-states and the Persian Empire.
    
- **The Battle of Mantineia (362 BCE)**:  
    The **Battle of Mantineia** marked the last major conflict between the leading Greek powers. Both Thebes and Sparta claimed victory, but the result was ultimately inconclusive. **Epaminondas**, the leader of Thebes, was killed, and neither side gained significant territory. The battle led to further confusion and instability within Greece, which became more fragmented as city-states struggled for influence and survival.
    

---

### **Aftermath and Continued Strife**

- After the **Battle of Mantineia**, Greece was left in a state of confusion, with no one city-state able to dominate. While **Thebes** had reached its zenith, it was unable to maintain its hegemony in the face of internal divisions and external challenges. **Sparta** had been weakened significantly, and **Athens**, though resurgent, was still recovering from the aftermath of its earlier defeat.
    
- The power vacuum that followed would eventually lead to the rise of **Macedon**, particularly under **Philip II**, who would eventually unite Greece under his rule, setting the stage for the rise of **Alexander the Great** and the Macedonian Empire.

### **Greek World Lecture 14: Understanding Slavery in Ancient Greece**

---

### **What Was a Slave?**

- **Dehumanization and the Concept of 'Half a Man'**:  
    In the Greek world, a slave was not considered fully human in the same sense as a free citizen. **Homer**'s _Odyssey_ (17.320-324) reflects this notion, suggesting that slaves lost their humanity and were viewed as lesser beings once enslaved, even if their master lost power or died. Their status as property was permanent, even if they were freed or their masters were no longer in control.
    
    - **Aristotle’s View**:  
        Aristotle, in _Politics_ (1253b), dehumanized slaves further by categorizing them as “living tools” necessary for the management of a household. In this view, slaves were considered not people, but mere tools for their owners' convenience.
        

---

### **Who Was a Slave?**

Slavery in ancient Greece was primarily the result of war, conquest, or punishment. Here are the key ways in which one could become enslaved:

- **War and Conquest**:  
    Many slaves were captured as prisoners of war. These individuals, often from non-Greek (barbarian) territories, were then sold into slavery.
    
- **Punishment for Citizens**:  
    Greeks could also be enslaved as a punishment for criminal behavior or as a consequence of a conquered city’s citizens being forced into servitude. For example, after the **Messenian War**, those who did not fight were made slaves by the Spartans and were called **Helots**.
    
    - **Strabo’s Account**:  
        Strabo (6.3.2) records the aftermath of the Messenian War, where the Lacedaemonians (Spartans) punished those who did not fight by making them slaves, referred to as Helots. Their children, born during the war, were stripped of citizenship and rights.
        

---

### **How Many Slaves?**

- **Census Estimates**:  
    According to an ancient census by **Demetrios of Phaleron**, Athens had a substantial slave population. **Ktesikles** (BNJ 245 frg. 1) states there were an estimated **400,000 slaves** in Athens during his time, though this figure may be exaggerated. A more reasonable estimate suggests **50,000 to 100,000** slaves lived in Athens alone, far outnumbering citizens.
    

---

### **Who Owned Slaves?**

Slaves were owned by the wealthy, as they were a symbol of status and power, but also by those with more modest means.

- **Wealthy Slave Owners**:  
    Wealthy individuals, especially entrepreneurs, could afford to own numerous slaves. Plato, in his _Republic_, provides the example of a man owning fifty or more slaves, suggesting it was not uncommon for the rich to maintain a large workforce.
    
- **Poor Slave Owners**:  
    Interestingly, even those of lower wealth could own slaves. In Aristophanes’ play _Wealth_, a poor character laments his financial state while still owning multiple slaves, which suggests that slave ownership was not restricted solely to the rich.
    
- **Helots**:  
    The **Helots** were a special category of slaves owned by the state, primarily in Sparta. They could not be sold or freed by private individuals, and were seen as inferior. In Sparta, **Helot killing** was part of the rite of passage for young men, demonstrating the brutal treatment they endured.
    

---

### **What Did Slaves Do?**

Slaves in ancient Greece were used for a variety of purposes:

- **Labor Force**:  
    Slaves worked in industries such as agriculture, mining, and construction. Many were employed to work on the **Acropolis** and other public building projects.
    
- **Servants and Entertainment**:  
    Slaves could also serve as domestic servants, providing personal assistance to their masters. In addition, they might be used in entertainment, especially at wealthy feasts and gatherings.
    
- **Enterprise and Business**:  
    Many entrepreneurs owned large numbers of slaves to maintain their businesses, relying on them to run farms, mines, or workshops. Slave labor was a key part of the economic infrastructure.
    

---

### **The Treatment of Slaves**

While slaves were often harshly treated, their treatment varied, and there were legal and social restrictions on the violence they could endure.

- **Physical Violence Restrictions**:  
    In Athens, laws prevented physical violence against slaves, not out of compassion, but to ensure that citizens did not mistake free people for slaves. **Xenophon** in _Athenian Constitution_ (1.10) notes that slaves and metics were often well-dressed and not treated as inferior in terms of appearance, implying that they were not always physically abused.
    
- **Punishment and Abuse**:  
    Despite these restrictions, punishment was common, and slaves could be whipped or harshly punished for disobedience or perceived transgressions. **Lysias** in his speech (1.18) offers a glimpse into the system of punishment, where a slave might be forced to choose between enduring harsh punishment or speaking the truth to avoid further harm.
    
- **Sexual Exploitation**:  
    Slaves were also vulnerable to sexual exploitation. In Athens, the fine for raping a slave was significantly less than for raping a free person, illustrating the legal devaluation of slaves. The fine for raping a slave was five drachmas, compared to 100 staters for raping a free person.
    

---

### **Slave Emancipation**

- **Manumission**:  
    Slaves could be freed by their masters, though proper documentation was often required. In certain circumstances, a slave might also be publicly freed, which would annul the need for official paperwork and make them a free man in the eyes of the community.
    
- **Wartime Freedom**:  
    During wartime, slaves could be granted freedom if they were useful in the war effort, incentivizing them to perform difficult tasks. However, this freedom did not grant full citizenship; instead, freed slaves became **metics**, meaning they were permanent residents but did not have the rights of citizens.
    

---

### **Alternatives to Slavery**

The concept of abolishing slavery was not entertained by Greek thinkers. **Aristotle** and **Krates** both argued that slavery was a natural condition for certain people and could only be ended if "all static implements" were capable of movement. In this view, slavery was seen as a permanent and inherent part of Greek society.

### **Greek World Lecture 15: Macedonia and Its Political Landscape**

---

### **Geography of Ancient Macedonia**

- **Location**:  
    Ancient Macedonia was situated southeast of modern-day Macedonia, with its capital, **Pella**, located just west of Thessaloniki in northern Greece. It was a region that was not fully Greek in its early phases, with Greeks themselves uncertain about its geopolitical identity and place in the Greek world.
    
- **Early Capital - Aigai**:  
    Before Pella, the capital was **Aigai**, which served as the seat of power from the 7th to the 5th centuries BCE. The transition from Aigai to Pella marked a significant shift in Macedonia’s political and cultural landscape.
    
- **Expansion**:  
    The Macedonian region began as a relatively small entity but expanded significantly during the 7th-5th centuries BCE through military conquests, reaching into the northeastern part of the Greek world. Over time, Macedonia would grow into one of the most powerful states in the ancient Mediterranean.
    

---

### **The Royal Dynasty of Macedonia**

- **Herodotus and the Royal Lineage**:  
    According to **Herodotus**, the royal family of Macedonia, starting with **Perdiccas I**, traced its lineage back to **Heracles**, making it a semi-divine royal line. Perdiccas was said to have originated from **Argos**, and with his two brothers, was banished to Macedonia. This connection to Heracles was integral to the legitimacy and status of the Macedonian monarchy.
    
- **The Genealogy of Caranus**:  
    Several sources, including **Satyrus**, **Diodorus**, and **Justin**, agree that the ancestor of the Macedonian royal family was a figure named **Caranus**. Little is known about Caranus, but the common mythological ancestry through Heracles was widely accepted in historical narratives.
    

---

### **Macedonia in the Persian Wars**

- **Persian Influence**:  
    During the **Persian Wars**, **Persia** demanded the submission of both Greece and Macedonia. **Herodotus** recounts that Persia sent an embassy to **Aigai**, demanding subjugation, but this was met with resistance. **Alexander I**, the Macedonian king at the time, ordered the assassination of the Persian embassy, which led to a cover-up to avoid open conflict with Persia. The subsequent Persian envoy was bribed and silenced by the Macedonians, with Alexander offering a sum of money and his sister in marriage.
    
- **Macedonian Counsel to the Spartans**:  
    **Alexander I** also advised the Spartans to avoid war with Persia, but his counsel was rejected by the Spartans, who viewed him as a **tyrant** and a foreigner. This might have been one of the reasons why Greek sources were hesitant to consider Macedonia as fully integrated into the Greek world during this period.
    

---

### **Macedonia in the Peloponnesian War**

- **Macedonian Role in the War**:  
    **Perdiccas II**, the Macedonian king, played a significant role in the events leading to the **Peloponnesian War**. He encouraged the **Athenians** to fight against the **Spartan alliance** to weaken the region. He also sought to use the conflict as a means to expand his influence over the region, particularly over **Potidaea**, a strategically important city on his borders.
    
- **Macedonian Treaty with Athens**:  
    Macedonia signed a treaty with **Athens**, which gave it control over certain fringe territories. The treaty also allowed for Macedonian military support, showing that the kingdom’s strength was in part due to alliances with other Greek states, though its internal cohesion and political stability were still fragile.
    
- **Fragile Kingdom**:  
    Despite its alliances, Macedonia was politically unstable and heavily reliant on the cooperation of smaller regional powers to maintain its power and territory. This fragile foundation would later contribute to the struggles the kingdom faced in securing its position in the Greek world.
    

---

### **Macedonian Political Turmoil**

- **Succession Crisis After Perdiccas**:  
    After the death of **Perdiccas II**, the Macedonian throne lacked a clear successor. **Archelaus**, a man with no legitimate claim to the throne (being the son of a slave of Perdiccas' brother **Alcetas**), seized the throne. To solidify his claim, Archelaus staged the restoration of **Alcetas** to the throne, only to have him and his son, **Archelaus’ cousin**, murdered. Archelaus even drowned his own seven-year-old brother, who was considered a legitimate heir to the throne.
    
- **Archelaus’ Violent Death**:  
    The brutal political maneuvering by Archelaus eventually led to his assassination by one of his pages. This set off another round of succession struggles, highlighting the unstable nature of Macedonian politics during this period. The throne was contested and vulnerable, as succession was often decided through violence and manipulation rather than any stable hereditary process.

</br>

### **Greek World Lecture 16: The Palace and Tombs at Vergina**

---

### **Palace at Vergina**

- **Construction**:  
    The **Palace at Vergina** was built by **Philip II** during his reign from **359-336 BCE**. It reflects the grandeur and sophistication of Macedonian architecture at the time.
    
- **Design and Decoration**:  
    The palace's rooms were known for their **detailed mosaics** underfoot, especially in the **banqueting rooms**, showcasing the luxurious lifestyle of the royal family.
    
    - The **residential and banqueting rooms** were located on the east and west sides of the palace.
        
    - The **north side** was designed more as a **balcony**, offering expansive views and perhaps serving as a space for ceremonial or public events.
        

---

### **Macedonian Tombs at Vergina**

- **Hellenistic Tombs**:  
    The tombs at **Vergina**, primarily from the **Hellenistic period**, have provided a wealth of archaeological information about Macedonian burial practices.
    
    - Most of the tombs are characterized by **elaborate wall paintings**, which were preserved by being buried underground.
        
    - The tombs often had **facades**, usually made of **plaster**, and were integrated into the landscape, built in complexes known as **tumuli** (mound tombs).
        
- **Cremation vs. Burial**:  
    While **cremation** was the norm, there are several **sarcophagi** and burial items that suggest some bodies were buried in the traditional sense, offering important insights into Macedonian funerary practices.
    
    - When the tombs were uncovered in the **1970s**, one tomb remained **unplundered**, providing a rare and invaluable glimpse into the past.
        

---

### **Tomb 1**

- **Date and Significance**:  
    **Tomb 1** dates around **336 BCE** and contains one of the best-preserved examples of **wall painting** from the ancient world, covering three sides of the tomb.
    
    - The painting portrays the myth of **Persephone's abduction by Hades**, a myth deeply connected to the themes of death and the afterlife. The choice of this image is significant given the tomb's purpose.
        
    - The **perspective** and **composition** of the painting offer a level of detail and artistic expression that was often difficult to achieve in other mediums, such as **vase painting**.
        
- **Artistic Elements**:
    
    - The chariot in the painting is depicted in a dynamic manner, with **wheels** shown as if they are moving towards the viewer, enhancing the sense of movement.
        
    - This use of perspective is one of the early examples of dramatic shifts in Greek art, showcasing the growing sophistication in visual storytelling.
        

---

### **Tomb 2**

- **Date and Significance**:  
    **Tomb 2**, built around **320 BCE**, is notable for remaining **unplundered** and for its **royal association**.
    
    - The tomb is thought to belong to a member of the Macedonian **royal family**, possibly **Philip III Arrhidaios**, step-brother of **Alexander the Great**. However, definitive evidence for the identity of the buried individual has not been conclusively identified.
        
- **Architectural Features**:  
    The **facade** of the tomb is adorned with a **Doric frieze** and **metopes**, similar to the design elements seen in the **Parthenon**, indicating the significance and wealth of the tomb.
    
    - The **wall painting** wrapping around the facade shows a **royal hunting scene**, which was unusual for Greek art at the time, especially in its use of **landscape**—a departure from the typical focus on figures.
        
- **Symbolism of Alexander the Great**:  
    The **figure in purple**, speculated to be a **young Alexander**, suggests that the tomb's occupant may have been closely associated with the royal family, perhaps representing **Philip II** and his son, Alexander.
    
- **Artifacts Found**:  
    The tomb contained **numerous artifacts**, including:
    
    - **Gold Larnakes (boxes)**, which held cremated remains, textiles, and **gold crowns**. One of the boxes contained the remains of a **woman**, while the other contained those of a **man**, possibly the buried royal figure.
        
    - A **stone couch** with **ivory decoration**, featuring portraits of **Alexander** and **Philip II**.
        
    - **Greaves** (leg armor) that were not equal in length, suggesting they belonged to **Philip II**, who had been maimed in battle.
        
    - A **gold quiver** and **alabaster vessels**, emphasizing the wealth and royal status of the tomb's occupant.
        

---

### **Tomb Paintings and Symbolism**

- **Depictions of the Deceased**:  
    In Hellenistic tombs, the deceased were often shown **seated**, sometimes with figures such as **Aiakos** and **Rhadamanthys**, mythological figures associated with the afterlife and judgment. This indicates the importance of the afterlife and the portrayal of the deceased in a manner befitting their status.
    
    - The wall paintings often depicted the deceased in a dignified, almost immortalized manner, conveying the belief in an ongoing legacy even after death.

</br>

### **Greek World Lecture 18: Succession and Power Struggles After Alexander's Death**

---

### **Succession Crisis**

- **Diverse Centres of Power**:  
    After **Alexander the Great's death** in 323 BCE, his vast empire was left without a clear successor, leading to multiple centres of power emerging. These centres were located in:
    
    - **Pella** (Macedonia), where Alexander's family originated.
        
    - **Babylon**, where Alexander had died and where his immediate successors initially convened.
        
    - **Asia Minor (Turkey)**, where powerful figures such as **Katelos** and **Antigonus** had strongholds.
        
    
    These territorial strongholds laid the foundation for future conflicts over the control of Alexander’s empire.
    
- **Succession Plans**:  
    Alexander had a **half-brother**, Arrhidaeus (often disparaged by contemporary sources as a "half-wit"), and an **unborn son** (later named **Alexander IV**).  
    The decision regarding succession was that both would share power: **Arrhidaeus** would reign as the nominal king, and the **son** (once born) would become co-ruler, with the understanding that the son would eventually assume full control when of age.
    

---

### **The Fight Over Alexander's Body**

- **The Body's Journey**:  
    Following **Alexander's death**, his body was initially intended to be transported to **Aegae**, the traditional burial place of Macedonian kings. However, **Ptolemy**, one of Alexander’s generals and future ruler of Egypt, intervened in the process.
    
    - Ptolemy **stole the body** during its journey and took it to **Egypt**, where he kept it in **Alexandria** as a symbol of his own authority and legitimacy.
        
- **Perdiccas’ Revenge**:  
    In response to this action, **Perdiccas**, another of Alexander’s generals, launched a military campaign against Egypt to reclaim the body. However, **Perdiccas’ campaign failed disastrously**, resulting in his **assassination**. This incident further complicated the succession struggle and highlighted the instability of the empire after Alexander's death.
    

---

### **Guardians of the Kings**

- **Arrhidaeus and the Guardianship**:  
    Following the power vacuum left by Alexander's death, **Arrhidaeus** (the half-brother) and **the unborn son** were meant to be the figureheads of the empire, under the guardianship of several prominent generals. Initially, the **guardianship** of the kings was entrusted to various commanders, but this system quickly became fraught with conflict.
    
    - **Arrhidaeus** and **his regents** relinquished the guardianship when **Eurydice**, the wife of **Philip III** (Arrhidaeus), turned several Macedonian factions against them.
        
- **Antipater’s Role**:  
    After the guardians resigned, **Antipater**, another of Alexander's generals, took over as the guardian. However, his position was also threatened by **Eurydice**, who sought greater power for herself and her son. Antipater, in turn, used **rhetoric and threats** to subdue Eurydice and maintain control over the Macedonian kingdom.
    

---

### **Olympias’ Control of the Throne**

- **Olympias’ Bold Move**:  
    **Olympias**, the mother of **Alexander the Great** and grandmother of **Alexander IV**, seized control of the kingdom in the aftermath of the political chaos. She effectively **captured the royal family** and took control without significant bloodshed.
    
    - **Olympias** imprisoned **Eurydice** and her husband **Philip III** under harsh conditions, eventually ordering **Philip’s assassination** at the hands of **Thracian guards**, further consolidating her position.
        

---

### **The Treaty and the Division of the Empire**

- **The Treaty to End the Succession Conflict**:  
    After a prolonged and bloody conflict over succession, the leading generals—**Cassander**, **Ptolemy**, **Lysimachus**, and **Antigonus**—negotiated a treaty to divide Alexander’s empire. The terms were as follows:
    
    - **Cassander** would be in charge of **Europe** (Macedonia and Greece) until **Alexander IV** came of age.
        
    - **Lysimachus** would rule **Thrace** (modern-day Bulgaria and parts of Turkey).
        
    - **Ptolemy** would take control of **Egypt** and **Arabia**.
        
    - **Antigonus** would have **precedence in Asia** (modern-day Turkey, Syria, and parts of Persia).
        
    - The **Greeks** were granted **autonomy**, with some cities being allowed a degree of self-rule.
        

This treaty marked the official beginning of the **Wars of the Diadochi**, the power struggles between Alexander’s former generals, as they sought to carve out their own territories from his vast empire.

---

### **Conclusion**

- The period following **Alexander the Great's death** was marked by **power struggles** and **political intrigue**. The succession crisis, the theft of Alexander's body, and the shifting alliances among the former generals played key roles in determining the fate of the empire. Ultimately, the empire was fragmented, and the generational conflict over control of Alexander’s legacy led to the establishment of several Hellenistic kingdoms.
    
- The narrative of **succession** is a critical part of understanding the **fragmentation** of Alexander’s empire and the enduring impact his death had on the ancient world.

### **Alexandria: A Hub of Greek-Egyptian Fusion**

- **Foundation and Layout**:
    
    - Founded in **331 BCE** by **Alexander the Great** as part of his vision to create a Greek city in Egypt.
        
    - Built on the **pre-existing** foundations of an ancient Egyptian settlement known as **Rhakotis**.
        
    - The city was **designed with a grid plan**, making it one of the most organized urban spaces of the ancient world. This layout was a hallmark of Greek urban planning.
        
- **Greek Identity in Egypt**:
    
    - Alexandria was a **Greek city** situated within the Egyptian state, and as such, its **citizens** were **exempt from the poll tax** that other Egyptians had to pay. This exemption emphasized the city's distinct Greek identity in a largely Egyptian context.
        

---

### **Tombs in Alexandria**

- **Alexander the Great’s Tomb**:
    
    - After **Alexander's death** in **Babylon**, his body was transported by **Ptolemy** to **Memphis** and then moved to **Alexandria**, where it was eventually entombed.
        
- **Ptolemaic Tombs**:
    
    - The **Ptolemaic dynasty** constructed their own tombs in Alexandria, often using **alabaster**, a unique material for the region. This contrasts with other Greek tombs, which were typically made from **marble** or other stones.
        
    - The design of these **Ptolemaic tombs** was **Macedonian** in style, maintaining a cultural link to the dynasty's Macedonian roots.
        

---

### **Pharos of Alexandria**

- **The Lighthouse of Alexandria**:
    
    - The **Pharos** was one of the **Seven Wonders of the Ancient World**, known for its impressive height, which ranged from **120-140 meters**. It was the tallest structure of its time.
        
    - Although **nothing survives** of the lighthouse today, **foundation blocks** and **coins** depicting the structure have provided insights into its shape and grandeur.
        
- **Sostratus of Rhodes**:
    
    - The construction of the Pharos is credited to **Sostratus of Rhodes**, a Greek engineer and architect, whose contribution is remembered for its innovation in ancient **Greek technology**, similar to the **Colossus of Rhodes**.
        
- **Remains and Depictions**:
    
    - **Sculpture fragments** and **blocks** believed to be from the Pharos have been found in the nearby waters, providing evidence of its existence and monumental status in Alexandria.
        

---

### **Serapis: The Greek-Egyptian Deity**

- **Creation and Worship**:
    
    - **Serapis** was a **syncretic deity**, combining aspects of both **Greek** and **Egyptian** religious beliefs. He was partially based on the Egyptian god **Osiris** and the Greek god **Hades**.
        
    - Worshipped at the **Serapeum** (the temple dedicated to him) in Alexandria, **Serapis** was often depicted alongside his wife **Isis**.
        
- **The Ptolemies and Serapis**:
    
    - It is believed that the **Ptolemaic rulers** used the **Serapeum** as a royal cult center, further solidifying their legitimacy and connection to the gods.
        
    - The worship of **Aphrodite** was also associated with the Serapeum, suggesting a blend of Greek and Egyptian religious practices.
        

---

### **The Library of Alexandria**

- **Intellectual Hub**:
    
    - The **Library of Alexandria** is said to have housed up to **700,000 papyrus scrolls**, making it one of the largest and most significant collections of knowledge in the ancient world.
        
    - The library attracted a vast array of **intellectuals, scholars, and philosophers** from across the Hellenistic world, making it a center for the exchange of ideas and knowledge.
        
- **Destruction**:
    
    - **Numerous fires** devastated the library over time, the most notable of which occurred in **48 BCE**. Despite these calamities, the Library of Alexandria remained a symbol of intellectual pursuit.
        

---

### **Ptolemaic Coinage and Imagery**

- **Depiction of Ptolemaic Kings**:
    
    - The **Ptolemaic rulers** were often depicted on coins in the guise of gods, reinforcing their divine right to rule. They typically wore a **diadem** in their hair, symbolizing their **deification**.
        
    - **Aegis of Zeus** was frequently worn by the Ptolemies on coins and sculptures, signaling their divine power.
        
- **Ptolemy I’s Egyptian Iconography**:
    
    - **Ptolemy I**, the founder of the Ptolemaic dynasty, was often depicted in a manner akin to an **Egyptian pharaoh**, reinforcing his role as both Greek and Egyptian ruler.
        
- **Bilingual Imagery**:
    
    - The **imagery** used in Ptolemaic coinage and other artworks was often **bilingual**, blending both **Greek and Egyptian styles** to represent the merging of cultures under the Ptolemaic rule.
        

---

### **Conclusion: Alexandria as a Cultural and Political Nexus**

- **Alexandria** stood as a central hub where Greek and Egyptian cultures merged, symbolized by the city's founding, monumental architecture, syncretic religious practices, and intellectual legacy.
    
- The **Ptolemaic dynasty** skillfully combined Greek traditions with Egyptian ones, both politically and culturally, leaving a lasting impact on the Mediterranean world.

### **Greek World Lecture 22: Hellenistic Communities and the Polis**

---

### **The Concept of Hellenism**

- **Hellenistic** was initially a term used to describe communities that lived in **urban areas** or **cities** (rather than rural settings) and had a **civic centre**. This distinction marks a shift from the previous, more rural-focused lifestyle in the Greek world.
    

---

### **The Polis: A Community in Partnership**

- The **polis** (city-state) was viewed as a collective **partnership** aimed at achieving the **common good** of its members. According to **Aristotle**, the polis was designed to foster the well-being of its citizens, with each individual playing a role in its functioning.
    
- Aristotle’s idea suggests that the **polis** wasn’t merely a Greek institution, as it was found in other societies as well, such as **Carthage**, **Sparta**, and **Crete**. These societies, although not Greek in the traditional sense, were still seen to possess **good constitutions**, structured around the well-being of the community.
    

---

### **Citizenship and Social Structure in the Polis**

- **Citizenship** was structured through various levels of **organization**:
    
    - Citizens were typically subdivided into **demes**, which were smaller administrative divisions, and further categorized by **tribe**. This structure ensured that there was a sense of local identity and belonging.
        
- **Eligibility for Civic Roles**:
    
    - Citizens who were raised within a **deme** could qualify for further **training at the Gymnasium**, which was a space for physical and intellectual education. This training was a significant aspect of Greek civic life, as it prepared individuals for **public duties**.
        
- Being trained at the Gymnasium made citizens eligible for:
    
    - **Marriage**: A citizen could marry within the community and form a family that participated in civic life.
        
    - **Priesthoods and Magistracies**: Citizens who underwent proper training and education could also serve in **priesthoods** (religious roles) and hold **magistracies** (political offices).
        
    - **Voting Rights**: Citizens had the right to **vote in the council** and **assemblies**, contributing to the decision-making processes of the polis.
        

---

### **The Polis as a Civic Model**

- The **polis**, as a civic institution, was a complex system where the **individual** and the **community** were in constant interaction. The structures of the polis, from the deme to the larger city-state, were designed to foster both **individual** and **community success**, ensuring that all citizens had opportunities to participate in the civic, political, and religious life of their city.

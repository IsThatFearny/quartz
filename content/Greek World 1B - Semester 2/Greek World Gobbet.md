Decree establishing Athenian judicial relations with Phaselis, OR 120, ca. 469–450 BC(?)  

The Council and the People decided. AkamantisV was in prytany. [Mnasippos or  
Onasippos] was secretary. Neokleides was chairman. Leon proposed: to inscribe the  
decree for the Phaselites. Whatever cause of action arises at Athens against any of  
the Phaselites, the trials are to be held at Athens before the polemarch, as for the Chians,  
and nowhere else. Of the other cases covered by judicial conventions, the trials shall be  
held in accordance with the existing judicial conventions with the Phaselites. The – – –  
shall be abolished. If any of the other officials accepts a case against any of the Phaselites  
[contrary to this?], if he condemns, the condemnation shall be invalid. If anybody  
contravenes what has been decreed, he shall owe ten thousand drachmas sacred to  
Athena. Let the secretary of the Council inscribe this decree on a stone stele and set it  
down on the acropolis, at the expense of the Phaselites.

The Phaselis decree was composed in c.450 BC  (although some argue that it is closer to c. 420).[^1] It is part of a series of Athenian imperial decrees that managed relations between Athens and her allies, such as Chalkis, Samos, and of course Phaselis. In this case, the aspect of inter-state relations covered is the trial of Phaselites in Athens when defending themselves against an Athenian prosecutor. The decree affords Phaselites the right to have their trial held in the court of the polemarch, the judge who held jurisdiction over those in Athens who held citizenship of another *polis*.[^2]

Before one can discuss the relevance of the decree for understanding Athenian imperialism, one must deal with the controversy over the exact implications of the Phaselis decree. It has historically been read as introducing two conflicting mandates: Phaselites must travel to Athens in order to defend their case; but are granted the privilege of having their case heard in the court of the polemarch (which tended to be a faster process).[^3] It would seem that Phaselites are simultaneously being punished and rewarded - not strictly a cause for criticism: it can be argued that it would be an example of imperialist strong-arming, making it more expensive for non-Athenians to have their case heard.[^4] Modern scholarship, however, has presented a secondary interpretation: that the decree applies only to Phaselites currently in Athens, and that the former view is caused by mispunctuation in the original translation (the Phaselite in question is not defined as being 'in Athens').[^5] The language used supports this: the decree is inscribed 'for' the Phaselites, implying that they are being favoured. Similarly, the fact that the Chians (notoriously loyal to Athens) are mentioned as an equal party strongly correlates the status of both parties as favoured by Athens.[^6] One may thus conclude that in interpreting Athenian imperialism through the decree, we should hold this view.

By this interpretation of the decree, Athens in this period is characterised as primarily using 'honorific diplomacy' in relations with other states.[^7] Indeed, other decrees show similar degrees of favour towards allies, using positive language to woo a stronger alliance and improved economic conditions (after all, Phaselis was a major merchant city).[^8] For example, in one decree the Eteokarpathians are referred to as "benefactors of the Athenians" and permitted autonomy.[^9] The Phaselis decree is one of the first Athenian uses of honorifics regarding entire communities, and this 'soft' wielding of imperial power would be used throughout the rest of the 5th century and into the 4th.[^10] This signifies a development in Athenian imperialism from the use of military might and naval strength to intimidate (seen in the decree on Chalkis - it is made clear that Athens took hostages to ensure cooperation),[^11] to a more diplomatic methodology in maintaining relationships with allies, upon whom Athens relied for favourable trade and military agreements throughout the empire. It should be noted that the motivations behind such decrees remained the same regardless of the approach used: complete hegemony over the Athenian empire.

### Research

**Key Points**

- Usually dated 469-450 (Fornara, *The Phaselis Decree*), although this is contested by some as having being written in c. 420 (James, *Repunctuating Athenian Imperialism in the Phaselis decree*; Lazar, *Athenian Power in the Fifth Century BC)
- Part of a series of imperial decrees that managed relationships between Athens and her allies. Similar decrees can be found relating to Chalkis, Methone, Samos, and various other states which were allies of Athens.
- The court of the polemarch (an Athenian official) was reserved for privileged foreigners - it is more favourable to the foreign defendant
- Lazar argues that Athens was primarily engaged in 'honorific diplomacy' in managing their allies


James argues that there are two interpretations of the Phaselis decree:

1. The Phaselis decree mandated that Phaselite defendants would have to argue their case in an Athenian court (albeit a privileged one). Inconsistent with inter-state tradition, in which a defendant would have their case tried in their own city (i.e. an Athenian would defend himself in Athens, a Phaselite would defend himself in Phaselis).

2. On the other hand one may also understand that the decree is being used not to call Phaselites to Athens from Phaselis, but applies only to Phaselites who are already in Athens.

The difference is marked, argues James, by a difference in punctuation, namely the positioning of a single comma, to emphasise that the Phaselite in question is *in* Athens at the time of the case arising. 

The latter intepretation would certainly fit in closer with the various other decrees that Athens passed in relation to other allied states, which Lazar argues tended to provide further autonomy from the Athenian imperialist machine, especially if said state brought significant benefits to the alliance (as Phaselis did through merchant activity). Similarly, James argues that the tone of the text is favourable to the Phaselites - it is written *for* them.

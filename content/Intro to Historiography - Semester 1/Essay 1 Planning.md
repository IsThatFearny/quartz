#### **Overview**

Dr. Oakland introduces **“history from below”**, a historiographical approach focusing on the lives, experiences, and agency of ordinary people—contrasting with traditional history’s emphasis on elites, politics, and warfare. It overlaps with **social history**, **labor history**, and **people’s history**, but centers particularly on marginalized groups.

---

#### **Origins and Definitions**

- Early precursors include **17th–18th century antiquarians** like _John Aubrey_, who linked social behavior to economic life.
    
- **Social history** studies societal structures using social science and quantitative methods.
    
- **History from below** (emerging in the 1950s–60s) emphasizes personal experiences, voices, and agency of the marginalized, often with leftist or Marxist inflections.
    

---

#### **The Annales School (France)**

- Founded by **Lucien Febvre** and **Marc Bloch** in the 1920s; later developed by **Fernand Braudel** and **Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie**.
    
- Advocated **total history**—long-term structures of economy, geography, and mentality—and later **microhistory**, detailed local case studies.
    
- Braudel’s _The Mediterranean_ exemplifies “parachutist” (broad-scale) history; Ladurie’s _Montaillou_ and _Carnival in Romans_ represent “truffle-hunter” (micro) history.
    
- The school blended social science and cultural approaches, later influencing cultural history.
    

---

#### **British Marxist Tradition**

- Rooted in early 20th-century socialism and the **Fabian Society** (e.g., Beatrice & Sidney Webb, founders of LSE).
    
- Post-war historians such as **E.P. Thompson**, **Christopher Hill**, and **Eric Hobsbawm** advanced **Marxist social history**.
    
- Journals like _Past & Present_ (1952) and _History Workshop Journal_ (1970s) provided key platforms, the latter emphasizing community-based and activist history.
    

---

#### **E.P. Thompson and the Making of the English Working Class**

- Thompson (1924–1993) moved from communism to **socialist humanism** and **the New Left**.
    
- His 1963 work _The Making of the English Working Class_ argued that class was **a lived cultural experience**, not just an economic structure.
    
- He sought to “**rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite, and the like from the enormous condescension of posterity**.”
    
- Later work (_Customs in Common_, 1991) explored **popular culture**, **moral economy**, **time-discipline**, and **ritual**—integrating cultural and social history.
    

---

#### **Methods and Sources**

- Challenge: ordinary people left few records.
    
- Approaches include:
    
    - **Quantitative methods** (e.g., _CAMPOP_ studies of height, mortality, labor change).
        
    - **Digitization projects** like _Old Bailey Online_ (criminal trial records, 1684–1913).
        
    - **Legal and coroner’s records** (e.g., _Everyday Life and Fatal Hazard in Tudor England_; _Forms of Labour_).
        
    - **Qualitative microhistories**, such as the case of _Lydia Armfield_ (1680s), revealing gender, religion, and cultural beliefs.
        

---

#### **Critiques and Developments**

- 1980s–1990s critiques: overemphasis on class, neglect of gender, race, sexuality, and individual agency.
    
- The **cultural and linguistic turns** questioned objectivity and representation, but ultimately led to a **synthesis**—social and cultural history seen as interdependent.
    
- The _Cultural and Social History_ journal (2004) formalized this reconciliation.
    

---

#### **Contemporary and Community History**

- Inspired by **Raphael Samuel** and the **History Workshop movement**, history from below expanded beyond academia.
    
- **Oral history** became vital for capturing working-class, ethnic minority, and LGBTQ+ voices.
    
- Civil rights and activist movements (e.g., feminism, Black Lives Matter) renewed focus on **marginalized histories** and **public commemoration** (e.g., _Edward Colston statue_).
    

---

#### **Conclusion**

“History from below” has evolved from Marxist and social-scientific roots into a broad, inclusive, and interdisciplinary approach. It continues to value **everyday life**, **agency**, and **cultural experience**, whether pursued in academia or through community activism.

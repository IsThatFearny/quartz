### Is 'history from below' a reflection of the theories of Marx and Engels?

Whilst 'history from below' has its roots in pre-Marxist scholarship, its true genesis was heavily influenced by the theories of Marx and Engels.[^1] Scholars such as E.P. Thompson and Eric Hobsbawm all had a Marxist or Communist background, practising history using Marxist methodology.[^2] However, modern 'history from below' has developed significantly since the scholarship of the mid-20<sup>th</sup> century and has diverged in methodology and ethos. One cannot therefore argue that 'history from below' remains true to its Marxist roots as its modern form has evolved too far to remain identifiable with Marxism.

'History from Below' was the brainchild of Marxist scholarship: the History Workshop at Ruskin College and the Fabian Society were major participants in its development.[^3] The works of Thompson, Hobsbawm, and Hill are all inherently Marxist: Thompson's seminal work 'The Making of the English Working Class' maintains a strong focus on the proletariat throughout..[^4] Marx saw 'history from below' as a method of studying History that would allow its practitioner to understand societal change and class struggle, and historical materialism and economic determinism shaped early theories.[^5] The primary focus of this form of history was the 'working class', and thus it was a majoritarian study.[^6] Early Marxist scholars often defined it as synonymous with 'labour history' or, as Thompson himself spoke of, 'history-as-industrial-relations'.[^7] Given the Marxist focus on the transition from Capitalism to Socialism, this makes sense: the proletariat are to be the primary agents in catalysing the revolution.[^8] However, late-19<sup>th</sup> century scholarship often makes the criticism that 'history from below' in this form focuses too much on class and collective agency, and tends to ignore individual characteristics such as gender, age, race, and individual agency.[^9] These criticisms led to significant development in the field: the modern form of 'people's history' distances itself from the focus on class struggle and the proletariat.[^10] Whilst still majoritarian, the focus has shifted away from class and economic determinism. This divergence from Marxist historical tradition leads one to argue that 'history from below' no longer reflects the theories of Marx and Engels.

Further, since the development of 'history from below', 'microhistories' have become increasingly prevalent. Marx's position was that 'history from below' was the means to realising the final goal, an understanding of how and why societies change on the whole.[^11] However, the pursual of microhistories undermines this goal somewhat: this history may not reveal a wider class struggle or societal shift, and is not a case study for broader theories, instead providing the historian with an understanding of an individual's life, a certain culture, an event, etc.[^12] Further, microhistories are not deterministic: a central tenet of Marx's form of history was that History develops necessarily and inevitably, with little interest in the 'mundane'; microhistories stray from determinism and hone in on the aspects of history that a broader study may gloss over.[^13] These microhistories still fit the definition of 'history from below' but do not fit Marx's original aims.

Whilst the Marxist, deterministic conception of 'history from below' can be glimpsed in the present iteration of the field, however 'reflection' is too strong a term. There remains a majoritarian focus, but the shift from class struggle, social strata, and industrial history to everyday life and individual narratives moves too far from the roots of 'history from below' to be associated with Marxism to the same extent. This becomes quite clear when one compares Engels' 'The Condition of the Working Class in England' (considered by Hobsbawn to be the first book using Marxist methodology) and Thompson's 'The Making of the English Working Class' to the more recent 'A People's Tragedy' by Orlando Figes: despite Figes' work focussing on a Leninist revolution from below, which one might take to mean that a focus on class struggle remains, there is a clear break from the example set by Marx and Engels.[^14] Figes avoids grouping the 'working class', emphasising the everyday lives of his subjects. It must, therefore, be determined that whilst 'history from below' remains indebted to Marxist ideology, but no longer mirrors Marxist historical theory.

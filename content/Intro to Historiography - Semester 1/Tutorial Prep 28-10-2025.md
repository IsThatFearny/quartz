### Part I

**Why does Rosenwein think emotions are important for the study of history?**

Political and economic events are not entirely unemotional affairs, and it is important to be able to attache emotion to these moments in order to understand motivations, causes, effects, etc. Fevre: "the emotional life is always ready to overflow the intellectual life". Fevre argued that history could not be understood nor be made to be understood "without this primordial interest that I call the psychological".


**What does Rosenwein mean by the 'hydraulic model' of emotions?**

The 'hydraulic model' is the historical view that describes emotions as "liquids" or "energies" which are pressurised and ready to release themselves. These can be suppressed, but if released they will lead to emotional outbursts. Rosenwein states that her models have superseded this.

**According to Rosenwein, what did Norbert Elias argue with respect to emotions in *The Civilising Process*? What impact, according to Rosenwein, has it had upon historical approaches to emotion?**

Elias welcomes emotionology, seeing it as a driving force behind understanding his 'civilising process'. Rosenwein: "it was in the psychology of the courtiers... that the civilising process first began". Elias' text has had a significant impact upon those studying the history of emotion, causing them to see the narrative of the West as a "history of increasing emotional restraint". This not only affected historians, but also those such as Weber, Freud, and Foucault

**What source material does she mention? What approaches to this material does she outline?**

Rosenwein refers to various primary sources: letters, diaries, court records, etc, as well as philosophical works and religious writings. She outlines multiple approaches, such as:

- viewing emotions as socially constructed
	- Emotions are not universal but are rather socially and culturally constructed, shaped by social norms
	- Historians must be careful not to be take an anachronistic approach when looking at past socieities
- emotions as 'cultural practices'
	- Historians should view emotions as cultural practices which are learn and practiced within specific historical and social contexts
	- emphasises the idea that emotional expression and regulation vary
- emotional communities
	- groups of people who share common emotional practices and norms
	- defined by religion, social class, gender, etc.
- private vs public emotions
- interdisciplanary

### Part II

**In the introduction, how does she characterise the tension between labour history and women's history? Why have women been excluded from histories of revolutionary movements?**

"women have come to revolutionary consciousness by means of ideas, actions, and organisations which have been made predominantly by men". Women have tended to be excluded from labour history and histories of revolutionary movements because they were often not the obvious agents of these movements. Revolutionary movements have traditionally been male-defined and women were subordinate. 

**In Chapter 5, what themes does Rowbotham consider? What chronologies does she cover in doing so? What is her geographical focus? What kinds of evidence does she use? How does this challenge traditional empirical practices? How does this build on and challenge the approaches taken by Raphael Samuel and E. P. Thompson?**

In Chapter 5, “Bread and Roses,” Rowbotham uses the language and episodes of women’s industrial struggle to show how working-class women’s campaigns combined immediate material demands (wages, bread) with social needs and dignity (roses). She concentrates on the industrial/late-nineteenth–early-twentieth century moment (largely in Britain and the U.S., while situating things in a wider European/global frame), and she assembles an archive made up of newspapers and organisational records **plus** memoirs, testimonies, songs and slogans — sources that make private/domestic experience visible. Methodologically this pushes against orthodox empirical history (which privileged institutional documents and male actors) and extends the “history from below” of Thompson and Samuel by showing that a fully empirical people’s history must be gendered.

**In both the introduction and chapter 5, how has Rowbotham's political activism and involvement in the women's liberation movement shaped her methods and practice as a historian? In what way does the personal become political in her history of resistance and revolution?**

Her experiences led her to the belief that Marxist and labour histories had failed to address the role of women as active participants in history. Her involvement with Thompson and Samuel shaped her methodology as seeing history 'from below'. Her activism also caused her to view history as a tool of liberation rather than merely a study. Feminist involvement creates a worldview that is a combination of marxism and feminisim, arguing that class struggle cannot be understood without the sexual division of labour.

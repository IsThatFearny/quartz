### Picturing Empire: Photography and the Visualization of the British Empire

"photographs made by British explorers in Africa tended to reinforce the established image of the African interior as a place of disease, death and barbarism... photography played a significant part in the process by which, as Patrick Brantlinger has put it, 'Africa grew "dark"...'". p. 30

"Burton [a British explorer] had projected 'the picture of one unbroken spread of vulgar, disunited, and drunken savagery over the entire land'" p. 42

"The image of Africa's environment and inhabitants together constituting 'one rude chaos' was hardly new" p. 42

"Commercial photographers keen to exploit the European demand for exotic scenes set out as early as the 1840s to 'discovers' foreign lands photographically" p. 45

"The global reach of travel photographers was such that in 1863 a young English photographer, Samuel Bourne (1834-1912), could declare in the *British Journal of Photography (BJP)*: 'There is now scarcely a nook or corner, a glen, a valley, or mountain, much less a country, on the face of the globe which the penetrating eye of the camera has not searched" p. 47

"the porters and guides upon whom [Bourne] depended feature in his photographs primarily as picturesque figures for graphic charm and scale rather than as individuals in their own right" p.g 51

"the inclusion of local inhabitants in Bourne's pictures depended upon their total acquiescence to his aesthetic... the idyllic conditions for photography... was marred only by the "obstinacy of the natives when I wanted to introduce them into my pictures. By no amount of talking and acting could I get them to stand or sit in an easy, natural attitude. Their idea of giving life to a picture was to stand bolt upright, with their arms down as stiff as pokers, their chin turned up as if they were standing to have their throats cut"" p. 52

"[Bourne] depended on the help of the English Commissioner resident at Srinagar to give orders for 'the best looking nautch girls' to be collected in order to be photographed" p. 53

"Bourne's attraction to [Indian women] as photographic subjects and his use of colonial authority to arrange them as photographic subjects and his use of colonial authority to arrange them before his lens are symptomatic of a wider currency in sexualised imagery of the colonial exotic... 'Nautch girls' were long regarded by the British as equivalent to high-class prostitutes and were treated with a mixture of fascination and disdain. They were a popular subject for photographers, who made pictures of them posed in their traditional costumes, occasionally in mid-dance, further amplifying the stereotype of the sexually exotic Oriental woman" p. 53

"Drawing on an established iconography of the Orient in painting, literature, and architecture, photographers invented their own 'Orient' in the studio with props, backdrops, and the directed poses of scantily dressed 'Oriental' female models" p. 53

"Thomson thus claimed that his photographs captured a complete and objective picture of China's landscape and people. However, like Bourne's view of India, his representation of China was carefully framed by both aesthetic and cultural conventions = from racial 'types' to landscape - which were in turn informed by imperial ideologies." p. 62

"commercial photography - particularly of the Near East - played a significant role in the naturalisation of European ideas of the Orient in the second half of the nineteenth century" p. 67

"Thomson also photographed Chinese settlements and architecture, particularly of a monumental and religious nature.  Although such photographs were made with a certain amount of admiration for indigenous architectural achievements, they were often taken to represent only ancient glories or some fundamental aspect of the Chinese character." p. 67

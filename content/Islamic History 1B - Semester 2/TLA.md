## Section A

**Assess the role played by trade in the conversion to Islam.**

### Outline 

#### **Introduction (Approx. 100 words)**

- **Contextualization:** Brief overview of Islam’s early spread beyond the Arabian Peninsula from the 7th century onwards.
    
- **Thesis Statement:** Trade played a crucial role in the conversion to Islam, especially in regions outside Arab conquest zones, by facilitating peaceful, sustained contact, offering economic incentives, and transmitting cultural-religious ideas. However, its influence was interwoven with political, social, and religious factors.
    
- **Signposting:** This essay will examine trade’s role in (1) facilitating contact, (2) offering economic advantages, and (3) enabling cultural transmission, while also acknowledging other influential forces.
    

---

#### **I. Trade as a Medium of Contact and Mobility (Approx. 200 words)**

- **Key Argument:** Trade routes (trans-Saharan, Indian Ocean, Silk Roads) created sustained and peaceful contact between Muslim and non-Muslim communities.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - West Africa: Berber traders spreading Islam via trans-Saharan trade routes; role of cities like Timbuktu and Gao.
        
    - Indian Ocean trade: Muslim merchants settling in port cities like Malacca, Mogadishu, and Kilwa.
        
- **Mechanism of conversion:** Non-coercive, gradual interaction through merchants who acted as cultural brokers and role models.
    
- **Analysis:** Conversion often began in trading hubs and urban centers where traders had influence.
    

---

#### **II. Economic and Social Incentives of Conversion (Approx. 150–200 words)**

- **Key Argument:** Embracing Islam could bring tangible economic and social benefits in trade relationships.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - Trust networks: Conversion granted access to Muslim trade networks governed by Islamic legal norms (e.g., _sharia_ contracts, honesty in trade).
        
    - Tax incentives: In some cases, Muslim rulers taxed Muslims differently than non-Muslims (e.g., _jizya_ vs. _zakat_).
        
- **Analysis:** These incentives encouraged conversion for pragmatic reasons, especially among elite and merchant classes.
    

---

#### **III. Trade and Cultural-Religious Transmission (Approx. 150 words)**

- **Key Argument:** Trade facilitated the transmission not only of goods but of Islamic ideas, texts, and practices.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - Role of Arabic language, Islamic education, and Sufi brotherhoods (e.g., Qadiriyya, Tijaniyya in Africa).
        
    - Port cities as religious centers: emergence of mosques, Islamic schools (madrasas), and clerical patronage linked to mercantile activity.
        
- **Analysis:** Islam was often integrated with local traditions, indicating a flexible and syncretic spread aided by sustained merchant presence.
    

---

#### **IV. Limitations and Other Contributing Factors (Approx. 100 words)**

- **Key Argument:** Trade was a vital vector but not the sole driver of conversion.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - Military conquest (e.g., North Africa, Iberian Peninsula).
        
    - State patronage and political alliances (e.g., Mali Empire's royal conversion).
        
    - Religious appeal and Sufi missionary activity.
        
- **Analysis:** Trade worked in conjunction with these other factors; conversion was multi-causal and context-specific.
    

---

#### **Conclusion (Approx. 100 words)**

- **Restate Thesis:** Trade was a powerful and peaceful engine of Islamic expansion, especially in Africa and Asia, where it provided both material and ideological incentives for conversion.
    
- **Synthesis:** It enabled sustained cultural exchange, supported Islamic institutions, and incentivized religious adoption—though it was most effective when working in tandem with political, religious, and social structures.
    
- **Final Thought:** The role of trade in the spread of Islam reveals how economic life can serve as a conduit for profound religious transformation.

## Essay

Trade was a major aspect of various Islamic empires, both with other Muslim entities or with non-Muslims. The part that it played in widespread conversion to Islam cannot be understated: it facilitated sustained, peaceful contact, offered economic incentives to those who converted, and was instrumental in transmitting Islamic religious and cultural ideas to those outside Islam. This essay will argue that trade had a vital influence of conversion to Islam, addressing the key themes of its role in creating points of contact, providing positive economic circumstances for converts, and enabling the transmission of Islamic texts and culture to non-Islamic regions.

Trade routes extending beyond Arab-conquered regions of the world established various points of contact between Muslim traders and non-Muslim communities in places such as Africa, Malaysia and Indonesia, and India. Trade routes across the Sahara and throughout the Indian ocean were frequented by many Muslim merchants. The Malabar coast of south-west India is a good example of a region whose widespread conversion to Islam was heavily influenced by trade routes. Merchants purchased spices, various woods, and products imported from elsewhere (predominantly China). As a result, some Muslim merchants settled in Kerala state, and by the 14th-century an established Muslim community (the majority of whom were converts) was recorded by Ibn Battuta. He noted that the Islamic culture which was prevalent amongst these settlements was near identical to his own. Ibn Battuta also hints that some of the Muslims in these communities (estimated to be 10,000 strong) were native-born, providing evidence of conversion amongst the indigenous population. He records that Islamic institutions were common across Kerala cities (primarily the presence of numerous mosques), and references the *ulama* who were present within these societies. This evidence was substantially influenced by trade routes throughout the region, as Muslim traders travelled to desirable areas, spreading their religion as they went. Similar processes occurred elsewhere. In the Indian Ocean, a prince travelled to the Arabian peninsula in the interest of strengthening trade relations, and converted as a result of his travels, bringing Islam back to his father's territory. Despite being exiled, he began the process of conversion and founded a new, rapidly-growing Islamic settlement. In North Africa, the substantial trans-Saharan trade in Islamic texts greatly influenced conversion to Islam in regions which were not already under Arab rule. From this evidence, it is very difficult to argue that the influence of trade on conversion was minimal: without Muslim trade routes, the spread of Islam would have been limited to areas taken by conquest, or directly bordering Arab states, and the diffusion of the religion would have remained bounded. It is clear that conversion to Islam began in these trading hubs, where Islamic merchants and traders had increased influence.

In a similar vein, trade also provided strong economic incentives for conversion to Islam. One such incentive was the use of Sharia contracts. The Qu'ran refers to moral obligations and contracts under Sharia law, specific to trade and mercantile activity. Inherent within Islamic trade was a system of trust and honesty as prescribed by the Qu'ran, reducing transaction costs and providing access to legal protection under Islamic law. This system was inaccessible to non-Muslims, and conversion within non-Muslim merchant communities was highly beneficial for all parties, as they could guarantee a certain level of trust in trading activity. This clearly demonstrates that trade was vital in influencing conversion to Islam - non-Muslim merchants converting for this reason was not uncommon in both Arab-ruled states, and those which were external to the Arab world. Economic incentives also existed within the trade taxation system. *Ushr*, for example, was a tax levied on those (to a greater extent on non-Muslims than Muslims) moving goods and livestock across Islamic borders - a convert, therefore, would be required to pay significantly less (some reports say up to half of the tax), saving a significant amount of money in transactions involving border crossings. Trade created these economic incentives which convinced many non-Muslims trading with Muslims to convert as a matter of convenience and fiscal efficiency in their ventures.

Trade created connections between Islamic territories and foreign lands, allowing merchants to settle and bring their religion with them, forming Islamic communities in trade hubs around the world. It brought economic incentives for conversion, leading to the conversion many non-Muslim merchants hoping to gain a competitive advantage. From these arguments, it becomes evident that trade played a key role in the Islamisation of much of the world, whether in the Arab world or externally.

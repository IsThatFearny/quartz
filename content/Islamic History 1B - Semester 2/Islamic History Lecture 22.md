### Nomadic Invasions

- 11th Century: Turkic people led by the Seljuq family invade the central islamic lands and establish the Great Seljuq Empire of Iran
- 13th Century: the Mongos invade central Islamic lands and establish the Ilkhanid Empire of Iran
- 14th Century: the Turco-Mongol Timurids invade the central Islamic lands and establish the Timurid empire
- The Great Seljuqs, Iran (1040-93) - Persian men of the Pen and Turkish men of the Sword

</br>

### The Seljuqs of Rum (1081-1307)

- Conquest of Byzantine/Eastern Roman (Rum) territory in Anatolia - rise of the Rum Seljuqs
- Urban Sunni Islam of the ruling Seljuq elite and syncretic Folk-Islam of their Turkmen followers in the periphery
- Continuation of steppe traditions: sovereignty as the possession of the leading family, not an autocratic ruler
- Migration of pastoral nomads and nomadisation of central Islamic lands and Anatolia

</br>

### The Mongol Peoples

- Mongol invasions led by Genghis Khan and his descendants
- Early Mongol rule in the Islamic World:
	- Pagan rulers
	- Legitimisation of power as Mongol conquerors
	- Allied to the Great Mongol Khan
	- Practised destruction and extortion of Muslim subjects
	- Nomadisation
- Later Mongol rulers:
	- Ilkhanids of Iran (1295-1388)
	- Conversion to Islam - Sufism played a major role
	- 1295-1304, the rule of SUltan Mahmud Ghazan Padshah-i Islam
	- Legitimisation of power as Muslim rulers, independent from the Great Khan
	- Iran and Anatolia incorporated into the Mongol world system, exchanging culture, knowledge, and goods
	- Gunpowder brought from China into the Islamic world
- Tamerlane (1369-1405)
	- Contradictions were part of his self-image, which was used for legitimisation
	- He was an exceptionally brutal conqueror but sponsored the arts and sciences
	- Massacred fellow Muslims and destroyed Muslim cities
	- Samarkand was built with wealth looted from sacked cities
	- Posed a serious threat to other Muslim states

</br>

### Crusades and Counter-Crusades

**Crusades: a series of military campaigns led by Latin Western Christians to free the 'Holy Land'**

- First Crusade (1096-1099) - response to Seljuq conquests of Byzantine territories
- Second Crusade (1145-1149) - response to the fall of Edessa
- Third Crusade (1189-1192) - response to the fall of Jerusalem to Saladin (led by Richard the Lionheart)
- The Fourth Crusade (1198-1204) - to take Constantinople
- There were four more crusades into the Islamic world
- Crusades also occurred in Europe in order to convert Pagans to Christianity, and the re-conquista of al-Andalus was also regarded as a Crusade

</br>
#### Religion and Politics

- Development of the concepts of 'Holy War' and *jihad*
- Holy war or conquest? Several Crusader states were founded
- *jihad* or state building?
	- Used to legitimise power which had been usurped by force: see the Zengids of Syria as an example
- Warfare, but also periods of peace and alliances, and continuation of trade relationships

</br>

#### The Mamluks of Egypt and Syria

- Military leaders in the service of the Fatimids of Egypt of slave-soldier origin
	- Bahri Mamluks, 1250-1390
	- Burji Mamluks, 1382-1517
- *jihad* against pagan Mongols and Christian crusaders as legitmisation of power:
	- The battle of 'Ayn Jalut', 1260 - the Mamluks were led by the Baybards (r.1260-77) and defeated the Mongols
	- Warfare against the Crusaders and their expulsion from Islamic lands (1260-1291)

### The Structure of Power and its Legitimisation

- Invaders were 'warlords' who needed to legitimise their rule
- Religion was used to legitimise power that had been taken by sheer force:
	- Often a connection was created between the invader and the Abbasid Caliphate, who were the existing legitimate power
	- The Seljuqs presented themselves as saviours of the Sunni Abbasid Caliph and the revivers of Sunnism
- There was a division of power in the Islamic world:
	- Caliph: spiritual and moral head of a Muslim community, and a source of legitimacy for warlords
	- Sultan: military power, a secular ruler
	- The *Khutba* (Friday's religious sermon) mentioned the name of both the Caliph and the Sultan
- Technically, there was no fixed division of power or official institutions - the structure of power changed significantly depending on the Caliph or Sultan in power, and between the different periods of Seljuq history
- All rulers had a religious role, there was no purely secular power
- Persian was used as the language of the administration and ancient Persian ideas of rulership were introduced in the East under the Samanids and the Ghaznavids
	- The Perso-Islamic concept of kingship derived from ancient Persian ideals of autocratic rulership, and was further developed under Seljuq rule by their Persian administrators, who had previously ben in Ghaznavid service
	- The main duties of a ruler was to be a defender of 'right religion' and justice
	- One would demonstrate their power through art and architecture

### The Gunpowder Empires

**The end of nomadic migrations and invasions**

- Foundation of new Great Islamic Empires:
	- The Ottoman Empire (1281-1923)
	- The Safavid Empire (1502-1736)
	- The Mughal Empire (1526-1857)

#### The Ottoman Empire

- Built on the ruins of the Rum Seljuq and Ilkhanid Mongol states
- Before 1453, power was legitimised through a claim of investiture by Rum Seljuqs
- After 1453, legitimisation of power was as a result of the conquest of Constantinople
- Imperial ideology was expressed in titles: Abu 'l Fath Mehmed = Mehmed the Conqueror; Qayser-i Rum = Caesar/Emperor of Rome
- Two sources of law: Shari'a (Islamic Law) and *kanun* (Sultanic laws/decrees)

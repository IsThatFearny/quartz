**Discuss the notion that Frankish societies were almost entirely segregated from indigenous Middle Eastern populations in terms of day-to-day relations.**

The Frankish crusades naturally resulted in the settlement of the Franks in the Middle East, living alongside indigenous inhabitants of the region. Whether the two populations had any day-to-day interaction with each other, however, is a matter of some debate amongst scholars of the period. 
The scholarly controversy surrounding this topic defines the two stances as 'assimilationist' and 'segregationist'.[^1] The assimilationist stance is the notion that Frankish societies became fully integrated with the local populations amongst which they settled, becoming naturalised in these communities. The converse position, segregationism, makes the argument that Frankish settlers were geographically, theologically, and culturally separated from the local populations whose land they invaded. This essay shall argue that day-to-day interaction was the norm in many areas of the Levant, and that Frankish societies were by no means segregated from the indigenous populations of the area.

Religion is one aspect of society for which we have clear contemporary evidence for interaction between communities, specifically in Jerusalem. As a shared site of worship and pilgrimage for Christians, Muslims, and Jews alike, there is evidence for a considerable amount of interaction between communities. One example is the anecdote of Usama ibn Munqidh, in which he recounts how he prayed in a small mosque which the Franks had converted into a church. He says that the Templars (who he even claims were his friends) would evacuate the mosque to allow him to pray, and would permit no interference to Usama's prayer by their fellow Franks.[^2] Such religious tolerance also extended to the Jewish population.[^3] There is evidence to show that Muslims regularly prayed at the Dome of the Rock, and even some to demonstrate an accord between the monotheistic nature of both Islam and Judeo-Christianity.[^4] This regular interaction between Frankish Christians and those of other religions is strong evidence for the argument that the communities were not entirely segregated from each other in day-to-day life. It is important to note here that the term 'indigenous populations' does not apply only to resident Muslims and Jews, but to local Christians as well, many of whom resided directly alongside the Frankish settlers, whether that may have been in Jerusalem or more rural towns.[^5] In these cases, the assimilationist view holds strong: Frankish settlers lived within the pre-existing local Christian communities, integrating well due to their shared religion. The Franks and the local Christian populations grew to become codependent on each other: the Christians depended on the Franks for protection, and the Franks depended on the Christians as they made up the majority of the population and were heavily involved in servicing the Frankish settlements; they are also known to have taken up military commands.[^6] Far from segregation, Frankish settlers and local Christians often lived cooperatively within the same communities, sharing resources and integrating with each other.

Trade was also a major source of Frankish interaction with local populations. European merchants travelled with the Frankish settlers, eager to take advantage of the favourable position of the region, but these only made up a part of the merchant activity that took place in the Levant. The European traders worked closely with their local counterparts who represented Muslim trading companies.[^7] There are written records which show that indigenous traders played a major part in local trade, with strong local trading communities in Tyre, Acre, and Damascus, all of whom regularly interacted with the Frankish traders who frequently sailed down the coast (one Genoese register references at least eleven voyages between February and June 1274).[^8] Day-to-day interaction such as this between Frankish and indigenous merchants was a necessity for efficient and profitable trade. To facilitate this, a variety of other connections had to be made, the most vital of which were those who were able to exchange money for foreign merchants.[^9] The transactions taking place in the background of this frenzy of trading meant that Franks and indigenous communities had significant further interaction with each other. This strongly supports the argument that the Franks were not segregated from indigenous communities in the Levant, but rather were inextricably interconnected. Demonstrating this ever more clearly is the archaeological record, in this case coinage. There are various examples of coins that were minted with both Arabic and Latin text (see Appendix A), which demonstrates that fiscal assimilation was so widespread that a common coinage was necessary, or at least coinage which could be interpreted by both indigenous populations and Frankish settlers.[^10] One cannot possibly argue that these societies were segregated from each other when they shared such deep-rooted fiscal connections.

Indigenous participation in Frankish societal infrastructure was also prevalent throughout the Levant. We have evidence of instances in which conquered Muslims served the Frankish settlers, often going so far as to assume their masters' religion.[^11] Some Muslims were employed as scribes for the Franks, some became knights or served the kings of Jerusalem, and some even held high office in the kingdom.[^12] Similarly to the merchant activity described above, there is also evidence for indigenous peoples and Frankish settlers entering into commercial agreements in a more local context.[^13] The Franks also imported the European form of serfdom, and in certain areas of the Levant would practise enslavement of conquered peoples.[^14] Thus, enserfed Muslims and Jews (for these denominations made up the overwhelming majority of serfs) would have regular interaction with their masters, although the relationship between them was likely not as cordial as the assimilationist model might imply.[^15] The indigenous communities under Frankish rule were not merely subjects: they were integral in the mechanism of governance, commerce, diplomacy, military operations, and so on. Yet again, all evidence points to the assimilationist model of interaction in the Levant, demonstrating that indigenous populations lived side-by-side with the Frankish settlers.

One cannot defend the segregationist model in light of evidence such as that which has been presented. Day-to-day interaction between indigenous Muslims, Jews, and Christians was widespread, regardless of whether it yielded positive or negative results for those involved. It is impossible to escape the fact that Frankish settlers tolerated, if not actively encouraged co-habitation of the Levant. Their willingness to share religious spaces in Jerusalem, cooperate and live in the same settlements as local Christians, and to permit non-Franks to take up positions of power within the bureaucracy of society provides irrefutable evidence that the assimilationist model of Frankish settlement is correct, and that the segregationist model should be consigned to the historiographical past.

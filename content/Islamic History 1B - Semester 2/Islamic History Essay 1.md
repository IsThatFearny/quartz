The Frankish crusades naturally resulted in the settlement of the Franks in the Middle East, living alongside indigenous inhabitants of the region. Whether the two populations had any day-to-day interaction with each other, however, is a matter of some debate amongst scholars of the period. 
The scholarly controversy surrounding this topic defines the two stances as 'assimilationist' and 'segregationist'.[^1] The assimilationist stance is the notion that Frankish societies became fully integrated with the local populations amongst which they settled, becoming naturalised in these communities. The converse position, segregationism, makes the argument that Frankish settlers were geographically, theologically, and culturally separated from the local populations whose land they invaded. This essay shall argue that the extent of day-to-day interaction between the Frankish settlers and local populations varied depending on the religion, location, and wealth of the community, but it is a mistake to argue that the two societies were entirely segregated. The most accurate position in this controversy is somewhere in between the assimilationist and segregationist stances.

Religion is the aspect of society for which we have the clearest contemporary evidence for interaction between communities, specifically in Jerusalem. As a shared site of worship and pilgrimage for Christians, Muslims, and Jews alike, there is evidence for a considerable amount of interaction between communities. One example is the anecdote of Usama ibn Munqidh, in which he recounts how he prayed in a small mosque which the Franks had converted into a church. He says that the Templars (who he even claims were his friends) would evacuate the mosque to allow him to pray, and would permit no interference to Usama's prayer by their fellow Franks.[^2] Such religious tolerance also extended to the Jewish population.[^3] This regular interaction between Frankish christians and those of other religions is strong evidence for the argument that the communities were not entirely segregated from each other in day-to-day life.

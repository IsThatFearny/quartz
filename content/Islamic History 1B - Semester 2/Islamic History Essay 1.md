**Discuss the notion that Frankish societies were almost entirely segregated from indigenous Middle Eastern populations in terms of day-to-day relations.**

The Frankish crusades naturally resulted in the settlement of the Franks in the Middle East, living alongside indigenous inhabitants of the region. Whether the two populations had any day-to-day interaction with each other, however, is a matter of some debate amongst scholars of the period. 
The scholarly controversy surrounding this topic defines the two stances as 'assimilationist' and 'segregationist'.[^1] The assimilationist stance is the notion that Frankish societies became fully integrated with the local populations amongst which they settled, becoming naturalised in these communities. The converse position, segregationism, makes the argument that Frankish settlers were geographically, theologically, and culturally separated from the local populations whose land they invaded. This essay shall argue that day-to-day interaction was the norm in many areas of the Levant, and that Frankish societies were by no means segregated from the indigenous populations of the area.

Religion is one aspect of society for which we have clear contemporary evidence for interaction between communities, specifically in Jerusalem. As a shared site of worship and pilgrimage for Christians, Muslims, and Jews alike, there is evidence for a considerable amount of interaction between communities. One example is the anecdote of Usama ibn Munqidh, in which he recounts how he prayed in a small mosque which the Franks had converted into a church. He says that the Templars (who he even claims were his friends) would evacuate the mosque to allow him to pray, and would permit no interference to Usama's prayer by their fellow Franks.[^2] Such religious tolerance also extended to the Jewish population.[^3] There is evidence to show that Muslims regularly prayed at the Dome of the Rock, and even some to demonstrate an accord between the monotheistic nature of both Islam and Judeo-Christianity.[^4] This regular interaction between Frankish Christians and those of other religions is strong evidence for the argument that the communities were not entirely segregated from each other in day-to-day life. It is important to note here that the term 'indigenous populations' does not apply only to resident Muslims and Jews, but to local Christians as well, many of whom resided directly alongside the Frankish settlers, whether that may have been in Jerusalem or more rural towns.[^5] In these cases, the assimilationist view holds strong: Frankish settlers lived within the pre-existing local Christian communities, integrating well due to their shared religion. The Franks and the local Christian populations grew to become codependent on each other: the Christians depended on the Franks for protection, and the Franks depended on the Christians as they made up the majority of the population and were heavily involved in servicing the Frankish settlements; they are also known to have taken up military commands.[^6] Far from segregation, Frankish settlers and local Christians often lived cooperatively within the same communities, sharing resources and integrating with each other.

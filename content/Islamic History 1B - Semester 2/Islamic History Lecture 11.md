### Developments in the Middle Period (c. 1050-1400)

- The 'Middle Period' was the time between the disintegration of the 'Abbasid empire and the rise of the 'gunpowder empires'
- Largely characterised by tribal migration

</br>

Nomad dynasties (mainly of central Asian slave origin) ruled their states with the help of the 'notables' (i.e. scholars, administrators, artists, etc.). The leaders of these states would sponsor learning and religious institutions in return for their aid. There was a clear connection between a military-based regime and religious scholarship.

Various new institutions were established and developed:

- Sultanate and Amirate (Emirate)
- Religious scholarships (*ulama*)
- *Madrasas* and *Khanqa*, both schools of religious education
- The rise of the administrative and military classes

The trade in which the Islamic world was engaged was diverse and widespread, with access to Mongolian trade routes and trading in commodities such as chattell, grain, wine, textiles, weaponry, etc. However the improved global connections also provided a path for disease, most notably the bubonic plague of which there was an epidemic in 1374.

</br>

#### Sufism

Sufism was a mystical Islamic belief in which Muslims sought the truth of divine love and knowledge through the medium of direct personal experience of God. A major Sufi ritual was the remembrance of God through his invocation, dancing, music, poetry, fasting, etc. The primary scholar and master of Sufism was Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058-1111) through whom the belief became highly mainstream in the Islamic world.

From the 11th century onwards, Sufism developed further, with lodges being built in order to teach Sufism to apprentices, and the state officially giving patronage to Sufism (and therefore legitimacy).

The appeal of Sufism was its similarities with Shamanistic beliefs, making it much easier for nomadic tribes to be introduced to it.

</br>

### Fragmentation of the Khanates

The Mongol Khanates fractured into smaller pieces in the 14th century: the Ilkhanate was divided into different principalities, all ruled by various different dynasties; and the Chagatai Khanate was divided into eastern and western domains as a result of a revolt against their leader's conversion to Islam.

</br>

### Timur/Tamerlane (1336-1405)

- Born in c. 1336 in the Chagatai Khanate into the Muslim Turco-Mongol tribe of Barlas (speaking a form of Turkic)
- A descendant of Karacar, who was a commander of Ghingis Khan (later serving his son Chagatai who founded the Khanate)
- Timur rose to power in the power vacuum which was left by the fragmented Mongol territories

#### Rise to Power

Timur acquired leadership through making alliances with various other tribes, becoming ruler of Transoxania. In the 1360s, he allied with Husayn (of the western Chagatai Khanate) against the easter Khanate, and he subsequently killed Husayn in 1369, taking power for himself. He was not able to claim the title of Khan, not being of noble descent, and therefore maintained a puppet ruler, naming himself 'commander' instead. He married a descendant of Ghengis Khan, Saray Malik, and was then formally recognised as the leader of the western Chagatai Khanate, using Samarkand as his capital.

</br>

#### Various Campaigns

- 1370s: Attacks on the eastern Khanate
- 1378: Took Kashgar
- 1380s: Installed Toqtamish as Khan of the Golden Horde
- 1385: Toqtamish rebelled
- 1395: Timur sacked Toqtamish's capital
- 1390s: Attacked Golden Horde territories as well as Moscow
- Throughout the 1380s and 1390s, Timur attacked former Ilkhanid territories in Iran and the Caucasus

</br>

#### Later Conquests

- 1398: Invaded India and sacked Delhi, massacring 80,000 inhabitants and deploying 90 elephants in order to return looted wealth. Spared administrators, scholars, artists, etc.
- 1400: Sacked Aleppo
- 1401: Sacked Damascus and Baghdad
- 1402: The Battle of Ankara. Timur captured the Ottoman Sultan Bayezid I, causing massive and widespread Ottoman instability (and delaying Ottoman attacks on Constantinople for by years)

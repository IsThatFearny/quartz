### Succession

The first four caliphs were all descendants of the Prophet. The Umayyad dynasty had a common ancestor in Umayyah, and the Abbasids in Abbas. 

There was no rule for succession beyond familial relations to the prophet. After Ali was murdered in 661, the Muslim community split into two factions with differing views about succession:

- Shi'at Uthman (Party of Uthman): Caliphs should be chosen from among the tribe of the Prophet, the Quraysh
- Shi'at Ali (Party of Ali): Ali should have become the first Caliph, and only members of the Prophet's immediate family should become Caliph

Shi'ism developed into a distinctive branch, leading rebellions against the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, but were never able to hold power.

</br>

### The Development of Sunnism

The Sunni doctrine developed in oppositions to Shi'ism amongst religious scholars during the 9th-10th century. There were four Sunni schools which were decided to be the 'official' schools (though several others were founded): 

- The Hanafi school
- The Maliki school
- The Shafi'i school
- The Hanbali school

According to the Sunni doctrine, the whole Muslim community had to reach a consensus in order to choose a leader. Said leader must not necessarily be from the immediate family of the prophet, but must be a part of the Quraysh.

Sunni religious scholars held equal authority to the Caliph.

</br>

### Leadership in Shi'ism

The Shi'i Muslims developed a system of leadership whereby the *Imam* was a direct descendant of the Prophet (must be a descendant of Ali and Fatima through their son al-Husayn). The Imam was the only authority.

Shiite religious scholars held power, but their authority was always second to that of the Imam.

</br>

### Fragmentation of the Islamic empire

From the 8th-11th century, there were three rival Caliphates: 

- The Sunni Abbasid caliphate (750-1258), controlling much land in the Middle East/Holy Land
- The Sunni Umayyad caliphate (929-1031), controlling the southern half of modern Spain
- The Shiite Fatimid caliphate of Egypt (909-1173), controlling the majority of north Africa (with the exception of modern-day Morocco)

Various regional dynasties arose in the east against the Abbassids, such as the Buyids and the Fatimids.

</br>

### Division of power

- Caliph: The successor of the Prophet and leader of the Muslim community, given the title *Amir al-Muminin* (the Commander of the Faithful)
- Amir/Emir: 'Commander', given to military leaders and other offices such as provincial governors
- Sultan: meaning 'power'/'state', was used as a title similar to *Amir* but was later bestowed on the Seljuqs as a title for supreme rulership.

Neither the *amir* nor the *sultan* had any explicit religious role, nor could one claim to be the successor of the prophet, as the Caliphs could.

</br>

### The Buyids

The Buyids were a family of local Persian leaders, from the mountains of Daylam in Northern Iran. They are described as warlords, as they conquered their territory with no religious or political legitimacy. They converted to Islam as they conquered Iraq, specifically to Twelver Shi'ism. Their army took supremacy within government, leading to a powerful military aristocracy.

## Prep

1. According to C. Hillenbrand, which aspects of Frankish presence are of most interest to contemporary Muslim authors?  

When Muslim authors make reference to Frankish presence (which is more seldom than one might think) they tend to focus on a handful of aspects: 

- Frankish leaders such as Richard the Lionheart, Reynald of Chatillon, etc.
- The character of the Franks (in general - it was rare that an Islamic source focused on an individual)
- Frankish women and female warriors
- Medicine

2. Look up Usama b. Munqidh in the Encyclopaedia of Islam (search for Munḳid̲h̲ in the second edition), how well can we argue that he knew Frankish/Crusader society in the Levant? Can we feel confident that his anecdotes truly reflect everyday interactions with the Franks?  

Usama's commentary on the Franks came from his numerous journeys to the Levant whilst he was an associate of Muʿīn al-Dīn Unur (pursuing a policy of detente with the Franks). Therefore, one could argue that in this role as an envoy, Usama was unable to get a clear picture of everyday interactions with Frankish society in the Levant. He did not live amongst them full time, and he was eventually forced out to Egypt, where there was very little interaction between him and the Franks.  

3. In what ways was Frankish rule experienced differently by Muslims of various social classes?

Interaction between Muslims and Franks seems to have been relatively limited - there was a significant language barrier, especially between the 'common people'; the upper classes of Franks spoke Arabic in order to communicate with their Muslim counterparts, so interaction was likely different in these circumstances. Similarly, diplomatic and mercantile activity required interaction and cooperation, so logically those involved in these activities, usually the upper classes, would experience Frankish rule very differently to the lower classes, who had little reason to cooperate with their Frankish rulers, who, as Usama relates, could be quite rude towards Muslim citizens.

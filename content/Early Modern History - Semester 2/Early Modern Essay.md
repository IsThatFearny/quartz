The development of increasingly accurate cartography and the increase in travel writing in the Early Modern period has, in recent historiography, given rise to 'global history', i.e. a form of history writing that seeks to emphasise the interconnection between various different cultures, states, populations, etc. This comes as little surprise: instance of interaction increased through trade and diplomacy, enabled by improved transportation and technology. However, despite the world becoming ever more connected in this period, from 2013 onwards various historians have warned against embracing the all-encompassing connectivity narrative, arguing that it may cause the historian to stray from instances of disconnectedness.[^1] Within the story of connectivity which maps and travel writing can tell, they also show inherent disconnections between societies. Biedermann makes the argument that we should use the term '(dis)connections' to describe the developing global links as "carrying simultaneously the potential for convergence and divergence".[^2] This essay shall argue in support of this view and shall demonstrate that maps and accounts of travel provide compelling evidence of disconnections between populations in the Early Modern period.

Cartography and maps became ever more important throughout the Early Modern period, whether their use was for navigation or simply to depict the world for the 'common man'. Their use was doubtless an aid in the creation of an interconnected world: one could now visualise trade routes and geopolitical divides with much improved accuracy. For example, the Cantino Planisphere (stolen from the Portuguese by an Italian) utilised rhumb lines and latitude markings, by which a navigator could plan their route with great precision. However, it was also the case that these maps could divide the world, as well as connect it. Perhaps the clearest division of the period was drawn out by the Treaty of Tordesillas, which divided the globe into two equal halves, the dividing line being 100 (subsequently changed to 370) leagues west of the Cape Verde islands: the Portuguese could lay claim to all land to the east of the line; the Spanish claimed all land to the west.[^3] In terms of connections, this would seem to have been beneficial: a clear treaty which allowed the colonial powers of Spain and Portugal to assert their claims on undiscovered regions. This facilitated ever more efficient trade and fostered a greater volume of exploration by both powers.[^4] However, the treaty inadvertently caused disconnections between various different societies.

The primary effect was that a disconnect was created between colonial powers and those who were colonised. The Treaty of Tordesillas and the subsequent cartography surrounding it immediately designated undiscovered regions of the world simply as lands yet to be colonised, with no regard for the possibility of their independence. Cartographers of these regions, when discovered, imposed artificial borders upon indigenous peoples based upon the terms of the treaty. Such borders gave future conquistadors a mandate (at least in their own view) to conquer foreign lands, stirring up local opposition and resulting in various genocidal events, especially during the Spanish conquest of much of Latin America.[^5] These events caused a disconnect between colonisers and colonised, despite the 'connection' that the conquest of Latin America is often purported to have created. This disconnect is, at its root, caused by the cartographical division of the world, and thus one provide a strong argument that maps are evidence of (dis)connections. The practise of dividing the world as such would set a precedent for the Dutch and te British, moulding the policy of the European colonial powers forevermore and creating ever more (dis)connections between the coloniser and the colonised.[^6]

Cartography also caused a very large disconnect between Europe and much of the rest of the 'discovered' world. Early Modern cartographers (such as Mercator and Waldseemuller) began to clearly define 'Europe' as a separate continent, and to use various map-making techniques to make it appear physically larger, to emphasise its importance, and to romanticise the continent.[^7] Some cartographers even went so far as to depict Europe as a female figure: Europa Virgo Crowned was a 'map' designed by Johann Putsch which was imitated multiple times in the Early Modern Period. This romanticisation and inflation of importance caused a disconnect between Europe and other continents: Europe was a defined region with a continental identity; other regions of the world were less well-defined and were seen to have less of a communal identity. Whilst Europe was indeed connected to parts of Asia, Latin America, and other areas, there is substantial evidence that there was a clear air of superiority surrounding European travellers, partially as a result of Europe's inflated importance in cartography. In a similar vein, European dominance in map-making entirely ignored African cartographic traditions, despite their techniques often being more accurate. This meant that the continent of Africa was highly marginalised by map-makers who had the ability to disseminate their creations (a point which shall be expanded upon later) Defining other continents as 'others' meant that the mutual respect implied by the word 'connections' was simply not present, and (dis)connections can be shown to be a much better term when describing these relationships.

Travel writing as a primary source characterises much of recent scholarship on global interconnectivity, written by those who explored 'new worlds' and made a foray into new regions of the world and into new cultures. Whilst at first glance, this can be seen to be evidence of connectivity, if one looks closer into the nuances of this writing there is clear evidence of (dis)connections between cultures, despite perceived positive interaction. If one takes, for example, the writings of Hasan Al-Wazzan (subsequently batpised as Johannes Leo Africanus), it can be seen that there was a fair amount of hostility towards other cultures present amongst contemporary travellers. Al-Wazzan was a North African diplomat, and travelled to various North African cities, all the while writing an account of the various societies which he came across. His attitudes towards many of said societies can be read as rather negative: he describes the people of Haha as 'wretched' and says that 'Justice in the region is unheard of';[^8] talks of the certain Arabs as 'the worst brigands in the world';[^9] and names the Numidians as 'treacherous and murderous thieves without any conscience'.[^10] This is clearly not a sign of positive connection with foreign populations, and when disseminated to various other audiences (Al-Wazzan was captured by Spanish pirates, taken to Rome as a slave in 1518, and subsequently wrote *The Cosmography and Geography of Europe* in Italian pre-1527) it did not portray much of the North African population as at all virtuous to a European audience. It is therefore easy to see how such harsh accounts contributed significantly to the creating the sense of 'otherness' and disconnection between different cultures and societies: those who remained at home had little else to use as a benchmark. 

Religious (dis)connections were also prevalent throughout the Early Modern world, however not in the way that one might assume. Religious divisions were often not along the lines of faith boundaries, but rather denominational partitions.

# Early Modern Essay

</br>

**Maps and travel writing are often thought to have enabled more connections within Europe and beyond. In what ways can such media also be seen as evidence of what Biedermann calls disconnections?**

</br>

The development of increasingly accurate cartography and the increase in travel writing in the Early Modern period has, in recent historiography, given rise to 'global history', i.e. a form of history writing that seeks to emphasise the interconnection between various different cultures, states, populations, etc. This comes as little surprise: instance of interaction increased through trade and diplomacy, enabled by improved transportation and technology. However, despite the world becoming ever more connected in this period, from 2013 onwards various historians have warned against embracing the all-encompassing connectivity narrative, arguing that it may cause the historian to stray from instances of disconnectedness.[^1] Within the story of connectivity which maps and travel writing can tell, they also show inherent disconnections between societies. Biedermann makes the argument that we should use the term '(dis)connections' to describe the developing global links as "carrying simultaneously the potential for convergence and divergence".[^2] This essay shall argue in support of this view and shall demonstrate that maps and accounts of travel provide compelling evidence of (dis)connections between populations in the Early Modern period.

Cartography and maps became ever more important throughout the Early Modern period, whether their use was for navigation or simply to depict the world for the 'common man'. Their use was doubtless an aid in the creation of an interconnected world: one could now visualise trade routes and geopolitical divides with much improved accuracy. For example, the Cantino Planisphere (stolen from the Portuguese by an Italian) utilised rhumb lines and latitude markings, by which a navigator could plan their route with great precision.[^3] However, it was also the case that these maps could divide the world, as well as connect it. Perhaps the clearest division of the period was drawn out by the Treaty of Tordesillas, which divided the globe into two equal halves, the dividing line being 100 (subsequently changed to 370) leagues west of the Cape Verde islands: the Portuguese could lay claim to all land to the east of the line; the Spanish claimed all land to the west.[^4] In terms of connections, this would seem to have been beneficial: a clear treaty which allowed the colonial powers of Spain and Portugal to assert their claims on undiscovered regions. This facilitated ever more efficient trade and fostered a greater volume of exploration by both powers.[^5] However, the treaty inadvertently caused disconnect between colonial powers and those who were colonised. The Treaty of Tordesillas and the subsequent cartography surrounding it immediately designated undiscovered regions of the world simply as lands yet to be colonised, with no regard for the possibility of their independence. Cartographers of these regions, when discovered, imposed artificial borders upon indigenous peoples based upon the terms of the treaty. Such borders gave future conquistadors a mandate (at least in their own view) to conquer foreign lands, stirring up local opposition and resulting in various genocidal events, especially during the Spanish conquest of much of Latin America.[^6] These events caused a disconnect between colonisers and colonised, despite the trade 'connection' that the conquest of Latin America is often purported to have created. This disconnect is, at its root, caused by the cartographical division of the world, and thus provides a strong argument that such maps are strong evidence of (dis)connections. The practise of dividing the world as such would set a precedent for the Dutch and the British, moulding the policy of the European colonial powers forevermore and creating ever more (dis)connections between the coloniser and the colonised.[^7]

Cartography also caused a very large disconnect between Europe and much of the rest of the 'discovered' world. Early Modern cartographers (such as Mercator and Waldseemuller) began to clearly define 'Europe' as a separate continent, and to use various map-making techniques to make it appear physically larger, to emphasise its importance, and to romanticise the continent.[^8] Some cartographers even went so far as to depict Europe as a female figure: 'Europa Virgo Crowned' was a 'map' designed by Johann Putsch which was imitated multiple times in the Early Modern Period.[^9] This romanticisation and inflation of importance caused a disconnect between Europe and other continents: Europe was a defined region with a continental identity; other regions of the world were less well-defined and were seen to have less of a communal identity.[^10] Whilst Europe was indeed connected to parts of Asia, Latin America, and other areas, there is substantial evidence that there was a clear air of superiority surrounding European travellers, partially as a result of Europe's inflated importance in cartography - This meant that the continents of Africa, Asia, and the Americas were highly marginalised by map-makers who had greater ability to disseminate their creations.[^11] Defining other continents as 'others' meant that the collaboration and implied by the word 'connectivity' was not present, and (dis)connections is shown to be a much more accurate term in describing these relationships.

Travel writing as a primary source characterises much recent scholarship on global interconnectivity, written by those who explored 'new worlds' and made a foray into new regions of the world and into new cultures.[^12] Whilst at first glance, this can be seen to be evidence of connectivity, if one looks closer into the nuances of this writing there is clear evidence of (dis)connections between cultures, despite perceived positive interaction. If one takes, for example, the writings of Hasan Al-Wazzan (born c. 1485 and subsequently baptised as Johannes Leo Africanus), it can be seen that there was a substantial amount of hostility towards other cultures present amongst contemporary travellers.[^13] Al-Wazzan was a North African diplomat, and travelled to various North African cities (Tunis, Fez, Marrakesh, Cairo, etc.), all the while writing an account of the various societies which he came across.[^14] His attitudes towards many of said societies can be read as rather negative: he describes the people of Haha as 'wretched' and says that 'Justice in the region is unheard of';[^15] talks of the certain Arabs as 'the worst brigands in the world';[^16] and names the Numidians as 'treacherous and murderous thieves without any conscience'.[^17] This is clearly not a sign of positive connection with foreign populations, and when disseminated to various other audiences (Al-Wazzan was captured by Spanish pirates, taken to Rome as a slave in 1518, and subsequently wrote *The Cosmography and Geography of Europe* in Italian pre-1527) it did not portray much of the North African population as at all virtuous to a European audience.[^18] It is therefore easy to see how such harsh accounts contributed significantly to the creating the sense of 'otherness' and disconnection between different cultures and societies: those who remained at home had little else to use as a benchmark.

Religious (dis)connections were also prevalent throughout the Early Modern world as a result of such media, however not in the way that one might assume. Religious divisions were often not along the lines of faith boundaries, but rather denominational partitions. A prime example is the Protestant reformation, led by Martin Luther. His base was Wittenberg, a provincial town described by Luther himself as "on the edge of civilisation".[^19] The connections made during the Early Modern period were vital for the diffusion of Protestantism to its potential adopters. Aside from Luther's own personal correspondence, trade routes established across central Europe encouraged the dissemination of Luther's views.[^20] However, as a result, the Catholic church significantly increased their censorship on travel writings and cartography which would expose a Catholic audience to the ever-expanding Protestant world.[^21] Whilst one might imagine that these documents would connect the Christian community across the world, this connection forced the Catholic church to insulate themselves from 'hostile' external forces, a clear example of a (dis)connection. Another example of religious (dis)connection is the influence of the writings of Matteo Ricci, a 16th century Jesuit missionary who was the first Christian missionary to enter the closed borders of early modern China.[^22] His major work written whilst travelling in China was *The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven*, in which he detailed the religion of the region and professed his view that Chinese Confucianism and Catholicism were surprisingly similar, and that the rituals involved in Confucianism could be reconciled with Catholic doctrine.[^23] Again, the connectivity that Ricci had pioneered also came with an inherent division: the Dominican and Franciscan orders strongly disagreed with Ricci and other Jesuit's views on Confucianism (it was argued that Jesuits were fundamentally altering Christianity in order to include Confucian rituals), reporting the issue to Rome and becoming embroiled in what became known as the 'Chinese Rites Controversy'.[^24] The controversy continued throughout the 17th century, and split the Catholic church throughout the terms of multiple popes, as well as involving many European universities and the office of the Holy See.[^25] This schism is yet another example of how, despite increased connectivity in the Early Modern period, divisions and (dis)connections were an inherent part of global history at the time.

It must be noted that all of the above examples would not have had the effect that they did without the advent of the printing press. This was the medium through which much of Luther's work was spread, travel writing was circulated, and maps were disseminated throughout the early modern world. Whilst it would be a mistake to disregard the progress made in terms of global connectivity in the Early Modern period, ignoring the potential for (dis)connections is much more grave of an error. Maps and travel writing did indeed aid the process of early globalisation, but they also sowed divisions and caused certain societies to become more, rather than less, removed from each other, whether that may have been in terms of religion, exploration, diplomacy, or even simply the attitudes of one community towards another. This kind of media (perhaps inadvertently) caused Europe to become increasingly isolationist in its stance towards their geographic 'others'. There is a significant amount of evidence pointing to Biedermann's concept of (dis)connections, where increasing connectivity simultaneously caused rifts between communities, and therefore one must conclude that this thesis is a strong, well-evidenced, and thoroughly thought out position which is difficult to refute at all convincingly.[^26]

# Early Modern Lecture 10

- Although there is a common misconception that the Mughal Empire introduced Islam to the 'western' world, Islam was actually around for many centuries before Mughal imperial control. They were, however, partially responsible for spreading the religion across the world
- Although most rulers were Muslim, this did not mean that their subjects were Muslim - in the case of the Mughals, all rulers were Muslim but the majority of the population were Hindu
- Often the Islamic empires are credited with bringing all faiths and cultures together under the banner of Islam, generally avoiding forcible conversion and accommodating various different religions
- Three primary Islamic empires:
	- Ottoman Empire c. 1300-1922 (The Fall of Constantinople c. 1453)
	- Safavid Empire c. 1501-1722
	- Mughal Empire c. 1526-1857
- These empires had significant crossover (as can be seen by the dates of their existence). From 1526-1722, all three existed at the same time

</br>

### The Fall of Constantinople

The Fall of Constantinople was highly important in global history, as it restricted European access to the silk roads. As a result, Europeans were forced to look elsewhere in order to trade with Asia, giving rise to maritime routes across the world.

</br>

### Similarities across empires

- Turkic-Mongol heritage
- All used Islam as their state religion, and Persian as their state language
- Agrarian-based
- Utilised a 'land-tenure' system which gave rulers significant centralised power
- All used gunpowder in their weaponry

</br>

### The Ottoman Empire

- Much of the population of Anatolia became Muslim under Seljuk rule (1071-1243). Sufi orders providing social services played an important part in the conversion process
- The earliest origins of the empire are in military bands which conquered Anatolia and parts of modern Bulgaria, Macedonia, and Greece in the 14th century
- The Ottomans seized Constantinople in 1453
- Persian began to flourish in the arts and at court in the 16th century

</br>

#### Geopolitics

- Hungary came under Ottoman rule in 1529 - the Ottoman-Habsburg wars continued for another century and a half, until the second Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683
- The empire was active in the Mediterranean, notably seizing Algiers in 1529, and Tunis in 1574
- The successors of Mehmed absorbed the Levant, annex eastern Anatolia and Northern Iraq (taken from the Safavids) in 1514, and move into the Indian ocean, engaging with the Portuguese for control of the spice trade

</br>

### The Safavid Empire

- Named after the Persian mystic Shaykh Safi al-Din (1252-1334), who founded the Safavid Sufi order
- The Safavids conquered Iran rapidly between 1500-1510, which led to the reign of Shah Ismail (r. 1500-1524)
- Shah Ismal, although originally a turkic speaker, introduced the use of Persian into the Safavid court
- Shah Ismal was succeeded by Shah Abbas (r. 1588-1629), who expanded the empire and expelled the Portuguese from Hormuz

</br>

#### Safavid Religious Differences

- Shi'ism was promoted as Iran's official religion, despite it being a minority religion
- The Safavids brought in shiite scholars from Syria, Iraq, the Arabian peninsula and Bahrain throughout the 16th century
- They also suppressed rival groups who promoted alternative forms of Islam such as Sunni
- The political divide between Sunnism and Shi'ism began as a result of this

</br>

### The Mughal Empire

- The first Mughal Emperor, Babur (b. 1483) was a descendent of Tamerlane - his native language was Chaghatai Turkic
- The Mughal empire reached its apogee under the reign of Akbar (r. 155-1605), who makes Persian the official language of the court
- The Mughal Empire encompassed a significant population, who were predominantly Hindu
- The court included elites from all groups and faiths who were all loyal to the ruler. Their loyalty was expressed through elaborate court rituals

</br>

### Agrarian-based empires

- The 'land-tenure system' was used in all three empires:
	- At the top was the state, which owned all the land in the empire
	- The rent collectors made up the middle class of the land system, who extracted surplus for the state
	- At the bottom were the peasants who worked the land in order to create this surplus - they stay connected to the land and were able to pass it on to their children, despite lacking ownership
- The middle class helped to run the empire and would be paid in 'land revenue'. This class was moved around often to avoid geographical centralisation of power
- Land-tenure had a unique structure, and it was this that held together the large empires of heterogenous populations. All were able to be integrated into the imperial elite
- Even enslaved people had the opportunity to advance themselves by becoming part of the military and administrative apparatus - this was permitted as a result of their indoctrination into the imperial apparatus and lack of connections and loyalty which lay elsewhere

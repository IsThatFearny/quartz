### The Military Revolution Debate

- Gunpowder technology quickly became popular - catalysed the invention of cannons, pistols, etc.
- The invention of new weaponry also caused a shift in the organisation of warfare, i.e. a change from cavalry to infantry, more regimented military systems, etc.
- Of course, this also led to higher mortality and casualty rates in war - internal injuries became much more common, which were harder to deal with medically and had a much higher chance of causing infection
- There was also a response to the development of gunpowder: fortifications improved to defend against cannon fire (e.g. stronger walls around strongholds)

#### The 'Bellicist' theory of state formation

- 'War made the state, and the state made war' - Charles Tilly
- States centralised themselves and their administrations around warfare and the military, developing taxation systems and bureaucracy to support the large costs of warfare - the state often went bankruptcy (e.g. the armada in Spain), but this was seen a necessity for both religious and geopolitical reasons
- Philip II of Spain even had a special chair and suit made so that he could carry on working to maintain the Spanish Empire, even throughout his arthritis

</br>

- Some scepticism about 'technological determinism' - gunpowder technology was highly unreliable (e.g. King James of Scotland blown up by 'The Lion', his own cannon)
- The adoption of new technologies largely depended on local social and political priorities

</br>

### The Military Revolution and European Colonial Conquest

- The ease of the Spanish conquest of Mexico, in which Hernan Cortes arrived with a large naval fleet and many members of the cavalry (1519-21), was hailed as miraculous
- Very few conquistadors were actually soldiers or armed with firearms - many were lawyers or the impoverished, looking to get rich quick; they largely used cavalry and exploited regional divisions to defeat their enemies, as well as using enslaved armies to make up their numbers
- The colonial presence of Portugal in India has largely been attributed to their superior naval might, rather than primarily their artillery - local technology was often used on the subcontinent - and similarly to the South American conquests by the Spanish, regional politics was often weaponised by colonial powers

</br>

### The Military Revolution Beyond Europe

- It is suggested that fundamental to Chinese success was resource transfer, rather than gunpoweder technology
- The British in India actively suppressed access to superior technology in the subcontinent, so that they could maintain their control
- The Mughal, Safavid, and Mongol empires are often termed the 'gunpowder empires'
- The closest equivalent of a military revolution (as a result in developments in gunpowder technology) was in Japan - their weapon production industry was very strong, making more guns than all European countries combined in 1600

</br>

### Conclusions

- Technical determinism is a dangerous school of thought - new technology is not always a game changer (or isn't for long)
- Military development and expansion *can cause* economics and political centralisation, but can also *be caused* by them
- Theories about centralisation can often overlook unseen labour (women, children, slaves, etc.)
- Domination is often secured by financial improvements and economic growth
- The model of 'military synthesis' is very useful in thinking about the ways that foreign powers could combine their own practises and local practises in new environments
- Overseas success of European colonial powers was not always due to gunpowder alone, but might have been linked more closely to exploitation of slave labour and political manouevres
- The 'military revolution' was not solely a European phenomenon - Japan, China, etc.
- Traditional practises such as forced famine or siege still persisted throughout this period

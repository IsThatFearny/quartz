# Historiography Essay

## How revolutionary was the American Revolution

</br>

The American Revolution is often described as the seminal moment in the birth of the modern USA, a 'radical' movement which upturned the character of the nation entirely, transforming it into what was allegedly the first modern democracy. However, there is great debate over how 'radical' this revolution truly was: many historians argue that the American Revolution was in fact not revolutionary at all, and was instead the embodiment of failed political aspirations to equality and virtue. This essay shall analyse these two perspectives, specifically with relation to two of the primary debates within the discourse: whether the Revolution's 'radicalism' should be judged by the amelioration of African-Americans and women (as would soon be observed in the French Revolution of 1990); and whether the revolution transformed the nation's governance and commercial aspirations as opposed to simply maintaining the ongoing trends of the colonies.

Perhaps the most recognisable proponent of the 'radical' revolution is Gordon Wood, who lays out his argument in *The Radicalism of the American Revolution*. Wood makes the argument that radicalism should be measured by the amount of social change that took place, and that the American Revolution "created a society fundamentally difference from the colonial society of the eighteenth century". Note here that his definition of 'social change' is not necessarily empirical - he appeals to how Americans 'felt' after the revolution. Whether or not their condition had improved substantially is, to Wood, irrelevant. He argues that the common American felt liberated by their oppression under the contempt of the elites. However, this argument is subject to fierce debate from two fronts. Some make the criticism that it excludes 'bottom-up' testimony and only focuses on the lived experience of elites - this seems entirely contrary to Wood's own argument. He reserves the term 'revolutionary' for these elites, and there is also the glaring omission of the experiences of those living in the South. It is therefore argued that he cannot claim give fair treatment to non-elite Americans. Other historians contend that this view of the American revolution marginalises the oppression of African-American slaves and of women, and therefore cannot be a true exposition of the 'liberation' of the American people. Not only this, but the criticism is levelled that this view also omits the role of African-Americans in the revolution itself, for example in the case of Lord Dunmore's 'Ethiopian Regiment'. Wood makes a single defence of these criticisms: he contends that "saying that all white males were equal in 1776 was something revolutionary and new under the sun", accusing his critics of gross anachronism when discussing the liberation of minority groups
